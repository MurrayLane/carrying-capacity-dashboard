## 1. Willett et al. 2019 (EAT-Lancet), L081

**(1) Citation as printed.** Walter Willett, Johan Rockström, Brent Loken, Marco Springmann, Tim Lang, Sonja Vermeulen, Tara Garnett, David Tilman, Fabrice DeClerck, Amanda Wood, Malin Jonell, Michael Clark, Line J Gordon, Jessica Fanzo, Corinna Hawkes, Rami Zurayk, Juan A Rivera, Wim De Vries, Lindiwe Majele Sibanda, Ashkan Afshin, Abhishek Chaudhary, Mario Herrero, Rina Agustina, Francesco Branca, Anna Lartey, Shenggen Fan, Beatrice Crona, Elizabeth Fox, Victoria Bignet, Max Troell, Therese Lindahl, Sudhvir Singh, Sarah E Cornell, K Srinath Reddy, Sunita Narain, Sania Nishtar, Christopher J L Murray. "Food in the Anthropocene: the EAT-Lancet Commission on healthy diets from sustainable food systems". *Lancet* 2019; 393: 447-92 (issue of February 2, 2019; published online January 16, 2019). DOI printed: 10.1016/S0140-6736(18)31788-4. The margin (p 447) says this is the corrected version (corrections February 7, 2019; June 27, 2019; January 30, 2020; October 1, 2020).

**(2) What it estimates or argues.** The Commission sets a universal "healthy reference diet" of food-group intakes with ranges for 2500 kcal a day (Table 1, p 451) and global "scientific targets" (planetary boundaries) for food production on six Earth-system processes: greenhouse gases, cropland use, freshwater use, nitrogen, phosphorus and biodiversity loss (Table 2, p 452). It uses a global food-system model with country detail to test which combinations of diet shift, improved production practice and halved food loss and waste keep 2050 food production inside those boundaries (Table 4, p 470; Figures 5-7, pp 472-475). It finds no single measure is enough, but the combination can feed "about 10 billion people" a healthy diet inside the boundaries (p 448 key messages; p 485 conclusion). It states that healthy diets from sustainable food systems "are possible for up to 10 billion people but becomes increasingly unlikely past this population threshold" (p 453).

**(3) Method family.** Scenario model (global food-system model under planetary-boundary constraints) with a reference diet set from the health evidence. B17 called it "nutrition optimisation"; the text does not optimise a diet, it sets intakes by judgment of the health literature (pp 453-459), so "scenario model with a reference diet" fits better.

**(5) Figures bearing on Australia or a capacity range that our registers lack.**
* **A stated global ceiling:** healthy diets inside the boundaries "are possible for up to 10 billion people but becomes increasingly unlikely past this population threshold" (p 453). This is the nearest thing in the paper to a global carrying-capacity figure and is the figure the methods paper should cite, not just "about 10 billion".
* **Population basis:** the analysis follows SSP2, "9.2 billion people" in 2050 (p 453), while the headline is about 10 billion and the UN figure given is 9.8 billion (p 453).
* **Cropland boundary:** 13 million km2 (uncertainty 11-15) (Table 2, p 452; p 469); 2010 baseline 12.6 million km2 (Figure 6, p 473). Divided by about 10 billion this is roughly 0.13 ha of cropland a person (our arithmetic, not printed). Comparable with Peters et al. 2016's 0.13 ha vegetarian figure (L015), which the Commission cites as ref 221.
* **Diet alone barely moves cropland:** "dietary changes resulted in small reductions in cropland use of 0-2%" (p 472); in Figure 6 (p 473), at business-as-usual production and full waste, cropland is 21.1 million km2 on the business-as-usual diet and 20.7 on the vegan diet, while greenhouse gases fall from 9.8 to 2.1 Gt CO2-eq a year. Diet moves emissions much more than cropland in this model, because legumes and nuts yield less (p 472).
* **Energy basis:** 2500 kcal a day reference intake; other analyses use 2100 (p 454); a 2100 kcal sensitivity case is in Figure 7 (p 475). Red meat 14 g a day (0-28) (p 455; Table 1).
* **Land shares:** agriculture about 40% of ice-free land, grazing about 23% of land surface (p 468).
* **Water boundary:** 2500 km3 a year consumptive use (1000-4000) (p 464; Table 2).
* Australia: not discussed. The text names Australia only in an author affiliation (CSIRO Brisbane) and a reference (the Brisbane Declaration, ref 163).

**(6) Cites Lane?** No. All reference pages (pp 485-492) read; no Lane in references or text. It does cite Peters et al. 2016 (ref 221), Tilman and Clark 2014 (ref 5), Clark and Tilman 2017 (ref 32) and Tilman et al. 2011 (ref 140).

## 2. Tilman and Clark 2014, L120

**(1) Citation as printed.** David Tilman and Michael Clark, "Global diets link environmental sustainability and human health", *Nature* 515: 518-522, 27 November 2014. doi:10.1038/nature13959. Received 20 April 2014; accepted 13 October 2014; published online 12 November 2014.

**(2) What it estimates or argues.** From 555 life-cycle analyses of 82 crop and animal products it gives cradle-to-farm-gate greenhouse gas emissions per kcal, per serving and per gram of protein for 22 food groups (Fig 1; Extended Data Tables 1-3). From FAO data for 1961-2009 for 100 nations in eight economic groups it fits income-dependent demand curves and projects a 2050 "income-dependent" global diet (Fig 2; Methods). It compares that diet with Mediterranean, pescetarian and vegetarian diets for health outcomes (cohort studies, Fig 3) and for 2050 emissions and cropland across 243 scenarios of yield, waste, pasture productivity, feed efficiency and trade (Fig 4; Extended Data Table 6). It concludes that the dietary transition, if unchecked, drives a large rise in food-production emissions and cropland clearing, and that the alternative diets would cut both while improving health (pp 518, 520-521).

**(3) Method family.** Scenario model built on a footprint (LCA meta-analysis) input; the 2050 cropland part is land per diet. B20's "land per diet" covers the cropland result only.

**(4) Register figures (L120 `what_taken`).**

| Figure | Where | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| Unchecked diet trends: about 80% rise in agricultural GHG by 2050 | Abstract p 518; derivation p 520 | Confirmed with a difference. The abstract says the diet trends "would be a major contributor to an estimated 80 per cent increase in global agricultural greenhouse gas emissions from food production and to global land clearing". The body figure is "an estimated 80% increase in global GHG emissions from food production (from 2.27 to 4.1 Gt yr-1 of CO2-Ceq) by 2050" (p 520): a 32% rise in per-person emissions combined with 36% population growth, cradle to farm gate, **excluding land-use change**, in carbon equivalents (CO2-Ceq), not CO2-eq. It is not all agricultural GHG. |
| and more land clearing | Abstract p 518; p 520-521; Extended Data Table 6b | Confirmed. The income-dependent 2050 diet needs a median 590 million ha more cropland than 2009 (Extended Data Table 6b); it needs 370 to 740 million ha more than the alternative diets, average 540 (p 521). Clearing 540 million ha would release about 0.6 Gt a year CO2-Ceq (p 521). |

**(5) Figures bearing on Australia or a capacity range that our registers lack.**
* **Australia is in economic Group A** (Extended Data Table 4, the 15 richest nations, 11.1% of world population in 2009). In 2009 Group A had 750% greater per-person demand for meat protein than Group F, and 1,400 kcal a day of "empty calories" against 285 (p 519). Actual consumption in Group A is estimated 20-25% below demand because of household waste (p 519).
* 2050 income-dependent diet against 2009 global average: 15% more total calories, 11% more protein, 31% more ruminant meat (p 519).
* Ruminant meat 62 g CO2-Ceq per g protein (Extended Data Table 3), about 250 times legumes per gram of protein (p 518). Protein conversion ratio for beef 20.0 (Extended Data Table 7).
* Each 1% gain in yield, trade, feed efficiency, waste reduction or pasture productivity cuts 2050 cropland by 1.3 to 8.8 million ha depending on diet (Extended Data Table 6a). Vegetarian diet: median cropland 16 million ha below 2009 (Extended Data Table 6b).
* The paper says ruminant production on land unsuitable for crops can raise food security (p 518), which bears on the grazing design choice in section 6 of the methods paper.
* Extended Data Table 1 lists source LCAs including Peters 2010 (Env Sci Technol 44: 1327-1332) and Ridoutt 2012 (Agric Syst 120: 2-9); the table does not state their countries.

## 3. Tilman et al. 2011, L111

**(1) Citation as printed.** David Tilman, Christian Balzer, Jason Hill and Belinda L. Befort, "Global food demand and the sustainable intensification of agriculture", *PNAS* 108(50): 20260-20264, December 13, 2011. www.pnas.org/cgi/doi/10.1073/pnas.1116437108 (DOI 10.1073/pnas.1116437108). Contributed by David Tilman, October 12, 2011.

**(2) What it estimates or argues.** For 100 nations (91% of 2006 population) in seven income groups it finds per-person crop demand for calories and protein (food, feed and losses) rises with per-person GDP in a consistent way since 1961 (Fig 1). With GDP and UN population forecasts it projects 2050 global crop demand (p 20260; Fig 2). It regresses yields on nitrogen use and other variables and builds four 2050 yield curves (current technology, improvement only, technology transfer only, both) to compare land clearing, GHG and nitrogen use under four trajectories (Fig 3). It concludes that moderate intensification of existing cropland in under-yielding nations (land sparing) meets 2050 demand with far less clearing and emissions than the past trend.

## 4. Poore and Nemecek 2018, L115

**(1) Citation as printed.** J. Poore and T. Nemecek, "Reducing food's environmental impacts through producers and consumers", *Science* 360: 987-992 (2018), 1 June 2018; issue 360(6392). DOI 10.1126/science.aaq0216. Submitted 5 October 2017; resubmitted 8 December 2017; accepted 17 April 2018. Each page carries "Erratum 22 February 2019. See Erratum."; the erratum itself is not in the file.

**(2) What it estimates or argues.** It builds a harmonised database from 570 studies covering about 38,700 commercial farms in 119 countries and 40 products (about 90% of global protein and calorie intake), with five indicators: land use, scarcity-weighted water, GHG, acidification and eutrophication (p 987). Impacts vary widely and are skewed among producers of the same product, and the lowest-impact animal products typically exceed vegetable substitutes (pp 987-990; Figs 1 and 3). It argues producers should monitor their own impacts and choose among several practices against targets, and that dietary change delivers benefits producers cannot (pp 990-991).

**(3) Method family.** Footprint (life-cycle meta-analysis).

**(4) Register figure.**

| Figure | Where | Verdict |

**(5) Figures bearing on Australia or a capacity range that our registers lack.**
* **Australia named:** average farm size ranges from 0.5 ha in Bangladesh to 3000 ha in Australia (p 987); in the Australian wheat belt, low-input farming puts both output per hectare and GHG in the bottom 15% globally (p 989); Australian conventional and no-till wheat farms and an Australian grass-fed beef farm appear among the below-median GHG examples (Fig 2C, 2D, p 989).

## 5. Hunter et al. 2017, L113

**(2) What it estimates or argues.** It argues the common call to double food production by 2050 rests on misreadings of two projections (FAO, Alexandratos and Bruinsma 2012; Tilman et al. 2011): ignoring the base year, and confusing FAO's price-weighted index with mass (pp 386-388). **(3) Method family.** Concept review (a re-basing of others' projections). B20 called it "scenario model"; it builds no model of its own.

## Claims whose characterisation of the paper the text does not support

No outline or draft (`papers/`) names any of the six papers, and no claims-register row cites them, so no outline claim fails. The characterisations that do not hold are in the literature register and in reports B17 and B20, which feed the methods paper's section 6:

1. **L120 and B20 (Tilman and Clark 2014): "about 80% rise in agricultural GHG".** The 80% is GHG from food production, cradle to farm gate, excluding land clearing (2.27 to 4.1 Gt CO2-Ceq a year, p 520). It is not all agricultural GHG.
2. **B17 point 3 ("Diet decides the answer": EAT-Lancet "points the same way").** For land, EAT-Lancet points the other way: diet change alone cuts global cropland only 0-2% (p 472; Fig 6: 21.1 against 20.7 million km2 for the business-as-usual and vegan diets). It supports diet as the main lever for emissions, not for cropland.
