# Referee report, second round: Paper A (results), Population and Environment

Referee standpoint: population and environment research, demographic projections and resource constraints. Materials read: the revised manuscript, the supplementary material, the reference list and the highlights and keywords. The drafter-notes appendix and the bracketed flags were not considered.

## 1. Summary of the submission

The paper brings a published Australian land-based carrying capacity model forward from a 2011 data year to a 2025 data year, holding the method fixed, and asks how many people each of 53 regions (52 NRM regions plus the Northern Territory) could feed from its own land with no trade. It reports that national short-term capacity, on the diet and production of the data year in an average year, rose from 33.3 to 41.5 million while the population rose from 22.3 to 27.6 million, so the ratio stayed near 1.5. A Shapley-style average over all 362,880 orderings of nine data layers attributes the rise to livestock output, consumption settings and a lower red-meat share, with land and livestock entangled through a shared grazing-area denominator. A long-term "100-year self-sufficiency plan" preset gives about 15.6 million, covering 57% of the 2025 population, and that figure is driven chiefly by the 1-in-100 cereal weather factor and the liquid fuel setting. The paper then tests the regions under bad years built from a cereal-yield record, Monte Carlo ranges over 13 or 19 sampled choices, and three method tiers; shows a "scale curve" in which forced self-sufficiency at SA2 level keeps only 37% of national capacity on state yields, attributed to the model's rule that cropland can stand in for pasture but not the reverse; and discusses one-at-a-time measures (livestock productivity, diet, waste, grain stores, drought-proofing) that would widen the margin.

## 2. Overall assessment

The question suits Population and Environment, which published the two predecessor papers, and the revision is careful in several respects: it now puts both years' regional populations on one ABS series and allocation, it averages the decomposition over all layer orderings instead of reporting one walk, it labels artefacts and non-robust flips openly, and it states plainly that the weather allowance is a cereal factor untested on pasture-fed output. Those are real improvements and the paper is more honest about its limits than most work of this kind. The contribution, however, remains mainly an update of one model's outputs, and the demographic side, which is what this journal's readers will come for, is thin: the inputs on the capacity side refer to different periods from the populations they are set against, the comparison with population projections is a single sentence that treats capacity as fixed, and the regional robustness test leaves out the two choices that carry most of the national uncertainty. My recommendation is **major revision**. The analyses are largely in hand; what is needed is a sharper statement of what the 2011 to 2025 comparison can and cannot show, a regional uncertainty test that matches the national one, and a proper treatment of projections, together with a much leaner presentation.

## 3. Major issues

**3.1 The two "data years" are not the years named, and the numerator and denominator refer to different periods.** Section 2 and Table S11 show that the "2011" capacity rests on land as a five-year mean of 2005-06 to 2009-10, crop yields from 2006-07 to 2009-10 and livestock output from the archive years 2006 to 2010, while the "2025" capacity rests on land for 2020-21, crop yields from 2016-17 to 2020-21 joined to 2022-23 to 2024-25, livestock for 2020-21 to 2024-25 divided by the 2020-21 grazing area, and diet moved by FAO food balance means of 2010-12 to 2021-23. The populations, by contrast, are ERP at 30 June 2011 and 30 June 2025. So the paper compares production of roughly 2008 with production of roughly 2021 to 2023, but sets each against a population three to four years later. The headline "capacity stayed about 1.5 times the population" (Abstract; section 3) and the status change "0.670 to 0.665" depend on this pairing. This matters because the reader of a population journal will read the ratio as contemporaneous, and because growth of about 1.6% a year means a three-year lag moves the denominator by roughly 5%, which is the same order as the change being discussed. The paper should state a reference period for each input in the main text (a compact version of Table S11 belongs in section 2, not the supplement), say what "2011" and "2025" mean, and either show the ratio against populations at the midpoint of each input window or argue why the 30 June figures are the right denominator. The same table should state the ERP vintage used for both years (preliminary, revised or final; rebased on which census) and confirm that the 2011 series is the post-2021 rebased series on 2021 SA2, since ABS revisions after the 2016 and 2021 censuses changed the 2011 figures.

**3.2 Part of the land and livestock change is a change of statistical definition.** Table S11 says that "the ABS changed its land-use categories in 2016, so part of the national pasture fall, from 3,639,794 to 3,318,689 km2, is a change of definition". The land layer is then reported in Table 2 as taking 3.06 million people off short-term capacity, and the livestock layer, whose 2025 yields are divided by that smaller 2020-21 grazing area, as adding 5.50 million. Section 4 concedes that the two are not independent and gives their sum, 2.4 million, but the abstract and highlight still name "livestock" as a cause of the rise. If part of the pasture fall is definitional, then part of the livestock "gain" is the same definitional change seen from the other side, and the 2.4 million combined figure also rests partly on it, through the stocking-rate arithmetic shown for Avon in Section S1 (yield 2.06 times, of which 1.59 times is the area). The decomposition should report land and livestock as one layer in the main table, estimate how much of the pasture fall is the 2016 reclassification (ABS published bridging guidance and a 2015-16 overlap), and drop "livestock" from the abstract as a separate cause unless it survives that adjustment. An external check is available and should be used: section 1.5 says "no source read here measures output across 2011 to 2025", but ABARES and ABS publish annual production of beef, sheep meat, milk and grains for the whole period. A comparison of national livestock product output in the two windows with the livestock layer's implied change would show whether the layer is plausible.

**3.3 The regional robustness test omits the choices that dominate national uncertainty.** Section 9.1 states that the regional ranges "come from 1,000 draws of the first 13 choices ... because the six added choices were drawn nationally only", and Table S8 shows that two of the six, the source of the land areas (32%) and the energy requirement (27%), carry nearly three fifths of the short-term national variance. The 13-choice national 5th to 95th factor is 1.26; the 19-choice factor is 1.58, with a 5th percentile at 0.75 of the base (Table S7). The regional flip test, the "clean and robust" labels and the regional counts at 1 in 10 and 1 in 20 therefore rest on the narrower range. The consequence is concrete: North Tasmania's 2025 capacity is 187,593 against a population of 156,393 (Table 7), and its 13-choice 5th percentile is 174,487; scaled by the national 19-choice 5th percentile it would be about 141,000, below its population. The same may hold for Mackay Whitsunday and South West Queensland. Since these three are the only flips the paper calls clean and robust, and they appear in the conclusions, the regional test needs the full set of choices. If the land-source and energy draws cannot be run by region, the paper should at least apply the national widening proportionally and say which flips survive, and it should stop calling the remaining flips robust.

**3.4 Change between years is not tested, yet it is the paper's headline.** The 2011 inputs carry no ranges, and the paper says so (sections 7.1 and 9.1: the 2011 figures "lie inside the 2025 ranges, and 2011 was not sampled"). That honesty is welcome, but the abstract and conclusions still open with "rose from 33.3 to 41.5 million" with no qualifier. With the 19-choice range at 31.1 to 49.2 million, a median of 39.0 million rather than the base of 41.5 (Table S7), and the base described as "not central", the reader cannot tell whether the national rise is larger than the uncertainty in either year. Most of the 13 sampled choices (diet shares, meat window, organic factors, resilience table) have 2011 analogues; drawing them for 2011 on the same distributions and reporting the distribution of the paired difference would answer the question directly. If that is not possible, the abstract should say that the rise is a comparison at fixed settings, as section 9.1 does.

**3.5 The bad-year results compare two different weather tables, and the drought factor itself needs more scrutiny.** Section 2 notes that "the 2011 releases use the thesis table, 0.599 at 20 years", while 2025 uses a new 153-season table with 0.634 at 20 years. Table 6 then reports the 1-in-20 multiple as 0.89 in 2011 and 0.95 in 2025. Because the average-year multiples are 1.49 and 1.50, essentially all of the apparent improvement at 1 in 20 (and the move in people over capacity from 1.12 to 1.05 in section 8.1) is the change of weather table, not a change in land, production or population. The paper should either apply one table to both years or say explicitly that the between-year difference at each return period is a table difference. On the factor itself: it rests on a detrended ratio over a ten-observation window from 1861, which is reasonable for a cereal series but invites three questions that the supplement does not answer. First, the early seasons (1861, 1866, 1871, then from 1875-76) are thin and drawn from a different crop geography; the 1-in-100 factor of 0.456 is an interpolation between the third-worst season (1941) and the worst (1915), so it is set by two observations, one over a century old. Second, Hochman et al. (2017), cited in section 1.5, document a climate trend in water-limited yield since 1990; a stationary return-period table drawn from 1861 to 2025 does not reflect that, and the paper should say whether the recent 35 years alone give a different 1-in-10 or 1-in-20 factor. Third, the "failure probability of about 5.8% a year, 1 in 17" (section 8.1; Section S8) is reported to two significant figures from a linear interpolation between two anchors, and S8 shows that a log interpolation gives 6.1%; given the 19-choice ranges in Table S9 (37% of draws below the population at 1 in 10), a single probability is spurious precision and should be replaced by the range or dropped from the conclusions.

**3.6 Projections are invoked but not analysed.** Section 1.1 cites the 2023 Intergenerational Report (40.5 million in 2062-63) and the 2025 Population Statement (41 million by 2065-66), and section 8.1 offers one sentence: capacity at 2025 data, 41.5 million, "is about equal to" these projections, and at 1 in 10 "falls short of both". For a population journal this is the place where most readers will look first, and it is underdeveloped in four ways. (a) It sets a 2025 capacity against a population 40 years later, while the paper's own decomposition shows capacity moving 24.6% in 14 years; the comparison needs either a statement that capacity is held at 2025 data by assumption or a sensitivity to plausible capacity paths. (b) The long-term preset, which the paper calls a 100-year plan, is the natural comparator for a 40-year projection, and on it the 2062-63 projection is about 2.6 times capacity; that is not stated. (c) Official projections come with alternative assumptions (the Population Statement and ABS Population Projections publish high and low fertility and migration series); a single central number gives no sense of the spread. (d) The regional story is the paper's distinctive contribution, and state agencies publish regional projections (for example the NSW Common Planning Assumptions, Victoria in Future and the Queensland Government Statistician's projections) that could be aggregated to the NRM regions with the same residential-land allocation used for ERP. Even a simple exercise showing which regions would cross their 2025 capacity under the projected regional populations would answer the second research question in a way a planner could use. If the authors prefer to keep projections out of scope, the section 1.1 framing and the section 8.1 sentence should be cut back accordingly.

**3.7 Per-person requirements are fixed while the population's composition changes.** Capacity is computed on a per-person diet and energy requirement (Section S4 gives "a food amount from 1.000 to 1.286 times the sedentary requirement"), applied identically to 2011 and 2025 and to every region. Australia's age structure aged markedly between 2011 and 2025, and regional age structures differ widely (retirement coasts, mining regions, the capitals, the Northern Territory). Since the energy requirement alone carries 27% of short-term national variance (Table S8), the effect of age and sex composition on per-person requirement is not a second-order point. The paper should either adjust the requirement for each year's and each region's age-sex structure using ERP by age, or report the size of that adjustment nationally and argue that it is small. The same applies to the projection comparison in 3.6, since the projected populations of the 2060s are older again. A related point: ERP counts usual residents, so regions with large non-resident workforces or tourist populations (Northern Territory, Rangelands, Wet Tropics) consume more than their ERP implies; a sentence acknowledging this would suffice.

**3.8 The scale curve is mostly a consequence of the substitution rule and the partition, and the framing should say so more plainly.** The abstract says the SA2 loss is one "the model's substitution rule accounts for", and section 6 says the national test of the rule was "run for Australia only, so the rule is not shown for regions". Section 5.2 then shows that the curve is not monotone in unit size (NRM regions 44%, SA4s 58%, SA3s 44%), that random groupings at 53 units keep 68 to 81% on a national-yield basis against 33% for the NRM regions (Section S2), and that capacity is exactly proportional to land when each unit has the national land mix. Taken together these show that the loss depends on how land types are split among units and on the assumption that pasture can never carry crops, not on unit size as such. The phrase "scale curve" and the comparison with Lane et al. (2015) invite the opposite reading. In addition, three yield bases are in play (state yields, own yields and the national yield vector), with different numbers for the same level (NRM regions 44%, 63% and 33%), and the reader has to work out which basis each sentence uses. I suggest presenting the result as a partition effect, stating one basis for the main text and giving the others only in the supplement, and adding a sensitivity in which a modest share of pasture can carry crops, so that the dependence on the rule is quantified rather than asserted.

**3.9 Missing literature that this journal's readers would expect.** The paper engages well with Australian policy sources and with the local self-sufficiency work of Kinnunen et al. (2020) and Clapp (2017), but it does not place itself in the carrying capacity and population literature that Population and Environment readers know. At minimum I would expect Cohen's review of human carrying capacity estimates and the critique of single-number capacity estimates that it contains; the foodshed and local self-sufficiency studies that compute exactly this kind of regional capacity (for example Peters and colleagues' foodshed carrying capacity work and Zumkehr and Campbell on US foodsheds, beyond Kurtz et al. 2020 which appears only in the supplement); global studies of production and consumption decoupling and of land-constrained food supply at subnational scale (for example Fader et al. on spatial decoupling and Pradhan et al. on regional food self-sufficiency); Gerten et al. (2020) on feeding people within planetary boundaries; and Australian population-environment work beyond Foran and Poldy (2002), including the debate around the 2010 sustainable population strategy. The comparison of these studies' methods is said to be in a companion paper, but this paper still needs a paragraph that tells the reader where its results sit.

**3.10 Presentation obstructs the argument.** The manuscript reads in places as a results log. Release names such as `2011-fixed-3`, `2025-mirror` and `2025-store-2` and repository file paths appear in captions and running text throughout ("Built from `analysis/paper_a/regional_flips-3.csv`"); these belong in a data availability note or a supplementary table of releases, not in the paper. Two inverse ratios, "margin" (capacity over population) and "status" (people over capacity), are both used, and sentences switch between them (section 8.1 gives 1.12 and 0.89 for the same case); one ratio should be chosen. The main text carries many numbers to four or more significant figures (Table 2 to the person; North Tasmania's 139,165 and 143,631) that imply precision the ranges do not support. All eight figures are still described in words only, so the reader cannot yet see the maps on which the regional argument depends; two captions say "no plotting script exists". The supplement contains working language ("decision 92", "These single-year figures are unverified (the second route is blocked)") that should not be in a submission. Section 11 reads as a list of one-at-a-time scenarios; most could move to the supplement with a single summary table in the text.

## 4. Minor issues

1. Section numbering skips 1.2.
2. Section 1.1: "Growth of 5 million over two decades" quotes Davies (2024) about a different period from the paper's 5.3 million over 14 years; say so or choose a quotation that fits.
3. Section 1.3: "work citing them was not checked" is unclear; if it means the authors did not review the citing literature, that is a gap to fill, not a statement to make.
4. Section 2 and Table S11: the national population row for 2011 (22,326,388) is the thesis figure while the regions sum to 22,336,514 on the ABS series. The national ratio and the 23.7% growth use the thesis row. Use the ABS national ERP for both years so that national and regional figures share a base.
5. Section 2: the 2025 national population of 27,608,200 is rounded to the hundred; give the source and release date of the figure.
6. Section 2: the share of a straddling SA2's population allocated by "residential land" uses the 2023 land-use raster for both 2011 and 2025. Say how many SA2s straddle boundaries and how much population this allocation moves.
7. Section 3: "The net fall of six regions in the short term is larger than the two flips that pass the tests below" is a comparison of unlike things (a net count against a count of robust gross flips); rephrase.
8. Table 1: the column "Land-definition or boundary artefacts" counts 3 short-term and 2 long-term, but the caption says "A flip can fail more than one test"; give the overlaps so the row can be reconciled with the flip count.
9. Table 2: the caption says `2011-fixed` while the rest of the paper uses `2011-fixed-3`; the caption explains this, but a reader will stumble. Report the averages to two significant figures.
10. Section 4: "the diet with less beef" rests on FAO food balance supply, which measures availability, not intake; the fall in beef and veal supply from 39.1 to 25.9 kg a person is large and should be checked against ABS Apparent Consumption of Selected Foodstuffs.
11. Section 4: the consumption layer "also carries the fuel, biofuel and timber settings, so it is not wholly diet"; the abstract nevertheless groups it with the red meat mix as if both were diet. Either split the layer or describe it accurately in the abstract.
12. Section 5.4 and Table 3: the 2011 SA-level shares are against a reference on 2020-21 land; this makes the 2011 column of Table 3 non-comparable down the column. Mark those cells in the table itself.
13. Section 7: "100-year plan" and "100-year self-sufficiency plan" suggest a planning horizon; the preset is a 1-in-100 worst year with fixed 2025 population and settings. A term such as "long-term self-sufficiency preset" would avoid the reader inferring a time path.
14. Table 5: the "fibre need (23 to 11.5)" row lacks units.
15. Section 8.2: "1 in 20 is not a threshold" (section 8.1) and "the rise from three regions to five is not a finding on its own" are helpful cautions but would be clearer stated once in a short box on what the bad-year results can bear.
16. Section 10: the choice of the five regions "from the largest movers" is not documented; give the selection rule.
17. Section 11.7: the FAOSTAT meat surplus of 57% is said not to be comparable with the model's 1.50; then it need not be in the text.
18. Section 12.4: the oil-stock figure from Soliman Hunter and Taylor (2021) is cited to "PDF p. 11"; use the journal's page or section.
19. References are in Elsevier Harvard form, and the declarations use Elsevier headings; Population and Environment is a Springer journal with its own reference and declarations format.
20. Bolleter (2018): the DOI suffix indicates a 2019 issue; confirm the year. Gifford et al. (1975) is cited at second hand through Henry and Godden (1995); cite the original if it can be obtained.
21. "Mcdougall" should be checked against the article's spelling of the author's name.
22. Keywords: consider adding "population projections" or "regional population" if section 3.6 above is taken up, and "food security".

## 5. Questions to the author

1. Which ERP vintage is used for 30 June 2011 and 30 June 2025, and has the 2011 regional series been checked against the post-2021-census rebased ABS series on 2021 SA2?
2. Can the 13 sampled choices that have 2011 analogues be drawn for 2011, so that the distribution of the change 2011 to 2025 can be reported?
3. Can the land-source and energy-requirement draws be run at regional level, and if so which of the three "clean and robust" flips survive?
4. How much of the 2016 to 2021 national pasture fall does the ABS land-use reclassification account for, and what are the land and livestock effects with the definitional part removed?
5. What does the bad-year comparison between 2011 and 2025 look like when both years use the new 153-season table?
6. What 1-in-10 and 1-in-20 factors does the cereal record give for 1990 to 2025 alone?
7. How large is the change in mean per-person energy requirement implied by the change in age-sex structure from 2011 to 2025, nationally and for the regions with the oldest and youngest populations?
8. Under projected regional populations (state government series aggregated to NRM regions), which regions would pass their 2025 capacity, and by when?
9. How does the scale result change if a small share of pasture is allowed to carry crops?
10. Is the single-year series of capacity across 2016-17 to 2024-25 (Section S4) to be verified and reported, given that it is the only observed check on the drought factor?

## 6. Note on the abstract and highlights

The abstract is accurate in most of its numbers and, unlike many, it states the cereal basis of the weather allowance and the Monte Carlo qualification on the bad-year result. It still says more than the evidence carries in three places. First, it opens with the rise "from 33.3 to 41.5 million" without saying that 2011 carries no range and that the 2011 value lies inside the 2025 range; the qualifier in section 9.1 belongs in the abstract. Second, "livestock, consumption and the red meat mix account for the rise" names livestock as a separate cause when the paper's own section 4 says the livestock and land layers share one denominator and that part of the pasture fall is definitional; the next clause gives the combined 2.4 million, which is the defensible figure. Third, "a loss the model's substitution rule accounts for" is correct as a statement about the model, but readers will take the SA2 figure of 37% as a finding about Australia; it should say that the figure follows from the rule and the partition. The abstract also does not mention the regional flips at all, though they are the subject of two of the five research questions, nor anything about projections, though section 1.1 opens with them.

The highlights are short and mostly faithful. The second ("Livestock, consumption, red meat mix account for the rise; land and livestock overlap") has the same problem as the abstract. The third ("Base capacity is above population at 1 in 10, below at 1 in 20; ranges include it") is unclear: "it" should be "the population". The fifth ("If every SA2 feeds itself, capacity is 37% of the national figure on state yields") omits that the own-yield bound is 54% and that the loss follows from a model rule. None of the highlights says that all results are for a single model with no trade, which is the frame a reader needs.

## 7. Five points I would most want answered in a response letter

1. A regional robustness test that includes the land-source and energy-requirement choices, with a revised list of flips that survive it, and the conclusions and abstract changed to match (major issue 3.3).
2. A clear statement of the reference period of every input in each "data year", with the population denominator justified against it, and the ERP vintage named (major issue 3.1).
3. An estimate of the definitional part of the pasture fall and a land-plus-livestock decomposition with it removed, checked against published national livestock output (major issue 3.2).
4. Either a sampled 2011 or a plain statement in the abstract and conclusions that the 2011 to 2025 change is untested across the settings; and the bad-year comparison redone on one weather table for both years (major issues 3.4 and 3.5).
5. A real treatment of projections, including the long-term preset against the projected populations, official projection variants and, ideally, regional projections, with the per-person requirement adjusted for age structure; or, if the authors decline, removal of the projection framing from the introduction and section 8.1 (major issues 3.6 and 3.7).

On these five a further round would be needed; if the authors address 1, 2 and 4 fully and give a reasoned answer on 3 and 5, the paper could then be judged on minor points.
