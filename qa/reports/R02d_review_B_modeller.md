# Referee report: "Well-known carrying-capacity methods tested on common Australian data" (Paper B, methods), submitted to Ecological Economics

Reviewer B, from the standpoint of quantitative modelling of food systems and land use. Files read: `paper_B_draft1.md` (main text, excluding the drafter-notes appendix and bracketed flags), `supplementary.md`, `references.md`, `highlights.md`.

## 1. Summary of the submission

The manuscript asks why published estimates of how many people Australia can feed run from about 9.4 to 100 million, a factor of 10.6. It catalogues the main method families (land-and-diet balance, energy and protein balance, commodity balance, export-inclusive counts, the ecological footprint, land-per-diet and people-per-hectare indices, optimisation, resource-flow, water-bounded and failure-probability methods), then re-implements six of them on one Australian data set (FAOSTAT 2021 to 2023, a 2025 population of 27.6 million, 363.5 Mha of agricultural land), checking each re-implementation against its authors' published figures where possible. The author's own land-and-diet model enters at three tiers (as built, corrected, improved). The central claim is that the spread among published figures is produced by what each method counts, not by its inputs: after harmonising inputs, the range among methods stays at 12 to 343 million, and on a ratio scale inputs account for about 10% (10 to 18% depending on the rows used) of each reading's distance from the corrected model. The paper then uses the author's model to measure, one at a time, how far each design choice (exports, diet, waste, drought allowance, land base, unit of self-sufficiency, time frame) moves the answer, and contrasts these factors (1.09 to 7.88) with a Monte Carlo spread across "defensible settings" of 1.26. It closes with a purpose-by-method rating table and a twelve-item reporting checklist.

## 2. Overall assessment

The question is worth asking and the paper does something rare: it re-runs published methods on one data set and is candid about where it could not reproduce them. The fidelity checks, the harvest-year pairing for winter crops, the reproduction of Henry and Godden's arithmetic and the reporting checklist are genuine contributions, and a careful version of this paper would interest Ecological Economics readers, who have argued about carrying capacity and the footprint in this journal for thirty years. As submitted, however, the headline claim, that "the gap between the methods is the method", rests on an input/method split that is drawn so that the decisive variable (world-average against actual productivity) is classed as method; on an attribution statistic that is defined against the author's own model and is not shown to be robust; and on a sensitivity analysis too narrow to support the contrast it is used for. The model's own key mechanism, the pairing of pasture-fed food with crops, is offered as the explanation of the remaining gap but is explicitly not tested. My recommendation is **major revision**. The analyses needed are within reach of the author's own pipeline, and the paper could be strong once they are done.

## 3. Major issues

**M1. The input/method split is drawn so that the main result is close to built in.** In section 5.11 every method "kept its defining choices, such as world-average productivity for the footprint and HALF and cropland only for people per hectare", and only the remaining inputs were equalised. But the "as run" readings in Table 5 column (a) were already computed on the common 2025 data of section 5.1 (Table 3), so column (b) changes only a few residual inputs: the input factor for HALF is 1.00, and the spread "12 to 343 million as run and 12 to 343 million harmonised" is unchanged largely because almost nothing was changed. More importantly, the paper's own local-productivity test (section 5.11, Table 5 column (e)) shows that when the footprint and HALF use Australia's actual productivity, they fall from 139 to 343 and 276 million to 64.8 to 84.6 and 76.0 million: from three to eight times the model to about 1.5 to 2 times. Whether a hectare is valued at world-average or Australian yields is, in the vocabulary of a land-use modeller, an input (a yield and land-productivity assumption), not a property of the accounting identity. Reclassify it and the bulk of the gap moves from "method" to "data". The paper should (a) state an explicit, defensible rule for what counts as an input and what as a method, before the results; (b) report the attribution under at least two classifications, one of which treats the productivity basis and the energy requirement per person as inputs; and (c) temper the abstract and highlights accordingly. As it stands, "inputs explain about 10% of the distance" is a statement about the split, not about the world.

**M2. The attribution statistic is undefined in the text, depends on the reference model and on row choice, and is reported as more general than it is.** Section 5.11 says that "on a ratio scale, inputs account for about 10% of the distance of each reading from the corrected model and the methods for about 90%, on one set of rows with the methods weighted equally; on other row sets the inputs' share is 10 to 18%." I could not find the formula, the row sets, or the weights anywhere in the main text or supplement. Three problems follow. First, the distance is measured from the author's corrected model, so the decomposition is of "distance from my model", not of the spread among methods; a different anchor (the median, or each pair of methods) could give a different share. Second, "each reading's distance" in the abstract is wrong: the 10% is an aggregate, and the per-method input factors in Table 5 column (c) run from 0.62 to 1.42, so for people per hectare and the energy balance the inputs moved the reading by 30 to 40%. Third, the choice of rows (as-run ranges or point values, which footprint build, which commodity) visibly matters (10 to 18%). What would resolve it: a proper two-way decomposition on log readings, method by input set, with the input sets defined in advance (for instance 1990-era and 2021-23 data, FAO and ABS land, intake and supply energy bases), reported as a variance share with an interval over row choices and anchors; or, at minimum, the formula, the rows, and a table showing the share under each defensible choice. Ranges defined by their extremes (12 to 343) are a poor statistic for "did the spread narrow" because the endpoints are a single-commodity fish reading and a footprint build; report a dispersion measure that does not hang on two cells.

**M3. The proposed mechanism for the remaining gap is not tested, although it could be.** Sections 5.11 and 7.6 say the residual gap "is consistent with the pairing of pasture-fed food with crops, which neither method carries; the pairing was not removed from the model to test it", and "an interpretation not tested". This is the one causal claim in the paper that a modeller would most want checked, and the author owns the model in which it can be switched off. Table S2 already shows that the default diet needs 5.93 ha of pasture a person against 0.30 ha of cropland, so the model's answer is in effect a beef-and-lamb capacity of the rangelands. Please run the model with the pairing relaxed (substitution between pasture-fed and crop-fed protein, or each land class solved separately) and report how much of the 1.5 to 2 times residual it closes. Without this, sections 5.11 and 7.6 should not use the pairing as an explanation.

**M4. The sensitivity analysis is too narrow to carry the contrast drawn in the abstract and section 6.10.** The paper's second headline is that "by definition alone the model spans 18.9 to 55.3 million, while its spread across defensible settings is a factor of 1.26". The 1.26 is a 5th-to-95th percentile range from 1,000 Latin hypercube draws over 13 "uncertain choices" (section S5), of which one, the window of herd and meat data, explains 81% of the variance and five range ends "are the author's judgement and have no outside source". The analysis leaves out the uncertainties a land-use modeller would put first: pasture productivity and stocking rates on 331.87 Mha of mostly arid rangeland, crop yields and their trend (the paper itself cites Hochman et al. 2017 on declining yield potential), the classification of FAOSTAT permanent meadows and pastures, nutrient composition and the energy requirement per person, and structural choices such as the fixed diet. It also uses "an additive shortcut", and the method for the variance shares is not stated (first-order Sobol' indices, a regression on the draws, or something else). The contrast with the design choices is not like with like: the design factors are one-at-a-time swings set at the ends that other methods take, the Monte Carlo is a central interval of a joint draw. Please (a) widen the parameter set to include yield, pasture productivity and requirement uncertainty with sourced ranges; (b) state the sensitivity estimator and report total-order indices so interactions are visible (the waste factor is 1.33 on the corrected release and 1.17 on the improved, which is already evidence of interaction); (c) report the full min-to-max as well as the 5 to 95 range; and (d) rephrase "defensible settings" as "the settings varied here". The key methods references for this (McKay et al. 1979; Helton and Davis 2003; Saltelli et al. 2008) are each marked "not seen; cited from its abstract", which a methods paper cannot leave standing.

**M5. Several "design choices" in Table 6 are not measured in the model, and the ordering mixes bundles with single factors.** The abstract says the design choices "were measured one at a time in the model". Three rows were not. The proportional export count (factor 2.41) is the CSIRO and UQ "around 100 million" divided by the model's 41.5 million; it is a comparison of two published figures, not the model run with exports counted, and the report's own parts sum to 90 million, which would give 2.17. The two energy-count rows (4.43 and 6.36) set "exports alone" against the model's domestic capacity, which compares a quantity of a different kind. The time frame (2.66) bundles a low-meat diet, organic yields, no waste, a 100-year drought allowance and a 30% reserve, so it double counts other rows. The optimised diet C (7.88) feeds 422.3 million on 0.06 ha of cropland and 0.13 ha of pasture a person, a diet chosen freely by a linear programme with ten micronutrients and, as far as I can see, no acceptability or cultural constraint; ranking it as the most influential "design choice" says more about the optimiser than about published estimates. Please run exports through the model itself (for example, domestic diet plus an export basket held on the land), remove or separately label the bundled time-frame row, add acceptability bounds (departure from current diet) to the optimised diets as diet-optimisation practice expects, and re-rank.

**M6. Data harmonisation leaves important bases mixed or unstated.** (a) Population: the per-person food balance rests on FAO's 26.20 million while the methods use the model's 27.6 million (section S3), a 5% mismatch that feeds directly into "people fed". (b) Energy basis: the harmonised consumption basis is FAO supply, 3,525 kcal a day, which includes retail and household waste, while Cassidy uses 2,700, Fearnside 2,550, Henry and Godden's factor implies 15.6 MJ (about 3,730 kcal), and the model's own diet energy is not stated. "People fed" is linear in this number, so it is a first-order input and needs one stated basis, with the alternatives as a sensitivity. (c) Land: 331.87 Mha of "pasture" from FAOSTAT is counted in full in the footprint at world-average productivity; the energy balance carries forward 1990-91 sown pasture because "the 2025 in-use land was not measured"; and the unit-of-self-sufficiency comparison sits on 2020-21 land for SA2s against the release land for the others. (d) Vintage: "the model's 2025 data were taken as the same vintage as FAOSTAT's data for 2021 to 2023", an assumption that should be checked, given the harvest-year offset the paper itself found for winter crops. A table of every basis (population, energy, land classes, data years, exports) by method, as run and as harmonised, would resolve most of this and is also exactly what the checklist asks of others.

**M7. The fidelity and gap-attribution results are weaker than the summary claims.** Section 1 and section 9.1 say that residuals "traced, where traceable, to data years, revisions and rounding". The supplement does not support that generality. For the footprint, crop land for plant foods is not reproduced by any build (0.074 to 0.142 ha against 0.312, a factor of two to four), wool pasture is not reproduced, and the builds disagree on pasture by up to 36%. For people per hectare, results are 22 to 42% above the paper and the United States is not reproduced. HALF for New Zealand is 94.8 against 135.76, and Australia's 0.8% residual "is not closed by any cause tested". Table S1 reports one-at-a-time "shares of the residual explained" of -915%, -621% and 227%; when residuals are under 1%, shares of this size mean the attribution is not identifying causes, and presenting them as shares invites misreading. Please (a) restate the conclusion as what it is (some residuals are within rounding, several are unexplained, and some implementations are not faithful), (b) replace the share table with the adjusted value under each cause, and (c) say plainly which test readings in Table 3 rest on an implementation that failed its fidelity check, since the footprint's 103 to 343 million and the people-per-hectare readings are used in the headline "three to eight times the model".

**M8. The failure-probability "method" is the model with a weather scalar, and the scalar is questionable for a pasture-bound model.** Section 5.8 applies "the failure frame ... as a test of the idea, not as an independent method", yet it is counted among the "seven methods" in the abstract and highlights and rated "well suited" for food security in Table 7. Mechanically, the drought allowance scales the harvest to the worst year in T from "a 150-year record of wheat, oats and barley yields". Since the default diet is bound by pasture-fed meat and dairy (sections 6.2, 6.4), applying a winter-cereal yield index to all yields, pasture and herds included, is a strong assumption: herds buffer a single bad year and fail over runs of years through destocking and rebuilding, which section 8.1 concedes no method captures. The record is treated as stationary despite the yield trends cited in section 8.1. The curve rests on six interpolated run points, and the failure probability at the 2025 population is 1.5%, 2% or 5.8 to 6.1% depending on the release (section S3.6). Please (a) count the methods honestly (five external methods plus the author's model read two ways), (b) justify or replace the single scalar with product-specific (at least crop versus pasture) and serially correlated shocks, (c) show sensitivity to detrending the record, and (d) report the failure probabilities with their dependence on the release in the main text, not only in the supplement.

**M9. The comparison is anchored on, and the purposes rated by, the author's own model.** Distances are measured from the corrected model; the aggregate methods are described as reading "about three to eight times the model"; the only method rated "well suited" for all four purposes in Table 7 is the author's own; and the ratings are "the author's judgement, re-derived blind by a second reader". The competing-interests statement is welcome, but disclosure does not remove the design problem. The model is itself a land-and-diet method with strong choices (a fixed diet with 12% meat and eggs, 57.8% of it red; United States loss factors in the thesis tier; "many constants carry no recorded source", section 8.5). Please (a) present results relative to a neutral anchor or to each other, (b) have the Table 7 ratings made by readers independent of the author against stated criteria, with agreement reported, and (c) include in the purpose table the model's own weaknesses, which section 8 lists (trade, no herd-cycle risk, unsourced constants), so that "well suited" across the board is earned rather than asserted.

**M10. Missing literature that this field and this journal would expect.** The global food-system modelling literature most relevant to the paper's own questions is absent or cited only second-hand: Porkka et al. (2013) and Pradhan et al. (2014) compute how many people domestic or local production can feed, and the latter is directly about the unit of self-sufficiency in section 6.7; Alexander et al. (2017, Agricultural Systems) on losses and inefficiencies through the food system bears on section 6.3; Kummu et al. (2012) on food losses and Kummu et al. (2020) on trade and food-system resilience bear on sections 6.3 and 8.2; Mottet et al. (2017) on livestock feed that people cannot eat bears on the pasture-crop pairing; Mueller et al. (2012) and Monfreda et al. (2008) on yields and harvested area bear on the productivity basis; Gaupp et al. (2020) on simultaneous breadbasket failures bears on section 8.1; Springmann et al. (2018) on diet and food-system scenarios bears on section 6.5. Gerten et al. (2020), central to "how many can be fed within limits", is cited only "as reported in" another paper, and Erb et al. (2016) "not seen". For Ecological Economics, the paper should engage the journal's own footprint and carrying-capacity debate, for example van den Bergh and Verbruggen (1999), Fiala (2008) and Arrow et al. (1995), and say what an economist would add: substitution, trade and prices, which the paper sets aside in one sentence ("trade is a way around its constraint, not part of it", section 8.2). That sentence carries much of the paper's framing and needs argument.

**M11. Results presentation: missing items, unstated uncertainty, inconsistent figures.** Table 2 and Figures 1 to 3 are referred to in the text (sections 4, 5.10, 6, 6.10) but are not in the submitted files, so the catalogue and the central "ladder" figure cannot be assessed. Readings in Tables 3 and 5 are ranges across builds, currencies and land cases, not uncertainty intervals, and Table 4's brackets are Monte Carlo percentiles; the paper says so in places, but the tables should mark each range's nature consistently. The energy count of exports appears as 184 to 264 million (section 6.1), 183.9 and 263.9 (Table 6) and 223.9 (Figure 3 caption), with no explanation of the third. "Factor" is defined as higher over lower (section 6), so direction is lost; a signed log ratio would read better and allow additive decomposition. The ladder of section 6.10 is, as the author admits, "a reading of the spread, not a decomposition", and its first step mixes a change of data with the correction of the method; I would drop it or replace it with a decomposition that is well defined.

## 4. Minor issues

1. Abstract and highlights: "about 9 to 100 million" omits Henry and Godden's 109 million, which section 1 concedes "sits above that span", and the 480 million of the Migration Committee; the factor of 10.6 depends on that choice of ends. State the selection rule for the span.
2. Section 1 and Table 1: the CSIRO and UQ figure is "around 100" from 27 plus 63, which is 90. Use 90 in calculations (factor 2.17, not 2.41) or explain why 100 is kept.
3. Section 1: citing the author's own three papers as "not seen; cited from their abstracts" reads strangely; either cite the papers properly or explain.
4. Several methods references (McKay et al. 1979; Helton and Davis 2003; Saltelli et al. 2008; Erb et al. 2016; Shepon et al. 2016) are "not seen"; for a methods paper these should be read.
5. Section 5.5: "Read in the footprint way, which HALF is not" and section 5.6 "Read as a capacity, which Cassidy's measure is not" are fair cautions, but then the same readings drive the headline "three to eight times the model". Say in the abstract that two of the aggregate readings are conversions the original authors did not make.
6. Table 5: column (a) is headed "As the authors would run it", but these are the readings on the common 2025 data, not the authors' own setup; for people per hectare the cell (145 to 183) mixes the 22-item reading and the all-cropland reading. Rename the column and use one basis.
7. Table 5, footprint: the "together" range in column (e) (64.8 to 84.6) and the "pasture alone" range (53.9 to 77.9) are both said to be excluded from the stated spread in some places and not others; make the exclusions consistent.
8. Section 6.5 and S4: the guideline diet at 2.5% meat and eggs needs 0.0 ha of pasture, yet 5% needs 2.29 ha; explain the switch from crop-fed to pasture-fed meat that causes the "cliff", or it will look like an artefact.
9. Section S4 says "a little pasture-fed meat beat none on the corrected release only: 2.5% meat and eggs fed 118.0 million", but Table S2 (improved release) shows 2.5% with no pasture. Clarify whether the 2.5% diet on the corrected release uses pasture.
10. Section 6.7: the SA2 comparison on 2020-21 land against release land "which give the same national capacity" should show that equality.
11. Section 6.6 and Table 1: the Jones committee's 30 to 35 million "brackets" the model's 30.8 million at 1 in 10 (section 5.8) but "cannot be placed on the model's curve" (section 6.6). Pick one framing; the second is the correct one.
12. Section 6.3: "The model's 6% setting halves its retail-and-consumer loss share" introduces a 6% setting not otherwise defined against the 12% default.
13. Section 8.2: Kinnunen et al. "11 to 28%", and Fader et al. "16%" as reported by Clapp; give these in the original.
14. Section 9.4: the archive has no DOI; Ecological Economics will expect a persistent deposit for a paper whose claim is reproducibility.
15. Units: "people a hectare" and "ha a person" should be "people per hectare" and "ha per person" throughout; state "per year" for land per person.
16. Terminology: "the balance", "the model", "the land-and-diet balance" and "the thesis method" all name the author's model; use one name.
17. Supplementary section numbering refers to "decision 57" (S2.1) and "decision 92" (preamble); these are internal references a reader cannot follow.
18. Table S1: the HALF Australia residual is negative (-0.92) while the others are positive; with shares of hundreds of per cent the sign convention needs an example.

## 5. Questions to the author

1. What exactly is the formula for the 10% share of section 5.11, and what row set, anchor and weights produce 10% and 18%? What is the share if the anchor is the median of the methods rather than your model?
2. If the productivity basis (world-average against Australian yields) were treated as an input, what share of the distance would inputs then explain?
3. What happens to the model's capacity when the fixed pairing of pasture-fed food with crops is relaxed? Does that close the 1.5 to 2 times residual left after the local-productivity adjustment?
4. How is pasture productivity represented in the model, and what part of the 331.87 Mha of FAOSTAT pasture is treated as usable? What does the Monte Carlo give when pasture productivity is varied?
5. What estimator gives the variance shares in Table S3, and what are the total-order indices?
6. What is the daily energy of the model's default diet, and how do the methods' readings change when all are put on one energy basis (intake or supply)?
7. Why is the drought scalar from winter cereals applied to pasture and livestock, and how do results change with separate crop and pasture shocks or with a detrended record?
8. Which readings in Table 3 come from implementations that failed their fidelity checks, and would the headline "three to eight times the model" survive their removal?
9. Were the Table 7 ratings made before or after the second reader saw the results, and what was the agreement?
10. Will the archive be given a DOI before publication?

## 6. The abstract and highlights

The abstract states the finding more strongly than the analysis supports. "Inputs accounted for about 10% of each reading's distance from the model" is wrong in its word "each" (it is an aggregate share, and per-method input factors reach 0.62 to 1.42) and omits that the share depends on the input/method split, the anchor (the author's model) and the rows (10 to 18%). "The gap between the methods is the method" is a slogan that the local-productivity result in section 5.11 undercuts. "Seven well-known methods" counts the author's model read through Fearnside's frame as a separate method. "The design choices ... were measured one at a time in the model" is not true of the export rows. "Its spread across defensible settings is a factor of 1.26" should be "a 5th to 95th percentile range of 1.26 across the 13 settings varied, dominated by one judged choice".

The highlights repeat these points in compressed form. "Seven methods run on one data set: the gap between them is the method" and "Equalising inputs leaves the spread; inputs explain about 10% of the distance" should be qualified as above. "By definition alone, one model spans 18.9 to 55.3 million people" is accurate as stated. "Across defensible settings the same model moves by a factor of only 1.26" overstates both the coverage of the sensitivity analysis and, through "only", its meaning. "Published estimates of Australian capacity span 9 to 100 million, a factor of 10.6" should note that the ends were chosen and that one published figure lies above them.
