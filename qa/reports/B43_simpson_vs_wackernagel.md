# B43: Simpson's conventions against Wackernagel and Rees 1996, as far as the preview shows (decision 16)

**Sources read.** Page images pp. 76, 77, 78, 79, 80, 81, 82 and 91 of Wackernagel and Rees 1996, *Our Ecological Footprint* (`Literature review/Wackernagel_Rees_1996_pages/`); the transcription `analysis/method_tests_2025/wackernagel_rees_1996_table3_3.csv` (checked against the p. 82 image: it matches); the Simpson section of `analysis/method_tests_2025/README.md`; `qa/reports/V04_simpson_food.md`; `qa/reports/V07_simpson_inference.md`; Simpson, Petroeschevsky and Lowe 2000, pp. 12 to 14 (Research Methods, Results, Table 1).

**Short answer.** On the conventions the pages state, Simpson follows the book closely. The pages give no pasture productivity and no crop yield, so they neither confirm nor rule out the 33 kg a hectare inferred in V07. The book's Canadian matrix does constrain the plant-food crop: Canada's 0.18 ha of crop (0.20 with garden) for fruit, vegetables and grain sits near the two rebuilds (0.107 and 0.142), not near Simpson's 0.312. Decision 16 cannot be closed on these pages.

Two further points the pages settle. Water (Australia sets aside 0.27 to 0.37 ha a city dweller for catchment, p. 76) and waste other than CO2 are excluded by Simpson (p. 12); the book says both belong in principle but that its own examples include only CO2 (p. 76), and Table 3.3 has no water column (p. 82), so Simpson matches the book's practice. Food packaging: the book puts food wrappings in the food row's forest cell ("f1", p. 81), as Simpson does (0.084 ha, p. 13).

A side check, outside the brief's list: at the book's paper ratios (1.8 m3 of wood a tonne, 2.3 m3 a hectare a year, p. 81), Simpson's paper forest (food 0.084, packaging 0.032, books and magazines 0.176; 0.292 ha) implies about 373 kg of paper a person, against 244 kg for Canada (p. 81). Either Australian paper use was that high or Simpson used other ratios. It does not bear on the food land question.

## 2. Do these pages supply or constrain the pasture productivity or the crop yields?

**Pasture: no figure, and no usable constraint.** The only productivities on the pages are 100 GJ of fossil energy a hectare (pp. 77, 80) and 2.3 m3 of wood a hectare a year (p. 81). Box 3.3 has three examples (energy, paper, built land; pp. 80 to 81) and none for food. No kg of meat, milk, wool or crop a hectare appears. The Canadian 0.33 ha of pasture (p. 82) would yield a rate only with Canada's 1991 consumption of beef, sheep meat and milk, which is not on these pages and not in the repository (the FAO extracts on hand hold no Canadian rows). What can be said: at V07's fitted 33.2 kg of red meat a hectare, 0.33 ha would carry 11 kg of red meat a Canadian and no dairy. If Canadian ruminant meat and dairy needed more than that, the book's pasture rate is above 33 kg a hectare or part of Canadian cattle feed sits in the crop column; the 0.42 ha of animal-products crop, larger than the pasture, points to the second. This is an inference from the shape of the table, not a figure the book prints.

**Plant crop: a constraint, not a yield.** No crop yield is printed. But the book's own Canadian row for fruit, vegetables and grain is 0.18 ha of crop plus 0.02 ha of garden (p. 82). Canadian and Australian plant-food consumption are of the same broad kind, so a reading that follows the book's convention would put Australia near 0.2 ha. Both rebuilds (0.107 and 0.142) land near that; Simpson's 0.312 is 1.7 times Canada's crop. The pages therefore support V04 and V07: the gap is in Simpson's inputs or scope (V07's candidate is total grain use including feed), not in the book's method.

## 3. What the Canadian matrix implies for animal products, and why Australia differs

| ha a person | Canada 1991 (p. 82) | Simpson 1991-92 (Table 1) | Ratio |
|---|---|---|---|
| Animal products: crop | 0.42 | 0.095 | 0.23 |
| Animal products: pasture | 0.33 | 1.758 | 5.3 |
| Animal products: crop plus pasture | 0.75 | 1.853 | 2.5 |
| Animal products: energy | 0.19 | 0.30 | 1.6 |
| Plant foods: crop (plus garden) | 0.18 (0.20) | 0.312 (0.344) | 1.7 |
| Food row total | 1.30 | 2.711 | 2.1 |
| Clothing: pasture (wool) | 0.13 | 0.854 | 6.6 |

Had Simpson copied Canada's animal-products cells, Australia's food crop and pasture would be 0.75 ha, not 1.853, and 56% of it would be crop. Simpson's split is the reverse (5% crop). Read side by side, the two tables imply the book charges livestock land by the system that fed the animals: Canadian meat and milk carry large feed-grain land; Australian meat, mostly grass-fed beef and lamb, carries almost all its land as pasture. Strictly, a consumption count at world-average productivity should not depend on where the meat came from, so this is a convention the pages show in result but do not state. The size of Australia's pasture follows from three things: more ruminant meat a person (about 37 kg of beef and 21 kg of sheep meat in 1991-92, README) than a feedlot-and-pork diet; nearly all of it charged to grass; and a pasture rate the pages do not give. Wool is the same story at larger scale: Simpson's clothing pasture is 6.6 times Canada's, which a consumption count would need Australians to wear several times the wool Canadians do; V07 found no wool consumption figure on hand to test it.

## Recommendation

Decision 16 cannot be closed on the preview pages filed. They confirm that Simpson's conventions follow the book (land categories, pasture for dairy, meat and wool, wool in the clothing row, 100 GJ a hectare for direct energy, world-average productivities) and that his CO2-intensity route for goods and services is a stated departure. They do not supply the pasture productivity that decision 16 was opened to find, and they give no crop yield; X045 stays open. They add one useful constraint: the book's Canadian plant-food crop (0.18 ha, 0.20 with garden) sits with the two rebuilds, not with Simpson's 0.312, which supports reporting the plant-food figure as unreproduced. Before closing, capture pp. 83 to 90 if the preview shows them; then record decision 16 as closed with "no pasture productivity in the visible pages; pp. 63 to 75 and 92 to 110 hidden" and move the open figures to decision 17, the note to Simpson and Lowe, asking for the pasture rate and its source, whether dairy is in the 1.758, the grain scope of the 0.312, the wool consumption behind the 0.854, and whether the matrix figures came from the 1993 report. Separately, the project's capacity reading should either apply the p. 78 local-productivity adjustment or say plainly that it does not.
