## 1. Paragraphs 2.13 to 2.16 (exact)

**2.13, printed pp. 39 to 40 (sheets 53 to 54).** "However, there is a parallel recognition that any quantitative estimate of 'carrying capacity' can only be made subject to a wide range of assumptions about how to measure quality of life and how such measures respond to a variety of factors. Apart from population size, these factors include where that population lives, what technologies and industries it has and the consumption/activity patterns of its members. The complexity of these poorly-understood relationships means that even serious attempts at calculating 'carrying capacity' or optimum population can be little more than 'guesstimates'."

**2.14, p. 40 (sheet 54).** "There are many aspects of quality of life which stand to be affected by population growth. The following section however concentrates only on those associated with traditional concepts of 'carrying capacity', namely food and water supplies and the ability of natural systems (airsheds, waterbodies, ecosystems) to process the residues from domestic and industrial processes."

**2.15, p. 40.** "The 1991 National Population Council report estimated 50 million as a maximum 'carrying capacity' 'involving significant social, economic and environmental costs'. However there is reason to believe that the long-run supply and demand curves for most resources are fairly elastic." (footnote 2: Ridker 1973)

**2.16, p. 40.** "Reliable estimates accept that in subsistence terms Australia has the arable land to produce food for 50 plus million people. But, allowing a safety margin for the possibility of a run of bad seasons, we should regard 30-35 million people as the population 'carrying capacity' in terms of the food constraint. The concept of absolute constraints on population size imposed by the availability of certain natural resources is not particularly useful at least not over the range of population sizes being considered in this Inquiry. What does need to be recognised is that imposing extra population onto a fixed natural resource base will raise the marginal (opportunity) cost of satisfying certain basic population needs such as food and water. Self-sufficiency is of course not the only strategy for achieving food security. However it is also difficult to be confident that food could be imported for such numbers under terms of trade implying real food prices akin to today's food prices. This would depend on levels of non-food exports and the supply and demand for food on world markets in a much more populous but still orderly world."

Two points the quotations bring out. The 30-35 million is the committee's own sentence ("we should regard"), tied to the food constraint and a bad-seasons margin; it is not a figure the report attributes to a named source. And 2.16 goes on to call absolute constraints "not particularly useful" over the range considered, so the figure is offered and then discounted. Para 2.20 (p. 42) and 2.21 (p. 42) go further: the inquiry must use a concept "determined by human choice, not one that is determined by the physical limits of the environment"; "Australia's 'carrying capacity' is a function of choices, not environmental constraints".

## 2. The two clusters (McNicoll 1996, thesis p. 98)

**The report does not state them.** There is no paragraph in the report that gives "20 to 25 million" or "50 to 60 million" as two clusters, and no text search finds "20-25", "cluster", "ecologist" or "two camps" in the body. The words "two ecologies" in the title mean the populated coastal strip and the arid rest of the continent (para 8.3, p. 143), not two schools of opinion. McNicoll's "two main clusters" is the reviewer's reading. The nearest report text:

Attribution: the 50-60 end rests on Borrie and Caldwell, both demographers, as McNicoll says; the report names no economists as holders of it. The 20-25 end rests on one witness, Stone, at a single figure of 23, who is an anatomist, not an ecologist or demographer in the report's description (1.52, p. 16); the report does not say "ecologists" or "low-growth demographers" anywhere. Para 8.11 (iv) puts "The stable population option [Possible range 17-23m]" as having "strong community support" (p. 145); that is the report's own band, and it is 17 to 23, not 20 to 25.

**Does the report endorse either?** No. 8.10 (p. 144): "The Committee does not propose a target figure - or an optimum - for Australia's population by a particular date ... the search for a magic figure of a Rubicon between safety and danger is chimerical." 8.15 (p. 146) repeats it. 8.11 and 8.13 (pp. 144 to 146) give option bands instead: high 50-100+m (rejected as "illusory, unattainable and undesirable", 8.12, with option vi); then 30-50m; 23-30m; 17-23m; 5-17m. 8.14 (p. 146) puts the break point at 23 million.

## Verdicts on claims

**B006** ("100 million", "61 million", "30 to 35 million" and "9 to 14 million" are all called carrying capacity). The 30 to 35 part holds as a quotation: para 2.16, p. 40, uses the phrase "'carrying capacity' in terms of the food constraint". It is the committee's food-constraint figure with a bad-seasons margin, so the claim is right only if it is stated with that framing. The other three figures are not touched by this report. Status can move from "not reached" to "30 to 35 confirmed (B99)" for that element.

**B196** (Jones committee 1994: 30 to 35 million allowing a safety margin for a run of bad seasons). Holds. Para 2.16, p. 40: "allowing a safety margin for the possibility of a run of bad seasons, we should regard 30-35 million people as the population 'carrying capacity' in terms of the food constraint." Add "in terms of the food constraint" to the claim text. The thesis summary (20 to 25 against 50 to 60) is a different statement, and defect X120's finding that the thesis has no 30 to 35 stands but does not contradict the report.

**B197** (Jones committee 1994: food for 50 plus million in subsistence terms). Holds. Para 2.16, p. 40: "Reliable estimates accept that in subsistence terms Australia has the arable land to produce food for 50 plus million people." The note that the thesis's 50 to 60 is McNicoll's economists' cluster is right: the report's 50-60 is Borrie and Caldwell (1.61, 8.4) and Gifford and others (2.17), a separate statement from 2.16.
