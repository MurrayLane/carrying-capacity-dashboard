# Referee report, second round: "Australia's carrying capacity 2011 to 2025 by region on the corrected releases" (Paper A, results)

Submitted to Population and Environment. Referee standpoint: land-systems and agricultural science (Australian land use, crop and livestock production, drought, and the ABS and ABARES statistics behind them). I read the revised paper, the supplementary material, the reference list and the highlights. As instructed, I have ignored the drafter notes appended to the paper file and the bracketed flags. I have not seen the first-round reports, so this reads the revised manuscript as a fresh referee would.

## 1. Summary of the submission

The author brings their own land-based carrying capacity model, built for a 2014 thesis and published from 2014 to 2017, forward from 2011 to 2025 data. The method is held fixed apart from two named corrections (biodiesel yield per tonne of oilseed and beef carcase-to-retail yield). Capacity is the number of people a closed region could feed from its own cropland and pasture on a stated diet. The model is run under a short-term preset (current diet and production, an average year) and a long-term "100-year plan" preset (low meat, organic farming, no avoidable waste, much less liquid fuel, and the worst year in 100). National short-term capacity rises from 33.3 to 41.5 million while the population rises from 22.3 to 27.6 million, so the margin stays near 1.5. A Shapley-type average over all 362,880 orderings of nine data layers attributes the rise mainly to livestock output, the consumption layer and a lower red-meat share; land and livestock are coupled because livestock yields are output divided by grazing area. Of eight regional status flips, the author keeps two as clean and robust. Long-term capacity barely moves (15.35 to 15.6 million), so the share of the population a 100-year plan covers falls from 69% to 57%. A single cereal-derived weather factor applied to every yield puts the base margin above balance in a 1-in-10 year and below it in a 1-in-20 year, with both inside the Monte Carlo range. When every SA2 must feed itself, the sum of their capacities is 37% of the national figure on state yields, a loss the author attributes to the model's rule that cropland can stand in for pasture but not the reverse. A set of one-at-a-time measures (livestock output, diet, waste, grain stores, drought-proofing) closes the paper.

## 2. Overall assessment

The question is squarely within the journal's scope, and an honest update of a published Australian carrying capacity model, with its corrections stated and its regional results tested for artefacts, is a useful thing to have. The revision is careful in many places: the artefact, boundary and range tests on the flips, the averaging over orderings rather than a single walk, the frank crop-only bound on the drought factor, and the separation of state-yield and own-yield bases are all to the author's credit. My concerns come from the land and production side, and they are serious. The 2011 and 2025 inputs sit on opposite phases of the climate cycle and on different ABS collection frames, so much of the "change" may be weather and statistics rather than land-system change; the short-term capacity is, by construction, close to a ratio of current livestock output to domestic consumption, which the paper does not say plainly; and the headline drought results apply a cereal yield factor to pasture-fed output when the paper's own single-year evidence points the other way. My recommendation is **major revision**. None of these issues is fatal, and each can be addressed with analyses the author's pipeline appears able to run, but as written the paper claims more about change on the ground, and about drought exposure, than the evidence supports.

## 3. Major issues

**1. The two data years sit on opposite climate phases, so the 2011 to 2025 change is confounded with weather.** Section 2 and Table S11 show that the "2011" inputs are a 2005-06 to 2009-10 mean for land and livestock, and crop yields from 2006-07 to 2009-10. These are Millennium Drought years (2006-07 and 2007-08 in particular were among the worst winter-crop seasons on record). The "2025" inputs are grazing area from 2020-21, livestock output from 2020-21 to 2024-25 and crop yields from 2016-17 to 2024-25, a window dominated by the three La Niña years of 2020-21 to 2022-23 and the record winter crops in them. Section 4 says the crop layer "includes two very good winter-crop years, but it weighs little", and that the livestock window "ends in two years of heavy slaughter", but it never says that the 2011 baseline was a drought baseline. On a grazing-bound model, livestock output per hectare is exactly what responds to this. The livestock layer is the largest positive term in Table 2 (+5.5 million on the average), and the inland regions with the largest gains (Central West NSW, Lachlan, Namoi, the Border Rivers regions) are the ones where the climate phase differed most. Why it matters: Population and Environment readers will read "capacity rose by 8.2 million" as a change in what the land can do; it may largely be a change in the weather. What would resolve it: (a) state the climate phase of each input window in section 2 and in Table S11; (b) use the model's own weather machinery to put both years on a common footing, for example by dividing each window's crop and livestock output by the cereal (or a pasture) ratio of the seasons it covers, or by using matched-length windows that each span a full herd and rainfall cycle; (c) report how much of the livestock and crop layers survives that normalisation. The herd-cycle restriction in section 9.1 is the right instinct but is applied to 2025 only.

**2. The land layer mixes a real change with changes in the ABS collection frame.** The 2011 cropland and pasture are ABS agricultural holdings data from 2005-06 to 2009-10; the 2025 values are the 2020-21 Agricultural Census (Table S11). Between these, the ABS raised the estimated value of agricultural operations (EVAO) threshold for scope from $5,000 to $40,000 (from 2015-16) and moved to a Business Register frame. That removes many small holdings, which are concentrated in peri-urban and coastal regions. Table S11 mentions only that "the ABS changed its land-use categories in 2016" and that "part of the national pasture fall ... is a change of definition", with no estimate of the part. The consequences show up in the paper's own results: Hawkesbury-Nepean-Sydney's cropland halves from 389 to 191 km2 "on an unchanged boundary" (Supplementary Section S5), Swan's land layer takes capacity to 0.01 of its 2011 value, and three of the eight short-term flips are land-definition artefacts. The land layer is also the largest negative term in Table 2 (-3.1 million) and the source of the land areas carries 32% of short-term and 58% of long-term variance (Table S8). Why it matters: the decomposition's land row, and therefore the land-plus-livestock row the abstract leans on, cannot be read as land-use change while the frame changes underneath it. What would resolve it: name the frame changes (EVAO threshold, frame, category changes) explicitly; rerun the land layer on a consistent time series, for example the ABARES national land-use maps, which exist for both 2010-11 and 2020-21 on one classification, and report the decomposition on that basis alongside the holdings basis; and give the artefact test a sensitivity check, since Hawkesbury-Nepean-Sydney's 0.491 sits "just below" an arbitrary 0.5 bound.

**3. Short-term capacity is close to a ratio of current livestock output to domestic consumption, and the paper should say so and use it.** Livestock yields are computed as observed output divided by observed grazing area (Section 4, Supplementary Section S1, Table S11), there is one pasture type per zone, and pasture binds nationally with "no pasture left unused" (Section 6). Under these conditions the pasture-bound capacity is, by construction, roughly current pasture-fed output divided by per-person consumption of it. That is why livestock output +10% gives +9.7% (Table S5), why field-crop yields 24% lower give only -0.2%, and why land and livestock nearly cancel in Avon. Section 11.7 then reports that Australia produced 4.66 Mt of meat and ate 2.96 Mt in 2021-23, a ratio of 1.57, and says this "is a different measure from the model's capacity of 1.50 times the population, and the two are not compared directly". A land-systems reader will see the near-identity immediately. Why it matters: it changes what the headline means. The model is not discovering a land capacity independent of current output; in the short term it largely restates the meat and dairy self-sufficiency ratio at the current herd. What would resolve it: state this identity in section 6, compare the model's short-term margin with production-to-consumption ratios for beef, sheep meat and dairy from ABARES or FAOSTAT for both years, and explain any gap. If the two agree, that is a useful validation, and it should be presented as one.

**4. Grain-fed and pasture-fed livestock are not separated cleanly, which bears on the substitution rule and the drought result.** Table S10 classes "cattle meat" wholly as pasture-fed. A large share of Australian cattle slaughter is now grain-finished in feedlots, and that share rose markedly over the 2020s; Table S11 mentions a "cattle on feed" series, but the paper does not say how it is used. If feedlot-finished output sits in the numerator of the pasture yield, the pasture yield is inflated, and part of the livestock layer's rise reflects feedlot growth on cropland, not pasture. Separately, Section 6 says about 175,000 km2 of cropland "grazes animals" because pasture runs short. At what yield does cropland carry pasture-fed output: the pasture yield, or something reflecting grain or fodder production? In Australia's mixed wheat-sheep zone, much land recorded as grazing is arable and sits in crop-pasture rotation, so the strict rule "pasture cannot carry crops" overstates the loss in mixed-farming regions while fitting the rangelands. That rule drives the scale-curve result in Section 5 and the abstract's 37%. What would resolve it: state how feedlot output is treated and, if it is in the pasture numerator, give a sensitivity with it moved to the cropland account; state the yield at which cropland carries pasture-fed output; and test the scale curve with the arable share of grazing land in the mixed zone (for example from ABARES land capability or the census's sown-pasture items) allowed to carry crops.

**5. The headline drought results rest on a cereal factor applied to pasture-fed output, against the paper's own evidence.** The weather allowance (Section 2, Supplementary Section S4) is the ratio of the unweighted mean of wheat, oat and barley yields to a ten-observation moving mean, from 153 seasons since 1861, applied to every yield. Section 8.1 says about 97% of the 1-in-10 drought effect falls on pasture-fed food, and that the crop-only bound gives 41.2 million at 1 in 10 against 30.8 million. Supplementary Section S4 then reports that the drought years 2018-19 and 2019-20 gave single-year capacities near the median, with the low point in 2020-21 as the herd rebuilt. That is what a grazing scientist would expect: in a drought year, destocking raises slaughter output, and the loss arrives a year or two later as a smaller herd. A same-year cereal factor on meat output therefore gets both the timing and probably the size wrong. Further points: the 1-in-20 factor is set by the 1896 season and the 1-in-100 factor by interpolation towards 1915, from a record whose early decades reflect different technology and a wheat belt concentrated in South Australia and Victoria; an unweighted mean of three cereals is not area- or production-weighted; and regions take their state factor, while Supplementary Section S6 shows state factors far deeper than the national one (0.22 for Victoria against 0.46), so regional exposure is understated. Why it matters: the abstract, the highlights and the third conclusion all lead on "above balance in a 1-in-10 year and below it in a 1-in-20 year", and the failure probability of "about 5.8% a year, 1 in 17" is reported to two figures. What would resolve it: either build a pasture-side factor from a grazing record (for example AussieGRASS pasture growth, or livestock numbers and turn-off with an explicit lag) or present the crop-only and all-output results as bounds with equal weight in the abstract; test the cereal factor on post-1950 seasons only; and drop the precise failure probability or report it as a range across the interpolation rule, the factor and the record period.

**6. The 2011 to 2025 change is not tested against input uncertainty, though it could be.** Section 9.1 says the 2011 capacities "lie inside the 2025 ranges, and 2011 was not sampled", and calls the rise "a comparison of two years at fixed settings". The 2011 value of 33.3 million lies inside the 2025 short-term range of 31.1 to 49.2 million. Many of the 19 choices (energy requirement, loss factors, red-meat share, avoidable waste) would apply to both years alike, so the uncertainty of the difference is much smaller than that of either level. What would resolve it: run the same draws on 2011 where the choice applies to both years, and report the distribution of the paired difference. Until then the abstract should say that the rise holds at fixed settings, as section 9.1 does. The same applies to the 2011 against 2025 drought comparison in Table 6, which also mixes two different weather tables (0.599 against 0.634 at 1 in 20, Section 2): part of the change from 1.12 to 1.05 people over capacity at 1 in 20 is the table, not the data.

**7. The regions are 2011 NRM boundaries, which weakens the planning relevance claimed in Section 1.4.** The 53 regions are the NRM regions of 2011. Several states have since redrawn them: New South Wales replaced its catchment management authorities with Local Land Services regions in 2014, and South Australia moved to landscape regions in 2020. Section 12 notes that "five zones carry older 2011 boundaries", and Swan is the clearest case, but readers in planning will want to know which present-day bodies the results speak to. What would resolve it: say plainly that the units are a fixed analytical partition, not current administrative regions, and, if the claim to inform a National Food Plan is kept, map the main results to current state planning regions or to SA4s, which the paper already computes and which behave better on the scale curve.

**8. The diet layer rests on one FAO series change that needs corroboration.** The consumption and red-meat-mix layers together contribute 5.6 million (68% of the short-term change), driven partly by FAO beef and veal supply falling from 39.1 to 25.9 kg per person (Section 4). FAO food supply is availability at carcase weight, not intake, and 2021-23 coincided with record cattle prices during herd rebuilding, which depressed domestic beef purchases. A one-third fall in beef availability is large enough to need a second source. What would resolve it: cross-check the change against ABARES or industry domestic consumption series for the same years, say whether the 2021-23 level is a cyclical low, and give the capacity on a diet window that spans a full cattle price cycle.

**9. Missing literature that this field would expect.** The paper engages well with food-security policy but thinly with land-system and agricultural science. I would expect: the Australian land-use futures and LUTO work (Bryan and colleagues) on land-use change and its drivers; ABARES climate-adjusted productivity and the work on climate effects on farm performance beyond Hughes and Lawson (2017); rangeland and pasture-degradation literature on drought and grazing (for example McKeon and colleagues); work on crop-livestock mixed farming in southern Australia; Peters and colleagues (2016) on the carrying capacity of US agricultural land under ten diets, which turns on exactly the cultivated against grazing-only land constraint of Section 6; and the global foodshed and local self-sufficiency literature beyond Kinnunen et al. (2020), such as Pradhan and colleagues and Fader and colleagues on spatial decoupling of production and consumption. The Kitagawa or Shapley lineage of the all-orderings decomposition should also be cited.

**10. The long-term preset is mainly an energy and weather scenario, and should be framed as such.** Table 5 shows the long-term figure moves from 15.6 to 34.3 million without the weather allowance and to 3.7 million with short-term fuel use. The author says so ("mainly a statement about planning for bad years"), but the abstract and highlights call it "a 100-year self-sufficiency plan" covering 57% of the population, as if it were a land-system finding. Organic farming at 100% and a 30% reserve are also strong assumptions. What would resolve it: describe it in the abstract and highlights as one scenario of the author's settings, name the two settings that dominate it, and give the share covered at a less extreme return period (for example 1 in 20) beside it.

## 4. Minor issues

1. Precision. Capacities are printed to the person (33,307,814; 41,503,537), scenario effects to two decimals (+48.45%, +64.47%, -8.15%) and a failure probability to two figures. Round to what the inputs support, two or three significant figures, in the main text.
2. The subsections of Section 1 run 1.1, 1.4, 1.5 and 1.3 in that order, and there is no 1.2.
3. Section 10 says "Five regions were chosen, one of each type, from the largest movers", but Table 7 has seven regions, Supplementary Section S5 says "Four regions in depth", and the Murray is not among the largest movers.
4. "Local basis" (Table 7), "own yields" and "each zone's own yields" are used for the same thing; pick one.
5. The parenthesis "(52 NRM regions and the Northern Territory)" follows "53 regions" more than a dozen times; define it once.
6. Release codes (`2011-fixed-3`, `2025-mirror`, `2025-store-2`) and file paths run through the prose and captions. Keep them in a supplementary key and data statement; the main text should say "corrected method, 2025 data".
7. Areas are given in km2 in most places and in hectares in Supplementary Section S1 (1,594,996 ha); yields are in g/m2. Use one area unit and say why g/m2.
8. Supplementary Section S4 calls the single-year figures "unverified (the second route is blocked)", and the supplement opens with "decision 92". Remove workflow language; if a figure is unverified it should not be in a submission.
9. Section 2 gives the national population row as 22,326,388 while the regions sum to 22,336,514. Say which is used for the national ratios in Tables 2, 4 and 6.
10. Section 6: "cropland binds only at a share well below the 10% at which the national test starts" sits uneasily with Lane (2014, p. 3), quoted as finding cropping share the most significant determinant. One sentence reconciling the two would help.
11. Section 8.4 cites Anderson et al. (2019) for "30 to 41% of wheat variability in Australia and East Africa". Give the Australian figure on its own if available.
12. Section 11.6 calls the 15.5 million gap "people-equivalents" that trade "recovers". Since transport losses, costs and storage are absent, call it the pooling gain of the closed model.
13. Supplementary Section S2's random groupings rest on five draws; say this in the main text where 68 to 81% is compared with 33%, or run more.
14. Table 6's footnote mentions "a rounding edge (81.55% and 91.48%)"; this is more detail than a reader needs, and it uses a different precision from the cells.
15. Reference list: Bolleter (2018) carries a 2019 DOI; check the year. Gifford et al. (1975) is cited second-hand; read it directly if possible, since Section 11 relies on its "1975 CSIRO case".

## 5. Questions to the author

1. How is the cattle on feed series used: is feedlot-finished output removed from the pasture yield numerator, and if so, where is it credited?
2. When cropland carries pasture-fed products (the 175,000 km2 in Section 6), at what yield per square metre is it credited?
3. How much of the national pasture fall from 3,639,794 to 3,318,689 km2 do you attribute to the EVAO threshold and frame changes, and how much to real change (pastoral lease surrender, conservation and Indigenous land acquisitions)?
4. What is the decomposition of Table 2 if both data years use input windows of equal length that each span a full rainfall and herd cycle?
5. How does the 1-in-10 and 1-in-20 factor change if the cereal record starts in 1950 or 1970, and if the three cereals are weighted by area?
6. Is there a reason not to build a pasture or livestock weather factor from a grazing record, given that Supplementary Section S4 shows drought years near the median and the trough a year later?
7. Do the regional flips survive if the artefact bounds are 0.4 and 2.5 rather than 0.5 and 2?
8. What does the paired 2011 against 2025 difference look like under the shared Monte Carlo choices?
9. Has the irrigated share (0.007 of cropland at base) been checked against the ABS water use on farms series? It looks low for a national figure that includes irrigated pasture and horticulture, and it controls the water discussion in Section 12.3.

## 6. Abstract and highlights

The abstract is long but mostly accurate to the results, and it now names the cereal basis of the weather allowance and the substitution rule behind the 37%. It claims more than the evidence in three places. First, "National short-term capacity rose from 33.3 to 41.5 million" should say that this is a comparison at fixed settings, that the input windows differ in climate phase, and that the 2011 value lies inside the 2025 range. Second, "the margin is above balance in a 1-in-10 year and below it in a 1-in-20 year at the base settings" leads with an all-output cereal factor that the paper itself says is untested for pasture, and that its single-year evidence contradicts; the crop-only bound (41.2 million at 1 in 10) should stand beside it, and the base is not central (37% of draws fall below the population at 1 in 10). Third, "A 100-year self-sufficiency plan would cover 57%" should say this is one scenario dominated by the weather and fuel settings.

The highlights compress these further. "Livestock, consumption, red meat mix account for the rise; land and livestock overlap" does not tell a reader that the livestock term is coupled to a grazing-area denominator. "Base capacity is above population at 1 in 10, below at 1 in 20; ranges include it" omits that the factor is cereal-derived and applied to pasture-fed output. "A 100-year self-sufficiency plan would cover 57%" omits the scenario's assumptions. The fifth highlight is accurate. The keywords are suitable; "livestock" and "scale" would help discovery.

## 7. Five points I would most want answered in the response letter

1. Normalise or at least quantify the climate-phase difference between the 2005-10 and 2020-25 input windows, and report how much of the livestock and crop layers survives (Major issue 1).
2. Separate the ABS frame and classification changes from real land-use change, ideally by rerunning the land layer on the ABARES national land-use maps for 2010-11 and 2020-21 (Major issue 2).
3. State and test the identity between short-term capacity and the current livestock production-to-consumption ratio, and say how feedlot output and cropland-as-pasture are credited (Major issues 3 and 4).
4. Either build a pasture-side weather factor or give the crop-only bound equal weight in the abstract, highlights and conclusions, and drop or range the 5.8% failure probability (Major issue 5).
5. Report the paired 2011 against 2025 difference under shared Monte Carlo draws, so the claim that capacity rose can be stated with its uncertainty (Major issue 6).
