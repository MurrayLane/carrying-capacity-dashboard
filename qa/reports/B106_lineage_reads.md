## L244 Wackernagel et al. 1999

Method in two lines. Each nation's consumption is built from UN data in biophysical terms (apparent consumption: production plus imports minus exports, p. 380) and divided by world-average yields to give hectares of world-average productivity; land types are scaled by equivalence factors and national land by yield factors, then footprint is set against bio-capacity per person (pp. 380, 383). Italy is the worked example (Tables 1 to 3); 52 nations are tabulated (Table 4).

What the authors say the comparison shows: humanity as a whole has a footprint larger than the world's carrying capacity (p. 385); the overshoot is possible only while natural capital stocks are depleted (p. 385); a country with a remainder often uses it to make export goods rather than keep it (Figure 1 caption, p. 385). Stated limits: UN data may be incomplete and inconsistent (p. 387); freshwater, contaminants and waste not counted (p. 387); apparent consumption differs from true household consumption (p. 380); sea yield factor set to 1 for every country (p. 383); yield factors overstate the potential of industrialised agriculture (p. 383, note 5); 12% is probably too small for biodiversity (p. 384).

Australian carrying capacity in people: not given. Australia appears only as one row of Table 4; no person count is derived for any nation.

Cautions on reading the row: the unit is hectares of world-average productivity, not Australian hectares; the 12% deduction is printed for Italy and stated as the rule on p. 384, and its use on every Table 4 row is implied, not printed.

What moves the estimates (pp. 200 to 203): the method (spatial extrapolation and multi-region modelling give higher values; actual supply of a resource and dynamic systems modelling give the lowest, Table 3); the limiting factor (nonrenewable resources, forest products, water and synergy of factors most restrictive; carbon and heat removal highest, Table 4); technology (positive, significant in model 1, coefficient 1.08); the diet assumption (spatially homogeneous diet, coefficient 1.46 in model 2); land availability (1.52) and energy intake per person (minus 1.08); and the actual population at the time of the study (coefficient 0.60 and 0.85), which they call an implicit frame of reference (pp. 203 to 204).

Australian figure: none. Box 1 lists two Australian-titled works (Knibbs 1917, Wickens 1925; pp. 198, 199; also p. 196 and p. 204) but no Australian number is printed.

On a single number: the authors give conditional meta-estimates, not one answer. They write "assuming it exists" of a limit (p. 201), that results depend strongly on the limiting factor (p. 202), that a limit rises with population while population grows so there is no absolute limit (p. 201), that no estimate is a guarantee of sustainability (p. 203), that virtually all primary studies are partial and incomplete (p. 204), and conclude that uncertainty about population limits remains large (p. 204). They do not say a single number is useless.
