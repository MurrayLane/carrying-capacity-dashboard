# B67b readings: urban agriculture, water, energy, synchronous failures

Five rows, all read in full. Each file was opened and matched to the row's title, authors and year before reading. Page numbers are the PDFs' own; the Anderson file is a PubMed Central text with no pagination, so its figures are cited by section. Rows are in `qa/reports/B67b_readings.csv`.

**L183, Mcdougall, Rader and Kristiansen 2020 (Sydney urban agriculture).** The paper takes one year of harvest, labour and input logbooks from 13 Sydney gardens (5.94 kg and 8515 kJ per m2, p. 5). It measures spare land in 20 SA1 blocks from aerial imagery and scales up to 6032 blocks. Using verges, spare land and 25% of yards would give 15.0% of the food energy of the 2.67 million people in those blocks; 50% of yards gives 24.4% and 75% gives 33.8% (Table 4, p. 8). In every amateur scenario the gardening labour needed is more than the population (p. 9). The characterisation partly holds. The 15% is food energy for the modelled blocks, not for all of Sydney.

**L186, Clinton et al. 2018 (global urban agriculture).** The paper maps vacant land, suitable roofs and facades in 2010 urban areas. It applies each country's FAO 2010 yields for crops suited to cities and finds 98 to 178 million tonnes a year (Table 2, p. 49). The two ends of that range come from two urban masks. They are not low and high adoption cases. The tonnage is 5 to 10% of world pulses, roots, tubers and vegetables (p. 51), and about 5% of those crops at most (p. 53). The characterisation holds. In two places the running text and the tables give different numbers, area (p. 47) and food value (p. 51); the table values are the ones taken.

**L190, Rosa et al. 2020 (economic water scarcity).** The paper runs a monthly crop water model over cropland as of 2000, using 1996-2005 climate, with environmental flows protected. It finds that 15% of cropland is rain-fed and has enough renewable water to irrigate. Irrigating that land would feed 620 million more people. With 50% deficit irrigation the area rises to 25% and the people fed to 840 million (pp. 3, 7). Global sustainable irrigation use is 810 km3 a year, against 1083 km3 consumed (p. 5). The Murray-Darling Basin is listed as short of blue water for several months of the year (p. 3). The paper does not model year-to-year swings in water (p. 8). The characterisation holds.

**L192, Ladha-Sabur et al. 2019 (energy in food manufacturing).** This is a review of 44 sources, 1980-2015. It gives specific energy per kilogram of product as final energy, with packaging, retail and consumers left out (p. 271). Globally, the food sector uses about 200 EJ a year, 45% of it in processing and distribution (p. 270). UK food processing used 117 PJ in 2017, 58% of it gas and 34% electricity (pp. 270, 276). Over 98% of UK food moves by road (p. 275, quoting Jones 2002). The powders and fried goods are the most energy-intensive products (p. 277). The characterisation holds. The paper has no farm-fuel or Australian figures.

**L218, Anderson et al. 2019 (synchronous crop failures).** The paper relates 1980-2010 crop statistics to ocean temperature patterns using maximum covariance analysis. Climate modes explain 18, 7 and 6% of global maize, soybean and wheat production variance, and 23, 15 and 17% of local variance (Global analysis). In Australia and East Africa, ENSO and the IOD together explain 30 to 41% of wheat variability. El Niño and positive IOD states favour drought across much of Australia (Regional analyses). Only ENSO forces globally synchronous failures, as it did for maize in 1983. The characterisation holds. The 18, 7 and 6% figures are for the globally aggregated series.

## Flags

1. L183: Results paper section 12 (forced local self-sufficiency: gardens) and `papers/A_results/DISCUSSION_MEASURES.md` D9. If Sydney urban agriculture is cited, the 15% is food energy for the 2.67 million residents of the modelled blocks, not Greater Sydney's 4.9 million (p. 5). In every amateur scenario the labour needed is more than the population (p. 9). The authors rule out the 75% scenario and may rule out the 50% one (p. 10).
2. L192: Results paper sections 7 and 8 (liquid fuel settings), DISCUSSION_MEASURES D7 (farm diesel), and methods paper section 8 (fossil energy). The paper covers post-farm manufacturing and distribution energy only. It has no farm-fuel figure, no liquid fuel per tonne-km in the main text (Appendix I is not on file) and no Australian figure. It can support a statement about post-farm energy, not the model's fuel settings.
3. L218: Results paper section 11 and DISCUSSION_MEASURES C and D5 (regions as a pool; "the states do not fail together"). The paper finds El Niño and positive IOD states drive poor wheat years across much of Australia at the same time. It names the mechanism that ties southern states' bad years together and can be cited beside the state worst-year factors. It does not change the measured figures. Methods paper section 8 (variability between years) is supported as written.
4. L186 and L190: none. Rosa (p. 4: little economically water-scarce land in high-income and arid regions; p. 3: Murray-Darling under blue water scarcity) is consistent with results paper section 11's 0 to 2% from irrigation.

## Wrong-value check

Example only, not a finding. Suppose L190 had said: "Economic water scarcity affects 25% of cropland under full irrigation, and irrigating it would feed 620 million more people." That would NOT hold. On p. 3, full irrigation gives 15% of cropland (0.14 billion ha) and 620 million people. The 25% figure is reached only with 50% deficit irrigation, and in that case the people fed are 840 million, not 620 million. A reader checking p. 3 would mark it "no".
