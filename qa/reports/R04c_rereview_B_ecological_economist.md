# Referee report, second round: Paper B, "Well-known carrying-capacity methods tested on common Australian data"

Submitted to Ecological Economics. Referee B (ecological economics; ecological footprint and carrying capacity debate).

I read the revised paper, the supplementary material, the reference list and the highlights. I have not seen the first-round reports and judge the revision as a fresh referee. As instructed, I ignore the drafter notes appended to the paper and the bracketed flags.

## 1. Summary of the submission

Published figures for how many people Australia can feed run from about 9 to 100 million. The author asks how much of that spread comes from inputs (data year, land base, consumption basis), how much from definitions (exports, weather, diet, unit, time frame), and how much from the method itself, and which method suits which purpose. Five published methods (Henry and Godden's energy and protein balance, a food-row ecological footprint after Simpson, Petroeschevsky and Lowe, the HALF land-per-diet index, Cassidy's people per hectare, and a capacity reading derived from Foran and Poldy's commodity balance) are rebuilt, checked against their authors' printed figures, and run on FAOSTAT data for 2021 to 2023. The author's own land-and-diet model enters in three releases and is also read through Fearnside's failure-probability frame. The author then harmonises inputs under two classifications of the productivity basis: if world-average hectares are part of the method (line a), equalising inputs closes 4.1% of the log distance to the model; if they are an input (line b), it closes 38.5%. At Australia's own productivity, the footprint and HALF read 1.6 to 2.8 times the model, and relaxing the model's pairing of pasture-fed food with crops raises it from 41.5 to 81.7 million. Section 6 uses the model to measure the factor each design choice moves the answer, and a 19-setting Monte Carlo gives a 1.58-fold spread. Section 7 rates seven method columns against four purposes, concluding that no method serves every purpose, and proposes a twelve-item reporting checklist.

## 2. Overall assessment

The question is a good one for this journal, and the paper has real strengths. The fidelity checks against each method's own published figures are unusually careful and honestly graded, including where implementations fail. The two-line treatment of the productivity basis is the most useful idea in the paper: it shows that much of the apparent disagreement between footprint-type readings and land-and-diet balances is a choice of accounting unit, which footprint critics (and defenders) will recognise. The reporting checklist is practical and would improve the Australian debate.

The revision still has problems that matter to an ecological economics readership. The most important are that the footprint is tested in a form that is neither its original 1990s food row (several components are not reproduced) nor the current National Footprint and Biocapacity Accounts, so readings in "world-average hectares" are attributed to the footprint when they mix actual and global hectares; that the author's model is at once a method tested, the reference, the instrument of section 6, two of seven columns in the suitability matrix and the only column rated well suited for three of four purposes, rated by the author with an AI session as second rater; and that the headline numbers depend on choices of reading, range and reference that are not reported with any uncertainty. These are fixable but not small. **Recommendation: major revision.**

## 3. Major issues

**M1. The footprint reading in "world-average hectares" is a units mismatch, not the footprint's reading, and the paper should stop attributing it to the method (sections 4.5, 5.4, 5.10, 5.11; Tables 3, 5, 6, S8; abstract).**

Section 4.5 states correctly that Wackernagel et al. (1999) put footprint and bio-capacity "both in hectares of world-average productivity, not of the nation's own land" (pp. 377, 380, 386). Yet section 5.4's secondary reading divides "Australia's land" in actual hectares by "the land a person needs" in world-average hectares, giving 146 to 343 million. Under the footprint's own accounting, that ratio is not defined; the method converts the nation's land into global hectares through yield factors (and, when land types are pooled, equivalence factors) before any comparison. What the paper calls line (b), "the adjustment Wackernagel and Rees (1996, p. 78) describe", is therefore not an alternative classification but the footprint as its authors intend it. Line (a) is then not "the productivity basis counted as part of the method"; it is a reading the method does not make.

This matters because the abstract and section 7.6 still lead with "in world-average hectares, 134 to 343 million, about three to eight times", and the 4.1% figure on line (a) is presented as one of two equally valid findings. A footprint specialist reading this will regard the line (a) footprint readings as an implementation choice that the paper then charges to the method. The same applies to HALF, whose authors state the index is "not predictive" (Alexander et al. 2016, p. 89); the paper notes "read in the footprint way, which HALF is not", but the abstract does not.

To resolve: (i) present the own-productivity reading as the footprint's reading and the world-average-hectare reading as a sensitivity showing what happens when the yield factor is omitted; (ii) say so in the abstract and highlights; (iii) reconsider whether the two-line framing of section 5.11 and 9.1 should remain symmetrical, or whether line (b) is the faithful comparison and line (a) a counterfactual; (iv) state how "crop and pasture together" is aggregated. If cropland and pasture hectares are summed without equivalence factors, the pooled reading is not a footprint reading either; if they are pooled in output-equivalent hectares, say which weights apply.

**M2. The footprint tested is neither Simpson's food row as reproduced nor the current national accounts (sections 4.5, 5.4, 8.6; Tables 3, S6; supplementary S2.5, S3.2).**

The fidelity check is commendably frank: plant-food cropland is 66 to 76% low, the crop-plus-pasture total 10 to 12% low, garden land 57% low, feed cropland 21.6% high on the thesis rates, and the three builds disagree on pasture by up to 36%. The footprint is still carried into the headline comparison and the purpose matrix. Meanwhile the obvious benchmark, the National Footprint and Biocapacity Accounts (Lin et al. 2018 is cited in S2.5 only), publishes for Australia a per-person food footprint by land type and a biocapacity in global hectares with Australia-specific yield factors. A reading from published national accounts needs no rebuild and has no fidelity problem.

To resolve: add a reading for Australia from the published national accounts (biocapacity of cropland and grazing land over the food footprint per person, by land type and pooled), compare it with builds 1 and 3, and use it as the footprint's primary entry, with the Simpson rebuild as the lineage check. If the author prefers not to, the paper should explain why a 1991-92 Australian food row is the right representative of a method whose current form is maintained annually.

**M3. The own-productivity readings are, algebraically, land-weighted production-to-consumption ratios, which links them to the export-inclusive counts; this should be shown, not left as "consistent with" (sections 5.11, 5.14, 7.6, 9.1).**

On line (b), Australia's land is expressed as the world-average hectares that would grow its output. The capacity reading is then (sum over products of Australian output times world hectares per tonne) over (sum over products of consumption per person times world hectares per tonne). That is the population times a land-weighted ratio of production to consumption: an export-inclusive count with world land intensities as weights, rather than the proportional shares of the CSIRO and University of Queensland count. The 64.8 to 117 million footprint readings (about 2.5 to 4.5 times FAO's 26.2 million) are then largely a statement about Australia's export surplus, and the remaining gap to the model is the difference between a pooled surplus and a binding surplus of the scarcest product.

This sharpens section 5.14 rather than weakening it. The pooled reading allows surplus grain to stand in for missing beef at world exchange rates; the model does not. The paper already reports, only in Table 5 column (e), a "pasture alone" footprint reading of 53.9 to 77.9 million. That reading is the footprint with land types held as separate constraints, the footprint analogue of the pairing, and its low end sits beside the improved model's 53.6. It is excluded from the footprint's headline range of 64.8 to 117.0 without explanation.

To resolve: state the algebra in section 5.11; report the binding-land-type (minimum) reading as the footprint's paired reading beside the pooled one; then say how much of the remaining gap is the pairing and how much is diet, yields and per-person requirement. That would let the paper replace "consistent with a feature that the model carries" (section 5.14) with a decomposition. It would also clarify how the trade question of section 8.2 and Cohen's (1995, p. 343) point bear on every own-productivity reading, not only on the export counts.

**M4. The author's model holds too many roles for the comparison and the rating to be read as independent (sections 1, 5.11, 6, 7.1, 9.5; Tables 6, 9, S9).**

The paper discloses this in sections 1, 7.1 and 9.5, and the disclosure is welcome. Disclosure does not fix the design, however. The model is the reference in Table 6 ("distance to the corrected model"), the instrument for every factor in section 6, two of the seven columns of Table 9, and the only column rated well suited for purposes 2 and 3, and well suited for all four purposes under one of its readings. The ratings were made by the author, and the "second blind rating" was made by "a blind checker session of an AI model, not a person", which is not independent in the sense this journal would accept for an inter-rater check; it also saw a rubric (section S7) whose purpose definitions are written in the model's own terms, for example purpose 1 as "whether a diet that has to balance can be supplied from domestic land". A method built on a diet that has to balance from domestic land will satisfy that definition by construction.

To resolve: (i) lead the harmonised comparison with the reference-free measures (the standard deviation of log centres and the highest-over-lowest ratio), which the paper already computes, and report distance to the model second; (ii) either remove the model from Table 9 and discuss it separately, or obtain ratings from two or more people outside the project, with the purpose definitions written before the ratings and without reference to any method's design; (iii) count the model once, not twice, in the matrix, since the failure frame is the model with a return period (section 5.8 and Table 3 say as much).

**M5. The headline shares rest on choices of readings, centres and weighting that are not tested (section 5.11; Tables 5, 6; S3.11).**

The 4.1% and 38.5% figures are the mean absolute log distance of five method centres, equally weighted, from one point estimate of the model. Several choices sit behind them: which readings form each method's set (19 in all; footprint build 2 excluded; the commodity balance's fish reading of 12 to 17 million included, though section 5.10 calls it "the single-commodity fish reading"); how a method's centre is formed from its readings; equal weighting; and the model's 41.5 rather than its 31.1 to 49.2 range or the improved 53.6. Section S3.11 shows the commodity balance moves -15% on both lines because its fish reading goes from 17 to 12 million, so a single fisheries ratio shifts the headline. With five methods and no interval, the reader cannot tell whether 4.1% differs from zero or 38.5% from 25% or 50%.

To resolve: report the shares with a simple robustness table (leave one method out; drop the fish reading; reference at 31.1, 41.5, 49.2 and 53.6; geometric mean versus median centres), and present the headline as a range if it moves materially. The sentence "Which line holds is a classification, not a finding" is right and should travel with the numbers into the abstract.

**M6. Harmonisation leaves the per-person requirement and waste basis unequalised, and these are as large as the productivity basis (sections 5.1, 5.11, 5.13; Table S5; S3.1).**

"Harmonised" sets every method to FAO's supply of 3,525 kcal (14.8 MJ) a person a day, which includes retail and household waste, while the model uses 8.0 MJ as eaten plus its own waste charge. Henry and Godden's factor implies 15.6 MJ; at 8.8 MJ the same production feeds 243 million (S3.1). The energy requirement is also the second-largest source of variance in the model (27%, section 6.9). So the per-person basis is a third line between input and method of the same order as the productivity basis, and the intake variant of section 5.13 handles it only from one side (it raises the methods; the model stays at 41.5).

To resolve: add a third harmonisation in which every method and the model share one per-person requirement and one waste treatment (for example, the model's intake plus a common waste rate), and report the shares closed on that basis. If that is not possible for some methods, say which and why.

**M7. Section 6 mixes one-at-a-time changes in the model with ratios between different methods, and then orders them as if they were alike (sections 6.1, 6.10, 6.11; Table 8; Table S4; highlights).**

The opening of section 6 says each design choice was measured "with the author's model on the same 2025 data". The three export rows are not: 2.41 is CSIRO and the University of Queensland's 100 million over the model's 41.5, and 4.43 and 6.36 are an energy count of exports alone over the model. None is the model run with exports counted. Likewise the optimised-diet factor (7.88) compares a diet within the model, but "Australian settings" (1.29) is a bundle of several changes. Placing these in one ordered list, and carrying them into the checklist (Table S4) as "factor measured in section 6", invites a reader to compare quantities that are not comparable. The ladder of section 6.10 is described by the author as "a reading of the spread, not a decomposition", since "the product holds for any steps chosen"; I agree, and on that basis I do not see what it adds to the argument.

The fifth highlight and the abstract say that "weather and waste take the model from 18.9 to 55.3 million". These are two separate one-at-a-time changes in opposite directions from 41.5, not a joint range, and the sentence will be read as a range of the model.

To resolve: either run the model with exports counted (for example, capacity with export production treated as available to the domestic diet), or move the export rows out of the ordered table into a separate comparison with other methods; mark bundled rows as bundles; drop or demote the ladder; and rewrite the highlight and abstract sentence to state the two changes separately.

**M8. The common data window and the model's data layer need a stronger defence (sections 5.1, 5.11; S3, S3.8; abstract).**

The abstract says the model runs "on its own 2025 layer", taken as "the same vintage as FAOSTAT's data for 2021 to 2023" (S3), with a meat window of 2020-21 to 2024-25. So the methods and the model do not in fact share one data set for production and yields; they share land totals and, for the methods, the FAO food balance. In addition, as far as I know 2021 to 2023 included unusually large winter crop harvests in Australia. Every cropland-bound and own-productivity reading (the latter scales with output) will be raised in such years, while the model applies trend windows. Since section 7.2 rates methods on food security and drought risk, the reader needs to know whether the comparison was run on an average or a favourable window.

To resolve: state how 2021 to 2023 compares with a longer average for Australian crop and livestock output, and rerun the own-productivity readings and the energy balance on a longer window (for example ten years) as a sensitivity. Also explain why the land-use source is the largest single driver of the model's variance (32% short term, 58% long term, Table S3) when the two land bases "agree to 0.01 Mha" (S3.8); presumably the raster and the holdings distribute land differently across zones or states, but the reader is not told, and this bears directly on whether the model's range reflects uncertainty in land or a modelling artefact.

**M9. Missing literature that this journal's readers will expect (sections 2, 4.5, 8.2; S1, S2.5).**

The footprint debate in Ecological Economics is not represented beyond Lenzen and Murray (2001), Giampietro and Saltelli (2014), Goldfinger et al. (2014) and Blomqvist et al. (2013). At minimum I would expect: van den Bergh and Verbruggen (1999) on the footprint's spatial and aggregation assumptions; Fiala (2008) on why the footprint is a poor measure of sustainability, including its treatment of productivity; Kitzes et al. (2009) on the research agenda for national footprint accounts; Monfreda, Wackernagel and Deumling (2004) or Galli et al. (2007) for yield and equivalence factors, which bear directly on M1; and Borucke et al. (2013) for the national accounts method. On carrying capacity in economics, Arrow et al. (1995) and Daily and Ehrlich (1992) are the natural anchors for section 2. On trade and land, trade-adjusted land footprints (for example the work of Kastner and colleagues on land embodied in trade) would support section 8.2 and M3. The van den Bergh and Rietveld (2004) meta-analysis is cited, which is good; its finding that estimates move with method and assumptions is close to this paper's, and the relation should be made explicit in the introduction rather than left to section 2.

## 4. Minor issues

1. The abstract says "the aggregate methods give about 65 to 211 million ... in world-average hectares, 134 to 343 million". Table 3 gives the energy and protein balance no reading in world-average hectares ("none: counts Australia's own production"), yet 134 is its low end. The world-average range should start at 146 (footprint) or the energy balance should be excluded explicitly. Table S8's "aggregate methods, secondary, 133.8 to 343.4" has the same problem.
2. The top of the primary range, 211 million, is the energy balance scaled to Nix's 70 Mha over the 1990-91 in-use land, with 1990-91 sown pasture carried forward (S3.1). A 1990-91 scale-up should not set the top of a 2021-23 range without a note in the abstract.
3. Highlight 1 and the opening sentence present 9 to 100 million as the span, but the 9.4 is a long-term bundle (low-meat diet, organic yields, 100-year drought allowance, 30% reserve) and the 100 includes exports. Section 1 says both ends are "soft"; the highlight should not lead with the span unqualified.
4. Table 1 gives Foran and Poldy "30 to 32 (meat) and 76 to 80"; Table 3's fidelity cell says "the 30 and 77 readings are derived here"; section 5.7 gives 40 and 41 million on 2021-23 data. Clarify which are the 2050-requirement readings on 1997-98 production and which on current production.
5. People per hectare appears as "145 to 147", "145 to 190", "about 190", "about 183" and "110.9 to 112.9" across sections 5.6, 5.11 and Tables 3 and 5. One table that lists each reading with its land base and pairing would remove the confusion.
6. Table notes contain production details that a reader cannot use and that weaken confidence: "`cross_method_local.csv` still prints the earlier 13-setting improved range and needs regenerating" (Table 3), the design-choices csv that "needs regenerating" (Table 8), and the csv cells that disagree with the table in Table S5. Regenerate the files and remove the notes before resubmission.
7. Table 2 and Figures 1 to 3 are placeholders. Section 4 relies on Table 2, and section 6 on Figure 3. The paper cannot be fully judged without them.
8. Table 4's mirror row keeps a 13-setting, 1,000-draw range while the other rows have 19 or 18 settings and 2,000 draws; the rows are not comparable and Table 3 says the thesis method as built "reproduces the thesis figures exactly". Rerun the mirror on the same design or drop its range.
9. Table 5's column (e) header cites "(p. 78)" with no author; write "Wackernagel and Rees 1996, p. 78".
10. Section 5.12 converts HALF points into people on Australia's 2011 land (396.1 Mha), while every other reading uses 363.5 Mha. State why, or use one base.
11. Section 6 opens in the past tense ("This section measured") while the rest of the paper is in the present.
12. Section 9.5 duplicates the declaration of competing interests; one is enough.
13. The declaration of generative AI use contains bracketed placeholders for model names, and the CRediT statement has "[Author name]". The reference list carries "[CHECK...]" notes for Wackernagel (1994) and Wackernagel and Rees (1996).
14. Section 9.4 says the archive has no DOI. A frozen, citable deposit should be in place before acceptance, given that the reproducibility claim is central.
15. The abstract is at 249 words and very dense with numbers; it would read better with fewer figures and one clear sentence of finding.
16. Section 3 states eligibility rules but not how many works each route returned or how many were kept; a sentence of counts would satisfy the reporting standard the paper itself cites (Haddaway et al. 2020).
17. The improved release's model default diet in Table S2 has 0.30 ha of cropland and 5.93 ha of pasture a person; it would help to say how this compares with Simpson's 1.758 ha and the national accounts' grazing footprint per person, since the pairing argument turns on it.
18. "No single method serves every purpose" (abstract, 7.6) is close to true by construction when the purposes are chosen to differ in what they need. Consider stating the finding as the specific mapping, not the negative.

## 5. Questions to the author

1. How are cropland and pasture combined in the "crop and pasture together" footprint readings: raw hectares, output-equivalent hectares, or with equivalence factors?
2. What does the footprint read for Australia if taken directly from the latest National Footprint and Biocapacity Accounts, by land type and pooled?
3. Why is the footprint's "pasture alone" reading at own productivity (53.9 to 77.9 million) left out of its headline range, and would you accept it as the footprint's paired reading?
4. How do the shares closed (4.1%, 38.5%) change if the fish reading is dropped, if each method is left out in turn, and if the reference is 53.6 rather than 41.5?
5. Can the model be run with exports counted, so that the export factor in Table 8 is a one-at-a-time change in the model?
6. Why does the land-use source carry 32% of the short-term variance when the two land totals agree to 0.01 Mha?
7. How representative are 2021 to 2023 of average Australian crop and livestock output, and how do the own-productivity readings move on a ten-year window?
8. Would you be willing to have Table 9 rated by two or more people outside the project, against purpose definitions written without reference to any method?
9. Does the model's 8.0 MJ a person a day as eaten represent the whole population, including children, and how does it compare with an estimated average requirement for Australia's age structure?

## 6. Abstract and highlights

The abstract states most findings accurately, and it is to the author's credit that it reports both lines of the harmonisation rather than the more favourable one. It overstates or blurs in four places. First, "in world-average hectares, 134 to 343 million, about three to eight times" attributes to the aggregate methods a reading that the footprint does not make (M1) and includes a method with no world-average reading (minor 1). Second, "Weather and waste take the model from 18.9 to 55.3 million" reads as a joint range when it is two opposite one-at-a-time changes (M7). Third, "This narrative critical review finds that no single method serves every purpose" rests on a matrix rated by the author with an AI second rater (M4), and the abstract should say so or drop the claim to a finding. Fourth, "applied to Australian data for 2021 to 2023 (FAOSTAT 2025; the model on its own 2025 layer)" is accurate, but the abstract should not then speak of "common" data without that qualification.

The highlights are within length. Highlight 2 ("closes 4.1% or 38.5% of the distance to the model, by line drawn") is accurate but will be opaque to most readers; "depending on whether productivity is treated as an input" would be clearer. Highlight 3 (1.6 to 2.8 times) is accurate for the pooled footprint and HALF. Highlight 4 should add that protein falls from 93 to 81 g a day under variant 1. Highlight 5 has the problem described for the abstract. Highlight 1 should note that the span's ends are not like-for-like (minor 3).

## 7. Points I would most want answered in a response letter

1. Whether the footprint's world-average-hectare reading is withdrawn as the footprint's reading, with line (b) presented as the faithful comparison, and whether a reading from the current national accounts is added (M1, M2).
2. Whether the own-productivity readings are shown algebraically as land-weighted production-to-consumption ratios, with the binding-land-type footprint reading reported beside the pooled one (M3).
3. How the author's model is separated from its roles as reference and rater: reference-free spread measures first, and Table 9 rated by people outside the project or the model removed from it (M4).
4. A robustness table for the shares closed, and a third harmonisation that equalises the per-person requirement and waste basis (M5, M6).
5. Whether the export factors in section 6 are recomputed as changes in the model, the weather-and-waste sentence is rewritten in the abstract and highlights, and the data-window and land-source questions are answered (M7, M8).
