# Referee report: "Well-known carrying-capacity methods tested on common Australian data" (Paper B, methods), submitted to Ecological Economics

Reviewer B, ecological economics (ecological footprint and carrying capacity literature)

## 1. Summary of the submission

The paper asks why published estimates of how many people Australia can feed differ by about an order of magnitude (9.4 to 100 million in the author's framing) and argues that the difference comes mainly from what each method counts, not from its data. It catalogues eleven families of carrying capacity and land-demand methods (section 4), runs six of them plus the author's own land-and-diet model on a single 2021 to 2023 FAOSTAT data set for Australia, checks each implementation against its authors' published figures where possible (section 5, Table 3, Table S1), and then reruns the methods on equalised inputs while keeping each method's defining choices (section 5.11, Table 5). It concludes that equalising inputs does not narrow the spread between methods (12 to 343 million both before and after) and that inputs account for about 10% of each method's distance from the author's corrected model. Using the author's model alone, it then varies seven design choices one at a time (exports, pasture, waste, land base, diet, drought allowance, spatial unit, time frame) and finds that definitions move the answer from 18.9 to 55.3 million, against a Monte Carlo spread of a factor of 1.26 across settings the author judges defensible (section 6, Table 6). It closes with a rating of seven methods against four planning purposes (Table 7), in which the author's model is the only method rated well suited for all four, and a twelve-item reporting checklist (Table S4).

## 2. Overall assessment

The paper has real merit. Running several capacity and land-demand methods on one data set, with explicit fidelity checks against the original authors' numbers, is rarely done and is worth doing; the reproduction of Henry and Godden (1995), the HALF index for most countries, and the gap-attribution exercise in Table S1 show unusual care, and the reporting checklist is a useful practical output. The topic fits Ecological Economics, which published Lenzen and Murray (2001) and much of the footprint debate the paper draws on.

My recommendation is **major revision**. The central claim, "the gap between the methods is the method", is close to true by construction of the harmonisation, and the decomposition that is meant to quantify it uses the author's own model as the yardstick. Four of the six comparator methods are not carrying capacity methods, and the paper's capacity readings of the ecological footprint in particular mix actual hectares with world-average hectares, which inflates the "three to eight times the model" result that the abstract leads with. The author's model is privileged at several points in the design (anchor, the only method given a sensitivity analysis, the author's own purpose ratings), and the claim that settings matter little beside definitions depends on where the author draws the line between the two. These are fixable, but they go to the heart of whether the method comparison is fair and correctly attributes each method's reading.

## 3. Major issues

**3.1 The headline finding is largely true by construction, and the decomposition that quantifies it is anchored on the author's model.**
Section 5.11 states that every method "kept its defining choices, such as world-average productivity for the footprint and HALF and cropland only for people per hectare", and that "no method was given the model's diet, yields or land classes". Equalising data while preserving definitions cannot be expected to remove definitional differences, so "the spread among the methods does not narrow" (section 5.11) is a confirmation of the design rather than a finding. The quantitative claim, that "inputs account for about 10% of the distance of each reading from the corrected model and the methods for about 90%", is then measured as distance from the author's corrected model, "on one set of rows with the methods weighted equally", with "10 to 18%" on other row sets. The main text does not define the ratio-scale decomposition, the row sets, or why distance from one tested method, rather than dispersion among all of them, is the right quantity. The range statistic itself ("12 to 343 million as run and 12 to 343 million harmonised") is set by two extreme cells, the single-commodity fish reading and the top footprint build, and would not change whatever happened to the other rows. This matters because the abstract and highlights present the 10% as an estimate of how much of the published spread is due to data. To resolve it: (a) state plainly that the harmonisation holds definitions fixed, and recast the finding accordingly; (b) report an anchor-free measure of dispersion (for example the standard deviation of log readings across methods, as run and harmonised) and a formal decomposition of log differences into input and definition components (a two-factor Shapley or ANOVA-style split would do), with the formula in the main text; (c) show the result with and without the model rows and with and without the extreme cells; (d) report the 10 to 18% range wherever the 10% appears, including the abstract and highlights.

**3.2 The capacity readings of the ecological footprint and HALF are not what their authors' methods produce, and the paper's own adjustment shows the difference is large.**
Section 5.4 divides "Australia's land" (363.5 Mha of actual cropland and pasture) by the food footprint per person, which Simpson, Petroeschevsky and Lowe (2000) express at world-average productivity, to obtain 146 to 343 million. That divides physical hectares by world-average hectares. National footprint accounting compares footprint and biocapacity in the same unit, with yield factors (and equivalence factors) applied to the nation's own land; the paper notes in section 4.5 that Wackernagel et al. (1999) did exactly this and printed Australia's biocapacity, and it describes the local-productivity adjustment in section 5.11 (Wackernagel and Rees 1996, p. 78). Applied, that adjustment brings the footprint to 64.8 to 84.6 million and HALF to 76.0 million (Table 5, column e), against 146 to 343 and 276 in Table 3. The column headed "(a) As the authors would run it" in Table 5 therefore does not contain the reading the footprint's authors would produce; column (e) is closer to it. HALF is additionally disclaimed by its authors as "not predictive" (section 4.6), and people per hectare is "not" a capacity in Cassidy's own sense (section 5.6). Yet the abstract's "The aggregate methods read 134 to 343 million, about three to eight times the model" and section 7.6 rest on the unadjusted readings. This is the point a footprint specialist reading Ecological Economics will raise first. To resolve it: present the footprint and HALF capacity readings in consistent units (yield-factor adjusted) as the primary readings and the world-average-hectare division as a labelled counterfactual; use current National Footprint Accounts methods (Borucke et al. 2013; Lin et al. 2018, which the supplement already cites) rather than, or alongside, a 1991-92 food row; and revise the abstract's "three to eight times" to the adjusted figures, which are roughly 1.5 to 2 times the corrected model.

**3.3 Most of the tested methods do not produce the published figures the paper sets out to explain.**
The motivating span in Table 1 comes from an export-proportional count (CSIRO and University of Queensland 2025), a newspaper article (Bellotti 2017), committee judgements (Jones committee 1994; National Population Council 1992), an unsourced statement (PMSEIC 2010) and the author's own thesis. Of the seven methods tested, only the energy and protein balance reproduces a published Australian people count. The commodity balance reading is "this project's" (section 5.7), the failure view is "the model's own runs" (Table 5), and the footprint, HALF and people-per-hectare readings are capacity readings their authors did not make. The paper therefore explains a span it partly constructs. To resolve it: add a column to Table 1 mapping each published figure to the tested method (or design choice in section 6) that reproduces it, and say plainly which published figures no tested method reproduces; move the claim in section 1 that "the spread is produced mainly by what each method counts" so that it applies to the readings the paper generates, unless the mapping supports it for the published figures.

**3.4 The author's model is privileged in the design, and the competing-interests statement does not cure that.**
The paper discloses that the model is the author's own and states that "it was tested by the same procedures as every other method" (section 9.5). It was not, in four respects. First, it is the reference point for column (d) of Table 5, for the 10% decomposition, and for "three to eight times the model". Second, it is the only method given a design-choice sensitivity analysis (section 6) and a Monte Carlo (section 6.9); no other method's internal uncertainty is reported, so the comparison of "definitions" against "settings" is available only for the author's model. Third, the failure-probability "method" is the model itself read through Fearnside's frame (sections 5.8 and 7.8), so Table 7 effectively scores the author's model twice. Fourth, Table 7 is "the author's ratings", and the model is the only method rated well suited for all four purposes. For purpose 2, the justification is that "one model run under each definition spans most of" the range (section 7.3), which only the author's model was given the chance to do. A "second reader" re-derived the ratings "blind" (section 7.1), but no criteria, rater identity or agreement statistic are reported. To resolve it: give the rating criteria in advance of the ratings, as a rubric tied to what each test showed; report independent ratings and inter-rater agreement; remove the failure view as a separate column or label it as a mode of the land-and-diet balance; anchor comparisons on a neutral reference (the cross-method median or the published figure) rather than on the model; and, if Table 7 cannot be made independent, move it to the discussion as the author's view.

**3.5 The contrast between "definitions" and "defensible settings" depends on a classification the paper does not justify.**
The highlights and abstract contrast a definitional span of 18.9 to 55.3 million with a factor of 1.26 "across defensible settings". The Monte Carlo varies 13 choices that the author judges defensible (section 6.9 and S5), and 81% of its variance comes from one of them, the window of herd and meat data; five range ends "are the author's judgement and have no outside source". The diet, which moves the answer by 2.14 to 7.88 (Table 6), the waste rate, and the reserve share are classed as definitions, not settings, although a planner would regard the diet and waste rate as uncertain inputs rather than as matters of definition. Yields under climate change, parameter uncertainty in conversion factors, and data revisions do not appear in the Monte Carlo at all. Read this way, "only 1.26" (highlight 5) is a statement about a narrow and author-chosen distribution, not about the robustness of the model. To resolve it: state the classification rule (what makes a choice a definition rather than a setting) and apply it to all methods; report a global sensitivity analysis on the model with diet and waste included as uncertain where they are uncertain; and remove "only" from the highlight.

**3.6 Section 6 compares heterogeneous objects under one "factor" and ignores interactions.**
Table 6 ranks choices by the factor each moves the answer, but the rows switch between the corrected and improved releases, two of them compare the model with a published count or a separate export arithmetic rather than changing a setting inside the model ("Exports counted ... the model against the CSIRO and UQ proportional count"; "exports alone as an energy count"), and one compares the 5th and 95th percentiles of a Monte Carlo. The diet results show strong non-linearity ("a dip at 2.5% meat and eggs and a cliff between 5% ... and 7.5%", section 6.5), which is typical of a fixed-proportion model in which whichever land runs short sets capacity. In such a model one-at-a-time factors are not additive and their ordering depends on the base point; the "ladder" of section 6.10 is acknowledged to be "a reading of the spread, not a decomposition", which in turn means Figure 3 cannot support the ordering in section 6.11. To resolve it: hold one release throughout Table 6; separate in-model changes from comparisons with external counts; run at least a two-level factorial (or variance-based sensitivity) over the seven choices to show interactions; and present the ladder, if it is kept, as illustration only.

**3.7 The pasture-and-crop pairing explanation is untested but carried into the conclusions.**
Section 5.11 says what remains above the model "is consistent with the pairing of pasture-fed food with crops ... the pairing was not removed from the model to test it", and section 7.6 repeats it as the interpretation of the gap. Since the model is the author's and the pairing is its distinctive feature, this is the one test the paper most needs. It also bears on economic substitution: a fixed diet with a binding scarcest land type assumes no substitution between foods, which the optimised diets (Table S2, up to 422 million) show to be the decisive assumption. To resolve it: run the model with the pairing relaxed (diet allowed to substitute within food groups, or pasture and crop pooled in calorie or protein terms) and report the reading beside the adjusted footprint and HALF figures.

**3.8 Fidelity failures are larger than the summary claims suggest.**
The abstract says the methods "were applied ... and checked against their authors' published figures where these could be reached", and section 9.1 says that residuals "traced, where traceable, to data years, revisions and rounding". But crop land for plant foods in the footprint is not reproduced by any build (0.074 to 0.142 ha against 0.312, S3.2), nor is wool pasture; HALF fails for New Zealand (94.8 against 135.76); people per hectare is 22 to 42% above the paper and the United States is not reproduced (feed share 41% against 67%); and three footprint builds disagree by up to 36% on pasture. In Table S1 several single causes explain -621.7%, -779.0% or -1056.6% of a residual, which shows the residuals are tiny relative to the sensitivity to plausible causes, so the attribution is ill-conditioned and cannot be read as tracing the residual to any cause. To resolve it: add a fidelity summary row to Table 3 that classifies each component as reproduced, partly reproduced or not reproduced with the residual; reword 9.1 so that the untraced failures are stated beside the traced ones; and in Table S1 report the sensitivity of each cause in absolute terms rather than as a share of a near-zero residual.

**3.9 The common consumption basis is inconsistent across methods and with the model.**
Section 5.1 sets the consumption basis at the FAO food supply per person, 3,525 kcal and 120.7 g protein a day, which is food available at retail and includes household waste; S3.1 notes that the energy-and-protein factor "implies 15.6 MJ a person a day, nearly twice a plain requirement of 8.8 MJ", and that at 8.8 MJ the reading is 243 million. Elsewhere the paper uses 2,700 kcal (Cassidy), 2,100 kcal (export energy count, section 6.1) and the model's own diet with its own waste rate. Equalising on food supply both penalises some methods and double-counts waste relative to the model, which charges waste separately. The FAO per-person figures also rest on FAO's head count of 26.20 million, not the common 27.6 million (S3). To resolve it: harmonise on one stated requirement (intake, not supply) with waste treated once, show the readings on supply and on requirement side by side, and reconcile the population basis.

**3.10 The framing of the published span and its factor of 10.6 rests on two soft ends the paper itself disowns.**
Section 1 says "Both ends of that span are soft": the low end, 9.4 million, is the author's own figure "before its errors were corrected"; the high end is a report that sums to 90, not 100, and calls its own metric "misleading as an indicator of food security"; and Henry and Godden's 109 million lies above the span. The section correctly concludes that the span "is not a measured spread of independent estimates", but the abstract opens with it and highlight 1 states it without qualification. To resolve it: either drop the factor of 10.6 from the abstract and highlights or report a span that does not depend on a superseded own figure and a self-disclaimed count (for example the interquartile range of Table 1, with a note on the excluded figures).

**3.11 Missing literature that an Ecological Economics reader would expect.**
The footprint critique is represented by Giampietro and Saltelli (2014), Blomqvist et al. (2013) and a reply, but not by the core critiques and reviews in this journal and its neighbours: van den Bergh and Verbruggen (1999, Ecological Economics) on spatial sustainability, trade and the footprint; Ayres (2000) and the commentary forum in Ecological Economics 32(3); Fiala (2008) on the footprint's measurement of land and technology; Kitzes et al. (2009) on the research agenda; Borucke et al. (2013) on National Footprint Accounts methodology; and van den Bergh and Grazi (2014) on the footprint's policy relevance. Human appropriation of net primary production (Haberl et al. 2007; Krausmann et al. 2013), the most direct ecological-economic alternative to land-based capacity, is absent as a family, though section 4.9 alludes to a cap on NPP. Arrow et al. (1995) on carrying capacity and economic growth, and the open-economy critique that runs from Cohen (1995) and Chisholm (2000), are mentioned but not engaged; the model is "self-sufficient by design" (section 8.2), which needs a defence in an economics journal, not only a limitation note. To resolve it: add these works where they bear on the argument, add HANPP to the catalogue, and add a paragraph in section 2 or 8 on what a capacity figure means in a trading economy.

**3.12 The review component does not meet the standard the paper sets for itself.**
Section 3.1 cites Snyder (2019) and Haddaway et al. (2020) for the rule that "any review should state its selection rule and what it left out", but no counts of works found, screened, read or excluded are given, and "well-known methods" is not defined. Several works central to the argument are "not seen; cited from its abstract", including the author's own co-authored papers (section 1: "the three papers not seen"), the Latin hypercube and variance-based sensitivity sources (S5), Erb et al. (2016) and Shepon et al. (2016). A methods review cannot rest key positions on abstracts, and readers will find it odd that the author's own papers are cited from their abstracts. The repository has no DOI and its deposit "is on hold at the author's decision" (section 9.4); Table 2 and Figures 1 to 3 are not built. To resolve it: report the selection counts and criteria; read or drop works cited from abstracts; deposit the archive with a DOI at submission; and supply the missing table and figures.

9. Section 5.10 compares the 1-in-10 point (30.8) with the Monte Carlo range of the average year (36.3 to 45.8). This is not informative, since the two answer different questions.

## 5. Questions to the author

1. What exactly is the "ratio scale" decomposition behind the 10%, and what are the row sets that give 10 to 18%? What is the share if the reference is the cross-method median rather than the corrected model?
2. If the footprint and HALF are run in consistent units (yield-factor adjusted) as their authors would, which of the paper's main claims still hold, and what is the multiple of the model?
3. What does the model read with the pasture-crop pairing relaxed? Is the remaining gap to the adjusted footprint and HALF then closed?
4. Which published figures in Table 1 does each tested method, or each section 6 design choice, reproduce?
5. What is the rule for classing a choice as a "definition" rather than a "setting"? Would a Monte Carlo with the diet and waste rate drawn from plausible ranges still give a factor near 1.26?
6. Who was the second reader for Table 7, what did they see, what criteria did they apply, and how often did they agree with the author?
7. Why does the harmonisation use FAO food supply rather than a requirement, and how do the readings change on a requirement basis with waste counted once?
8. What would a footprint built on current National Footprint Accounts give for Australia's food row, and how does it compare with Simpson's 1991-92 row?
9. Why are the author's own 2014, 2015 and 2017 papers cited from their abstracts?
10. Will the archive be deposited with a DOI before publication?

## 6. Note on the abstract and highlights

The abstract states the comparison and its main numbers clearly and discloses that the author's own model is among the methods tested. It says more than the evidence supports in four places. "Published estimates ... run from about 9 to 100 million, a factor of 10.6" presents as a measured span what section 1 itself calls a span of two soft ends. "The aggregate methods read 134 to 343 million, about three to eight times the model" rests on capacity readings that the footprint, HALF and people-per-hectare authors do not make, in mixed units, and falls to roughly 1.5 to 2 times once the paper's own local-productivity adjustment is applied. "Inputs accounted for about 10% of each reading's distance from the model: the gap between the methods is the method" omits the 10 to 18% range, the anchoring on the model, and the fact that definitions were held fixed by design. "Its spread across defensible settings is a factor of 1.26" does not say that the settings and their ranges are the author's judgement and exclude the diet. The sentence "The paper is a critical review, with a narrative synthesis" sits oddly late and undersells a paper whose contribution is a quantitative method test.

The highlights repeat these points in stronger form. Highlight 1 drops the soft-ends qualification. Highlight 2, "the gap between them is the method", states as a finding what the design assumes. Highlight 3 gives the 10% without its range or anchor. Highlight 5's "only 1.26" overstates robustness for the reason given in issue 3.5. I would ask for highlights that state what was done (seven methods, one data set, fidelity checks), the adjusted multiple of the aggregate methods over the model, the input share as a range, and the reporting checklist, which is the paper's most transferable contribution.
