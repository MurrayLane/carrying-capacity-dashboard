# B66e readings: Paper B sections 1 to 3 (targeted reads)

Eleven register rows were read at TARGETED depth against the PDFs in `Literature review/`. Every file was opened first and is the paper its row names (title, authors, year). Page numbers are the PDF's own printed pagination; for Greenhalgh 2018 they are the footer "x of 6". Machine-readable version: `qa/reports/B66e_readings.csv`.

## One paragraph per paper

**L193 Biermann and Kim 2020.** A review of the planetary boundaries framework, built on a citation analysis of the three key planetary boundary papers (p. 498). It sets out critiques from earth system science (pp. 501-502), development studies (pp. 502-503) and science and technology studies (pp. 503-504), covers downscaling and national assessments (pp. 504-506), and ends on earth system targets and the Earth Commission (pp. 511-513). It concludes the framework lacks support from the Global South (p. 513). The term carrying capacity does not appear. The characterisation holds.

**L194 Rees 2023.** Argues from evolutionary ecology and energy dissipation that humanity is in advanced overshoot and has already exceeded the long-term human carrying capacity (p. 509). It gives eight billion people in November 2022 and a more than 100-fold rise in real gross world product over two centuries (p. 515), and predicts a major population correction (p. 509). Long-term carrying capacity is put at 100 million to 3 billion, cited from two outside sources (p. 522). The characterisation holds.

**L195 O'Neill and others 2018.** Compares 2011 consumption-based footprints for seven biophysical indicators, shared equally per person from planetary boundaries for 7 billion people, with 11 social thresholds for over 150 countries (pp. 88, 94). No country meets basic needs at a globally sustainable level of resource use (p. 88). The ecological footprint boundary is 1.72 gha per person; 43 per cent of 149 countries are within it (p. 90). The characterisation holds and is fuller than the title.

**L196 Montoya, Donohue and Pimm 2018.** A three-page Forum piece arguing that the planetary boundary for biodiversity lacks theoretical or empirical support for a global threshold, is too vague for managers and can promote harmful policies (pp. 71-72). Box 1 argues the extinction rate tipping point is operationally undefined (p. 71). It proposes measuring resilience, resistance, variability and persistence instead (p. 73). Biermann and Kim cite it (pp. 501-502). The characterisation holds.

**L197 Swiader and others 2020.** Uses ecological footprint and biocapacity to assess environmental carrying capacity of Wroclaw, Poland, for 2016, comparing a top-down footprint, a bottom-up carbon footprint and a hybrid (pp. 56, 65-66). Footprint is 5.419 gha per person top-down and 7.323 hybrid; biocapacity is 0.091, so the footprint is about 60 and 80 times biocapacity (pp. 65-67). The city exceeds its capacity and both footprints likely overestimate (pp. 56, 70). The characterisation holds.

**L198 Zhang and others 2018.** Builds an index system of urban resource and environment carrying capacity for China from 18 indicators in five groups, weighted by the Analytic Hierarchy Process (pp. 90-94). For Tianjin, 2007 to 2015, the comprehensive score rises from 24.04 to 71.23 on a 0 to 100 scale (p. 96). Lane 2010 is cited once, in a list of land-use planning applications (p. 91). The index system and the Lane citation hold; "among the most cited" is a citation count the paper cannot show. Partly holds.

**L199 Qiu 2024.** Chapter 3 of Qiu's book zones China's 31 provinces into four functional types using 40 carrying capacity and carrying pressure indicators (pp. 151, 172, 179-181). Data are 2006 values from the 2007 China Statistical Yearbook (p. 182). The carrying ratio is pressure divided by capacity, above 1 meaning overloaded (p. 185); five regions exceed 1.1 (p. 192). It is the same book as L108 (DOI suffix _3 against _1). The characterisation holds.

**L200 Haddaway and others 2020.** A perspective naming eight problems in the conduct and reporting of environmental reviews that claim to be systematic, each with a fix (pp. 1583-1586). It states that not all reviews need be systematic and that transparency suits every review (p. 1583). Box 1 lists actions (p. 1587). The title line holds, but the eight problems are set against systematic review standards. Partly holds.

**L201 Greenhalgh, Thorne and Malterud 2018.** A perspective arguing that systematic and narrative reviews serve different purposes and are complementary: systematic reviews summarise data on narrow questions, narrative reviews give interpretation and critique (p. 2 of 6; conclusion p. 4 of 6). It describes hermeneutic, realist, meta-narrative, integrative and critical styles of narrative review (p. 3 of 6). The characterisation holds.

**L203 Snyder 2019.** An overview of three review approaches, systematic, semi-systematic and integrative, compared in Table 1 (p. 334), with a four-phase process (Table 3, p. 336) and criteria for judging a review (Table 4, p. 338). For integrative reviews the typical purpose is to critique and synthesise and the search is usually not systematic (p. 334). The characterisation holds.

**L223 Grant and Booth 2009.** Analyses 14 review types against the SALSA framework: search, appraisal, synthesis, analysis (Table 1, p. 94). A critical review shows extensive reading and critical evaluation, goes to conceptual innovation, and yields a hypothesis or model; it seeks the most significant items, has no formal quality assessment and is typically narrative (pp. 93, 94, 97). The earlier text was an empty placeholder, so nothing was held; the row text is replaced by the content above.

## Flags

1. **B OUTLINE section 2 and section 3 (L193, L194, L195, L196).** Section 2 names Hardin, Seidl and Tisdell, Sayre, Meyer and Ausubel, Price, Cohen and the thesis typology; the planetary boundaries debate and the overshoot argument are not named there or in the section 3 selection statement. An addition is needed if B1-2.a stays.
2. **B OUTLINE section 2 wording (L193).** Biermann and Kim write about planetary limits and never use carrying capacity. A sentence citing them should say planetary limits.
3. **B OUTLINE sections 1, 3, 4 (L199).** The Qiu chapter is dated 2024 by publication but its data year is 2006 (p. 182) and it serves the 2006 to 2010 plan period (p. 151). A rule "literature since 2011" gives different answers by publication year and by data year; the selection statement should say which.
4. **B OUTLINE section 3 and Type line (L200).** Haddaway and others list lack of a protocol and of comprehensive search as problems for reviews claiming rigour (pp. 1584-1585) and ask all reviews for transparency (p. 1583). Section 3 should state the selection rule and exclusions plainly.
5. **B OUTLINE section 3 (L201 against L203).** Greenhalgh and others regard how-to guides that require explicit search and strict inclusion criteria for narrative reviews as misleading (p. 3 of 6); Snyder asks for reasoned, transparent inclusion criteria in any approach (p. 337). Section 3 should say which it follows.
6. **B OUTLINE Type line and B3.1 (L223).** Grant and Booth define "critical review" as a type and treat narrative as a style of synthesis (Table 1, p. 94). "Critical narrative review" combines Grant and Booth with Greenhalgh and others; cite each for its part.
7. **Register only (L198).** "Among the most cited works citing Lane 2010" cannot be tested from the paper; keep it only with a named count source. A sentence saying Zhang builds on Lane 2010 would not be supported (p. 91). No paper line is affected.

No flag touches Paper A's OUTLINE. No claim in either paper is changed by these readings.

## Wrong-value check

Had the L195 line read "Finds most countries meet basic needs within planetary boundaries", it would NOT hold: O'Neill and others report on p. 88 that no country meets basic needs at a globally sustainable level of resource use, and on p. 90 that only 16 countries stay within all seven per capita boundaries.
