# B66a readings: Paper A section 1 (population debate, food security in planning)

Eight rows read at TARGETED depth. Every file is the paper its row names (title, authors, year, volume checked on the first page). Page numbers are each PDF's own pagination; for L149 and L154 the PDF carries only an article number, so page numbers are the PDF's printed 1 to N.

**L145.** Parr (2018) is a commentary in Australian Population Studies. It argues that Australia should adopt an integrated population policy built on three strands: migration, fertility and research. It names the number of permanent visas as the main lever, asks for settlement to spread beyond Sydney, Melbourne and Brisbane, and says fertility policy should aim at family wellbeing rather than birth rates. It sets no national population size and treats growth in the largest cities as the harder problem. The register line held on topic; size was not a topic and research policy was missing, so both are corrected. Holds: partly.

**L147.** McDonald (2017) splits the rise in employed persons from July 2011 to July 2016 (738,800) into net migration (613,400), population growth (98,900) and participation change (26,500), using ABS data and survival methods. The migration share is a residual that also carries error. Skilled migration appears as background from 1995, not as a measure of population growth. The paper closes by asking governments to plan for the infrastructure effects of growth. The register line said it showed the skilled-migration basis of population growth; the paper measures employment, so the line is corrected. Holds: partly.

**L148.** Summerhayes and Baker (2026) combine 102 food policy documents, 25 interviews and 500 surveys. The policy documents and interviews span Queensland, Western Australia and Victoria; the surveys were all taken in Brisbane. Twenty documents cite food security and nine set it as a specific goal. The authors read the results as showing weak food security agendas at all levels and propose a three-tier, food systems and rights-based approach. The register line held except the survey location, which is corrected. Holds: partly.

**L149.** Summerhayes and Baker (2024) set out the Tr-GaF framework and apply it to 102 food policy documents from 40 agencies, collected in 2021 and 2022. Food-related urban planning is the dominant frame in 13 documents and appears in 49, the fewest dominant cases of four themes. The authors report weak integration, with gaps between levels of government and between sectors. The register line held. A printed state-level count of 37 in Table 2 does not match its rows, which add to 47. Holds: yes.

**L150.** Summerhayes and Baker (2025) report 25 interviews with senior planners and policymakers in Queensland, Western Australia and Victoria, held from September to December 2022. Eighty-four per cent agree food is a stranger to urban planning. Few food elements are integrated, mainly protection of rural agricultural land and urban food retail. Market pressure, land scarcity and stakeholder conflict limit more. The register line said these were the only elements; the paper says primarily, so the wording is softened. Holds: partly.

**L151.** James and O'Neill (2016) set ABS agricultural census data for the Sydney Basin from 1992 to 2011 beside 23 non-ABS reports. Farm area fluctuates without a confirmed decline, ABS counts rose with a 2005-06 method change, and error margins exceed 25 per cent in many figures. Some farm value is turf and cut flowers, not food. The paper leaves open whether Sydney's food supply is at risk and asks for consistent longitudinal data. The register line held on contested data; the method change and non-food share are added. Holds: partly.

**L154.** Falster, Coats and Abram (2024) ask whether the 2017 to 2019 Murray-Darling Basin drought lay outside the range of natural variability. They build linear inverse models from 1900 to 2016 rainfall and ocean temperature. The basin-wide three-year cool-season and annual deficits were unprecedented in the instrumental record and beyond the model range, most strongly in the northern basin; most single years were inside the range. The register line held at basin scale. The three-year sequence is what made the event unusual. Holds: yes.

**L224.** Lawrence, Richards and Lyons (2013) argue that Australia's export-oriented, productivist farming, shaped by neoliberal policy, will not secure long-term food security. They report 93 per cent of food eaten at home is grown in Australia and 60 per cent of output is exported, and set out limits from land, water, climate and productivity, with 5 to 10 per cent of people food insecure. All figures come from other sources dated 2008 to 2011. The register row was empty, so the text is new. Holds: no (no prior line).

## Flags

- L154 (Falster 2024): Paper A OUTLINE section 8 (drought by return period: the worst year of 5, 10, 20, 50, 100) and the narrative arc point 3: this paper finds the unusual feature of the 2017-2019 event was the three-year sequence (PDF pp. 6, 9); a single-year return-period reading does not describe it. If section 8 or A1.5 cites the event as a benchmark, state it as a three-year basin-wide event.
- L224 (Lawrence 2013): Paper A OUTLINE sections 8 and 12, and Paper B OUTLINE section 6 (whether exports count): the 22 million fed at home plus 40 million fed abroad (p. 30) comes from a government expert panel report with no base year and is not on the same footing as the model's short-term capacity of 33.3 million for 2011 (A OUTLINE headline). Quote it only with its source and say the diet and export treatment differ.
