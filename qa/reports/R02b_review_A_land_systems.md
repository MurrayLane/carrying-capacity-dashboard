# Referee report: "Australia's carrying capacity 2011 to 2025 by region on the corrected releases" (Paper A, results)

Submitted to Population and Environment. Referee standpoint: land-systems and agricultural science, with working knowledge of Australian land use, crop and livestock statistics (ABS, ABARES), drought and NRM regional geography. I read the paper, the supplementary information, the reference list and the highlights. I did not read the drafter notes appended to the paper file, as instructed.

## 1. Summary of the submission

The author takes a land-use carrying capacity model built for a doctoral thesis (Lane 2014) and published in this journal (Lane et al. 2015; Lane 2017), freezes its method, and re-runs it on 2025 inputs beside corrected 2011 inputs. Capacity is defined as the number of people a region's agricultural land could feed on a stated diet with no trade. Two presets are run: a "short term" on the diet and production of the data year in an average season, and a "long term" 100-year self-sufficiency plan with a low-meat organic diet, minimal liquid fuel and a 1-in-100 year weather allowance. Nationally, short-term capacity rose from 33.3 to 41.5 million while population rose from 22.3 to 27.6 million, so the ratio held near 1.5; long-term capacity barely moved (15.35 to 15.6 million), so a 100-year plan would cover 69% and then 57% of the population. A cumulative walk attributes the short-term gain mainly to livestock output (+4.7 million), lower per-capita consumption (+4.2 million) and a shift in the red-meat mix (+1.8 million), offset by the land layer (-2.6 million). The paper argues that capacity is set by whichever of cropland and pasture runs short, that pasture binds nationally, and that this explains why field-crop yields barely matter and why capacity falls sharply when small units must feed themselves (SA2 sum 37% of national). Regional status flips between 2011 and 2025 are screened for land-definition and boundary artefacts and against a 2025 Monte Carlo range; two short-term flips (Mackay Whitsunday, North Tasmania) survive. A drought allowance built from state cereal-yield records puts national capacity above the population in a 1-in-10 year and below it in a 1-in-20 year. The paper closes with one-at-a-time measures (livestock output, diet, waste, grain stores, drought-proofing) and limitations.

## 2. Overall assessment

The question is a good one for this journal, and a fourteen-year update of a published regional capacity model, with an explicit attempt to separate real change from data artefacts, would be a useful contribution. The author deserves credit for several things that are rarely done in this literature: the decomposition sums exactly and is shown in full (Table 2, which I checked); the regional flips are screened rather than simply counted; the North East Victoria population error is reported openly; and the paper is candid that the 2011 inputs carry no uncertainty. The arithmetic I could check from the tables is internally consistent.

My recommendation is **major revision**. The headline numbers rest on three components whose agricultural meaning has not been established: a land layer whose change between 2011 and 2025 looks to be dominated by a change of land-use source and classification rather than a change on the ground; a livestock layer whose gain may reflect the cattle cycle, carcase weights and grain finishing rather than pasture productivity; and a drought factor derived from cereal yields that is applied without test to pasture and livestock output, which carry most of the short-term capacity. The paper also leans heavily on internal release names and on a companion paper for key numbers, and parts of the submission (Table S1, several figures) are not yet built. None of this is fatal, but each must be addressed before the regional and production figures can be relied on.

## 3. Major issues

**3.1 The land layer change is largely a change of data source, not of land use, and it is not separable from the livestock layer.**
Where: section 4, Table 2; section 3 (Swan); section 10 and S5 (Hawkesbury-Nepean-Sydney); section 5.4; S1.
The land layer removes 2.6 million people from short-term capacity and 1.2 million from the long term, the largest single negative effect. The paper itself shows how much of this is definitional at regional level: Swan's land layer takes capacity to 0.01 of its 2011 value because of a boundary change; Hawkesbury-Nepean-Sydney's cropping land halves from 389 to 191 km2 "on an unchanged boundary"; SA Arid Lands' capacity rises 6.5 times from the land layer alone. Australian land-use mapping changed substantially between the thesis-era inputs and the 2020-21 data used here (the ABARES catchment-scale and national land-use products, the ALUM classification versions, and the ABS agricultural census redesigns all differ in what counts as cropping, modified pasture and grazing of native vegetation). A national-level fall of this size between 2011 and 2025 is not something the land-use literature would recognise as real. The regional 2 and 0.5 thresholds screen only the extreme cases; the bulk of a national -2.6 million could come from many regions each moving by 10 to 40%, none of which trip the flag.
A second problem compounds this. If pasture and livestock yields are derived as observed output divided by the area in the same land layer, as is usual in models of this kind, then a smaller pasture area in 2025 mechanically raises yield per km2, and the land and livestock effects are two halves of one identity. In a cumulative walk with land placed first, the land layer would then take capacity off and the livestock layer would hand much of it back. The paper notes that "each effect depends on its place in the order" but gives no order test.
Why it matters: the abstract and highlights name livestock as the leading driver; if half the livestock effect is the land artefact reversed, the attribution is wrong.
What would resolve it: (a) state the 2011 and 2025 land sources, classification versions and the crosswalk used, and give national areas of cropland and pasture in both years beside the ABS agricultural census and ABARES land-use totals for the nearest years; (b) state how zone pasture and crop yields are computed and from which area; (c) report the decomposition in at least the reverse order, or a Shapley-type average over orders, so readers see how much attribution moves; (d) if feasible, rerun 2011 on a land layer built with the 2025 classification (or the reverse) to isolate the definitional component.

**3.2 The livestock gain needs an agricultural explanation: herd cycle, carcase weight and grain finishing.**
Where: section 4 (Table 2 and the caveat on "two years of heavy slaughter"); section 6; S4 (meat window explains 81% of short-term variance); S6 (herd-cycle range 43.5 to 55.4 million).
Livestock output adds 4.7 million people, the largest single positive effect, and the meat-data window alone explains 81% of the variance of 2025 short-term capacity. The 2025 window, 2020-21 to 2024-25, runs from a herd-rebuilding trough with very low turn-off to the heavy destocking of 2023-24 and 2024-25. The paper does not say what window the 2011 thesis inputs used or where it sat in the cattle cycle (the years following the Millennium Drought were themselves a rebuilding phase). Comparing a rebuild-phase base with a window that includes liquidation would bias the change upward. Heavy slaughter during dry seasons is herd liquidation, not sustainable output from pasture.
Two further sources of the gain need separating. Average carcase weights have risen markedly over this period, and the share of cattle finished on grain in feedlots has grown. Grain-finished beef draws on cropland, not pasture. If the model credits all beef output to the pasture area, rising feedlot turn-off will appear as a gain in pasture productivity, inflating exactly the land type the paper says binds. The same question applies to dairy, where supplementary grain and irrigated pasture are a large part of output in the southern regions. The paper cites Wiedemann et al. (2015) for long-run productivity gains, but those figures run to 2006-10 and cannot by themselves explain a 2011 to 2025 change.
Why it matters: the paper's central mechanism (pasture binds, so livestock output drives capacity) makes this layer carry the headline; if part of it is cycle timing or grain feeding, the "rise" is partly an artefact.
What would resolve it: give the 2011 livestock window and its cycle phase; show herd numbers, slaughter, carcase weights and meat output for both windows; state how grain-fed output is allocated between cropland and pasture; and report the 2011 to 2025 change on a cycle-matched pair of windows (the herd-cycle runs already exist on 2025-organic and should be moved to the corrected release).

**3.3 The drought allowance applies a cereal-yield factor to pasture and livestock output without test, yet carries the headline about bad years.**
Where: section 2; section 8 and Table 6; abstract and highlight 3; section 11.5; S6 (state factors).
Every yield, including pasture products and livestock, is scaled by one factor per state taken from wheat, oats and barley over 150 years. The paper says, correctly, that this "has not been tested", but then builds the abstract statement "above balance in a 1-in-10 year and below it in a 1-in-20 year" on it, along with the long-term preset (where the weather allowance is worth +119%), the grain store equivalents and the drought-proofing measure. From a land-systems standpoint the assumption is doubtful in both directions. Livestock output does not fall with pasture growth in the drought year: destocking raises meat output in the short run, and the fall comes in later years through a smaller breeding herd. High-rainfall and irrigated dairy and beef districts (Gippsland, the south-west of Victoria, northern Tasmania) do not lose output in proportion to Victoria's worst cereal year; applying Victoria's 100-year factor of 0.22 to Corangamite's or West Gippsland's dairy output is not credible without evidence. Conversely, multi-year pasture deficits do more damage than a single cereal year implies. In addition, a cereal series of 150 years detrended to a single factor mixes very different technology and land-use eras, and the paper does not show how the trend was fitted.
Why it matters: with pasture binding, the drought result is almost entirely a statement about livestock output in bad years, which the factor was not built to describe.
What would resolve it: test the factor against observed livestock and dairy output in recent drought years (2002-03, 2006-07, 2018-19, 2019-20) using ABS and ABARES series, and against modelled pasture growth (for example the AussieGRASS series used by the Queensland and Commonwealth drought programs); if a separate livestock factor or a lagged response cannot be built, present the drought results as a crop-yield scenario and soften the abstract and highlight accordingly. Show the trend fit behind the state factors.

**3.4 Regional population inputs and boundaries are not on a consistent footing.**
Where: section 2; section 3 (five zones on older boundaries; North East Victoria); section 10 and S5 (SA Arid Lands, Swan).
The 2011 population of SA Arid Lands is "the model's own figure" while 2025 is "allocated from estimated resident population by residential land", and the region's population falls from 31,361 to 8,049. A 74% fall in the population of the SA rangelands over the period did not happen. North East Victoria's 2011 population was found to be a copy of Goulburn Broken's late in the work. NRM region boundaries themselves changed during the period (most notably in New South Wales, where catchment management authorities gave way to Local Land Services regions in 2014, and in parts of Queensland and Western Australia), and the region names used here mix eras. The paper flags five zones but does not say which boundary version each of the 53 regions uses in each year, or why the count is 52 NRM regions plus the Northern Territory.
Why it matters: status depends as much on population as on capacity, and the flip tests examine capacity only, as the author concedes ("a population input can make or unmake a flip").
What would resolve it: rebuild 2011 and 2025 regional populations by the same method from ABS ERP by SA2 (2011 and latest available) on one boundary version, list each region's boundary source in a supplementary table, and rerun the flip classification on the consistent populations. A full audit of the 2011 population inputs against ABS ERP should be reported, given that one copy error has already been found.

**3.5 The "pasture binds" mechanism and the scale curve are properties of the model's fixed-proportion structure, and should be presented as such.**
Where: section 6; section 5 and Table 3; S2 ("capacity was exactly proportional to land"); abstract and highlight 5.
The mechanism is a Leontief rule: a diet needs cropland and pasture in nearly fixed proportion; cropland may substitute for pasture but pasture may not substitute for cropland. Under that rule capacity is linear in land at a fixed mix (S2 confirms this exactly), field-crop yields cannot matter while pasture binds (a 24% yield fall costs 0.2%), and any partition that separates rangeland from cropland must lose capacity. The SA2 figure of 37% and the region figure of 44% therefore follow largely from the model's assumptions and the geography of where pasture and cropland lie. That is a legitimate result, but the paper presents it as a finding about Australia rather than about the model. Several empirical facts cut against the strength of the rule: grain is fed to pigs, poultry and feedlot cattle, so cropland is already part of meat supply; sown and irrigated pastures in cropping districts can be cropped; and arid-zone pasture has very low and highly variable productivity, so the "stranded pasture" of SA Arid Lands and the WA Rangelands carries little food in any case. The national cropland-share test was run for Australia only, as the paper notes.
Why it matters: the abstract treats the scale curve as a result ("their capacities sum to 37%"), and section 11 uses the mechanism to rank policy measures.
What would resolve it: state plainly in the abstract and section 5 that the scale loss follows from the fixed-proportion assumption; report a sensitivity in which a modest share of pasture in cropping zones can be cropped, or in which monogastric meat is allocated explicitly to cropland; run the cropland-share test for at least a few regions; and report pasture area by productivity class so readers can see how much of the binding pasture is arid rangeland.

**3.6 Three yield bases, and comparisons across them, make section 5 and section 8 hard to follow and easy to misread.**
Where: section 5 (state-yield basis), sections 7, 8, 11 (local basis), S2 (national yield vector, under which the regions keep 33%, not 44% or 63%); Table 3; section 8.2 ("The 44% of section 5 ... is a different measure").
A reader meets 33%, 44% and 63% for the same 53 regions, 54% and 58% for the same SA4s, and a 2011 reference of 30.7 million beside 33.3 million. The random and contiguous-split comparison in 5.3 and S2 is made on a third basis to the one in Table 3. The paper tells the reader each time, but the net effect is that no single number for "how much the regions lose" survives a first reading.
What would resolve it: choose one basis for the main text (the local basis is the natural one for a regional self-sufficiency question), move the others to the supplement with a single reconciling table, and run the random and contiguous-split comparison on that basis.

**3.7 Uncertainty is incomplete and the treatment of change is asymmetric.**
Where: section 2; section 9.1; Table 1; S4.
The 2011 results carry no range; the change is compared with the 2025 range only, which the paper rightly says "is not a test of whether it is real". The ranges sample method and judgement choices, of which five ends have no outside source and together drive most of the width. They do not sample the input data themselves. ABS agricultural statistics at sub-state level carry substantial sampling error (the paper cites James and O'Neill (2016) for errors above 25% in the Sydney Basin), and the land-use mapping carries classification error. North Tasmania's 2011 status sits at 1.04 times capacity and North East Victoria's at 0.93; both rest on unsampled inputs.
What would resolve it: sample the 2011 inputs with the same Monte Carlo, so the change can be tested directly; add input-data error from published ABS relative standard errors by region; and either source or narrow the five judgement ends.

**3.8 The submission is not self-contained, and internal release names obscure it.**
Where: throughout; title; Table S1 ("not built"); figure placeholders ("no plotting script exists"); section 8.1 and Conclusions (failure probability cited to "Paper B"); section 2 (method "in the supplementary information" but the supplement does not contain it).
The title and text use software release names (`2011-fixed`, `2011-fixed-2`, `2025-mirror`, `2025-store-1`, `2025-organic`) in nearly every paragraph. A reader of this journal needs to know which method and which data year a number uses, not a repository tag. The 5.8 to 6.1% annual failure probability, quoted in the conclusions, is not derived here. Section 2 says the full model is in the supplementary information, but the supplement holds regional detail only; the 2011 and 2025 data sources (land use, crop and livestock production, consumption, population) are never listed by source and year. Table S1, which the method section relies on, is not built, and several figures are not yet drawn.
What would resolve it: replace release names with plain labels (for example "2011, corrected method"; "2025, published method") and a one-line key in section 2; add a data table giving each input layer's source, years and geography for both data years; build Table S1 and all figures; and either derive the failure probability here or drop it from the conclusions.

**3.9 Claims for measures are stronger than the evidence.**
Where: abstract (last sentence); section 11; section 8.3; section 11.4 ("The store is free in the model").
The abstract says measures "widen the margin by acting on what binds: livestock output, diet and waste in the short term, drought-proofing and grain stores in the long term". These are one-at-a-time model perturbations with no cost, feasibility or time dimension, as section 11.7 concedes. A 10% rise in livestock output is not a measure until it is tied to a mechanism (genetics, pasture improvement, feedlotting, which itself consumes grain). The grain store figures use 303 kg a person of feed grain from a 2050 scenario of Foran and Poldy (2002) applied to 2025; current domestic feed grain use is reported annually by ABARES and industry and should be used instead.
What would resolve it: reword the abstract to say which settings move capacity most in the model; tie each measure to a real pathway and cite the agricultural evidence for its plausible size; replace the 2050 feed-grain figure with current data.

**3.10 Missing literature a land-systems reader would expect.**
The paper does not engage with the Australian national land-use and agricultural-outlook modelling (Grundy et al. 2016 is in the reference list but not cited; the Australian National Outlook work and the land-use futures studies of Bryan and colleagues are absent), with the ABARES land-use mapping and ABS agricultural census as data sources and their known breaks, with the rangeland pasture-growth and drought literature (AussieGRASS and the grazing-land drought studies), with the evidence on organic yield gaps (the long-term preset assumes fully organic farming, worth -18% here, with no source for the penalty), or with the classic carrying-capacity framing in this journal's tradition (for example Cohen's "How many people can the Earth support?"). "CSIRO and University of Queensland 2025", cited in section 1.1, is not in the reference list. Seventeen entries in the list (for example Ananda et al. 2023, Han et al. 2024, Payen et al. 2022, Timmer 2024, Wood et al. 2018) are never cited.

## 5. Questions to the author

1. What are the land-use sources and classification versions for the 2011 and 2025 land layers, and what are the national cropland and pasture areas in each? How do they compare with ABS agricultural census totals for the nearest years?
2. Are zone pasture and livestock yields computed as output divided by area in the same land layer? If so, how much of the land and livestock effects cancel?
3. What years and cycle phase does the 2011 livestock window cover? How do herd numbers, slaughter and carcase weight compare between the two windows?
4. How is grain-finished beef, and grain and supplement fed to dairy cattle, allocated between cropland and pasture?
5. Has the cereal drought factor been compared with observed livestock or dairy output in any drought year? What does the drought result look like if livestock output is held at trend in the drought year and reduced in the following year instead?
6. Why does the SA Arid Lands population fall by three quarters between the two years, and would a consistent ABS ERP allocation change any flip?
7. What drives the North Tasmania gain of 35% in short-term capacity? Is it dairy expansion on newly irrigated land, and if so, how does a model with no water limit treat it?
8. What drives the Mackay Whitsunday flip, given that the region's agriculture is dominated by sugarcane, a field crop?
9. Can the 2011 inputs be sampled so that the change, not only the 2025 level, carries a range?
10. Which NRM boundary version does each of the 53 regions use in each year?

## 6. The abstract and highlights

The abstract states the national numbers accurately and I could reproduce them from Table 2 and Table 6. It overreaches in four places. "Livestock, consumption and the red meat mix of the diet drove the rise" omits that the land layer was the largest single negative effect and that the attribution depends on order (issue 3.1); two of the three named drivers are lower per-capita demand, not more food from land, and that should be said. "The margin depends on the weather: it is above balance in a 1-in-10 year and below it in a 1-in-20 year" rests on an untested cereal factor applied to livestock (issue 3.3) and should name that basis. "When every SA2 must feed itself, their capacities sum to 37%" should say this is on the state-yield basis and follows from the model's fixed-proportion rule (issue 3.5); on the local basis it is 54%. The closing sentence on measures states model perturbations as policy findings (issue 3.9). The title's "on the corrected releases" is jargon for this readership.

The highlights inherit the same problems in compressed form. Highlight 1 should say "short-term" is the model's definition and that 41.5 million assumes no trade and the 2025 diet. Highlight 2, "Livestock, diet and consumption drove the rise", omits land. Highlight 3 should say the bad-year factor comes from cereal yields. Highlight 5 should name the yield basis. Highlight 4 ("57% of the 2025 population") is accurate as stated.
