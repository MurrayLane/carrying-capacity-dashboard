## Headline

**The report does not contain the 60 million, or any statement of how many people Australian food production feeds or could feed.** "60 million" and "million people" (in the sense of people fed) occur nowhere in it. It has no food energy, kilojoule or calorie figure for Australia, no people-count method and no statement that Australia produces a given share of the food it eats. It is a statistical yearbook of money values (production, processing, retail, trade) plus commodity supply-and-use tables in tonnes.

So the trail for the 60 million ends unsourced. Ridoutt et al. 2017 (L079), p. 212, writes: "On the basis of food energy requirements, Australian agriculture currently produces enough food for some 60 million people, which is more than twice the current local population. Exports of meat, grain, dairy products and wine are most significant (Department of Agriculture 2014)." The citation sits at the end of the second sentence. What the report does support is that second sentence: Figure 1 (p. 9) gives export shares of grains 35%, meat 24%, dairy products 7%, wine 6%. Ridoutt's citation is best read as covering the export composition, not the 60 million; at most it is a misattribution. PMSEIC 2010 (L136) gives the figure with no source (B52). Neither source shows a calculation.

## (1) Statements of how many people Australian food production feeds

None. The only "million people" or "million persons" in the report are employment counts:

- Minister's welcome (unnumbered, PDF p. 4): "this edition of food stats shows that 1.6 million people are employed throughout our food sector".
- p. 9: "The food industry in Australia ... employed around 1.6 million persons in 2012–13, around 14 per cent of total employment in Australia."

Nearest material, none of which is a people count:

- p. 11, on population: "Australia's population grew by 1.8 per cent in 2012–13, compared with an average of 1.3 per cent over the previous 20 years (Figure 5)." Table 10.1 (p. 171) gives Australia's 2013 population as 22.5 million (CIA World Factbook). Twice that is 45 million, not 60, so Ridoutt's "more than twice the current local population" cannot be rebuilt from this report's own population figure either.
- p. 11 also mentions energy, but only as a dietary note: "the required energy intake increases as children mature and then declines as people age (Nutrition Australia 2012)." No national energy supply is given.
- Food balance sheets, Tables 9.1 to 9.13 (pp. 144-169): production, imports, exports, domestic supply and food consumption (kt and kg per person) by commodity group and country, "Annual average, 2007–2009", source "Food and Agriculture Organisation, FAOSTAT nutrition data". They are in tonnes, not energy, and the report does not convert them into people fed. Example, Table 9.2 continued (p. 147), Australia, cereals: production 31 352 kt, imports 437 kt, exports 17 879 kt, food consumption 1 972 kt, 92 kg per person.

What the 60 million would count (food energy, exports in or out, which year) cannot be settled from this source because the figure is not in it.

## (2) Share of domestic food consumption produced in Australia

Not stated. No percentage of food consumed that is produced in Australia appears (PMSEIC's "almost 93 per cent", citing DAFF 2009, has no counterpart here). The closest statements:

- p. 27: "Australia's food imports are generally processed, high-value products. Food imports are a relatively minor source of nutritional value compared with domestic food production (Thompson & Penm 2011)." The underlying source is Thompson, N & Penm, J 2011, 'The nutritional value of Australia's food imports', Australian commodities, vol. 18, no. 2, pp. 18-22 (reference list, p. 34). That article, not seen, is the likelier origin of a domestic-share figure.
- p. 20: "Trade in minimally transformed food is dominated by exports. Imports amounted to only 6 per cent of the value of exports in 2012–13 (Figure 19)."
- Dairy essay, p. 36: "Australia has recently exported around 40 to 45 per cent of its milk production, although historically it has exported over 60 per cent."

## (3) Food exports and imports, 2012-13, as stated

Values (Australian dollars, ABS International trade, cat. no. 5465.0; 2012-13 marked "p Preliminary" in Table 1):

- Table 1 (p. 8): value of food exports $31.8b; share of total merchandise exports 12.8%; minimally transformed share of food exports 41.4%; value of food imports $11.6b; net food exports $20.2b; value of farm and fisheries food production $42.8b.
- p. 19: "The value of Australian food exports increased by 4.3 per cent to $31.8 billion in 2012–13, the highest (in 2012–13 dollars) since 2001–02 ... The value of Australian food imports was $11.6 billion in 2012–13, largely unchanged from the record level of the previous year. As a result, Australia's net exports of food, the difference between the value of food exports and food imports, increased by 5.3 per cent to $20.2 billion in 2012–13."
- p. 24: "Food exports are dominated by grains ($8.6 billion in 2012–13) and meat ($7.7 billion)."
- p. 27: "Australian food imports increased to $11.6 billion in 2012–13, $280 million or 2.5 per cent higher than in 2011–12."
- Table 5.1 (p. 68), total food and beverage exports 2012-13: $31 755m (minimally transformed 13 134, substantially transformed 18 304, elaborately transformed 316).
- Table 6.1 (p. 90), total food and beverage imports 2012-13: $11 568m (minimally transformed 784, substantially transformed 10 270, elaborately transformed 515).
- Figure 1 (p. 9) composition: exports $31.8b (grains 35%, meat 24%, dairy products 7%, wine 6%, seafood 3%, other 25%); imports $11.6b (beverages 20%, horticulture 17%, seafood 13%, dairy 6%, other 44%).
- p. 33: "Australia was ranked 13th as a food exporter in 2012"; Table 2 (p. 31) gives Australia 2.4% of world food exports by value in 2012.

Volume: the report gives no total volume (tonnes) of food exports or imports. Volumes appear only commodity by commodity in the supply-and-use tables, on marketing years, with the note "Export data are not comparable with financial year export figures published elsewhere" (Table 2.1, p. 50). Example: 2012-13 wheat production 22 461 kt, exports 18 644 kt; canola production 4 010 kt, exports 3 512 kt. The dairy essay (p. 36) ranks markets "by volume" but gives no tonnage.
