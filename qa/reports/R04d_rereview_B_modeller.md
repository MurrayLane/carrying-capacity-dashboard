# Referee report, second round: "Well-known carrying-capacity methods tested on common Australian data" (Paper B, methods)

Reviewer standpoint: quantitative modeller of food systems and land use. Files read: `paper_B_draft1.md` (drafter notes and bracketed flags ignored), `supplementary.md`, `references.md`, `highlights.md`. Figures are still placeholders and were judged from their descriptions.

## 1. Summary of the submission

Published figures for how many people Australia can feed run from about 9 to 100 million. The paper sorts carrying-capacity methods into families (section 4) and runs five published methods on FAOSTAT data for 2021 to 2023. These are the Henry and Godden energy and protein balance, a food-row ecological footprint after Simpson et al., the HALF land-per-diet index, Cassidy et al.'s people per hectare, and a commodity balance derived from Foran and Poldy. Beside them it runs the author's own land-and-diet model at three tiers, with the model's drought allowance also read through Fearnside's failure-probability frame. Each implementation is checked against its authors' published figures where these could be reached. The checks give a mixed result: Henry and Godden and the thesis reproduce, HALF and Simpson's pasture partly reproduce, and Cassidy and several footprint components do not. The central comparison (section 5.11, Tables 5 and 6) asks how much of the distance between each method and the model closes once inputs are equalised. The answer turns on whether the productivity basis of the footprint and HALF counts as method or input: 4.1% of the log distance closes on one reading and 38.5% on the other. Once those two methods are expressed at Australia's own productivity, the residual gap above the model (1.6 to 2.8 times) is said to be consistent with the model's pairing of pasture-fed food with crops, which binds on pasture. Relaxing that pairing takes the model from 41.5 to 81.7 million (section 5.14). Section 6 uses the model to measure how far each design choice moves the answer, from 1.09 for land to 7.88 for an optimised diet, and sets these beside a 19-setting Monte Carlo spread of 1.58. Section 7 rates the methods against four purposes and proposes a twelve-item reporting checklist.

## 2. Overall assessment

The question suits Ecological Economics. Carrying capacity has a long history in this journal, from Seidl and Tisdell onward, and running published methods side by side on one data set is a useful thing to do. The paper's strongest features are its candour and its audit trail. Reproduction failures are reported, not hidden (Table S6). Gap attribution refuses to tune outputs (section 5.12, Table S1). The paper concedes in section 5.11 that "which line holds is a classification, not a finding". The definition-versus-setting distinction of section 6.9 and the reporting checklist (Table S4) are the most transferable contributions.

As an analysis, however, the comparison does not yet support the weight placed on it. Three of the five "methods" are turned into capacities by the author's own conversion, which their authors did not make. Two of those conversions rest on implementations that do not reproduce. The headline closure statistic is measured against the author's model as reference. The "harmonised" comparison leaves the largest differences in energy basis and data layer in place. The attribution of the residual gap to the pasture-crop pairing is a single-variant test that overshoots two of the three readings it is meant to explain. The uncertainty analysis does not draw the parameter that binds the model, pasture productivity. Several tables still carry notes that their source files "need regenerating", and the figures are not drawn. **Recommendation: major revision.** The design is sound enough to be worth finishing, but the headline numbers would change, or would need to be framed far more narrowly, once the issues below are dealt with.

## 3. Major issues

**M1. Three of the five comparators are capacities the author constructs, and the comparison mostly measures that construction.** HALF is, in the authors' words, "not predictive" (section 4.6). Cassidy's measure is a yield metric. Foran and Poldy "give no capacity figure; one can be read as production over the requirement per person" (section 4.3). The manuscript says so at each point ("Read in the footprint way, which HALF is not", section 5.5; "Read as a capacity, which Cassidy's measure is not", section 5.6). Yet Tables 3, 5 and 6 treat these readings as each method's verdict on Australia, and the abstract and highlights report multiples of the model on that basis. For HALF the capacity reading is Australia's land divided by the diet's world-average hectares. On line (b) it becomes 76.0 million only after a further conversion to Australian productivity that HALF's authors never make. In that case the reading belongs to the converter, not the method. The fish reading of the commodity balance (12 to 17 million) is a single commodity with no substitution (section S3.5). Yet it sets the low end of the commodity balance and drives that method's negative share closed (-15%, section S3.11). *What would resolve it:* separate, in Tables 3 and 5 and in the abstract, the methods that produce a people count (energy and protein balance; footprint, via bio-capacity over footprint) from those the author converts (HALF, people per hectare, commodity balance). Report the closure statistics on the first group alone, as well as on all five. Drop the fish reading from the method centre, or justify it.

**M2. The harmonisation leaves the largest input differences in place, and the line between input and method is blurred for more than productivity.** Section 5.1 calls the data common. The abstract, however, says the model runs "on its own 2025 layer", and Table S5 shows that the bases still differ in ways a modeller would treat as inputs. The methods divide by FAO supply at 3,525 kcal (14.8 MJ). The model divides by 8.0 MJ as eaten and charges waste separately. That gap is a factor of about 1.85 in the denominator, larger than most of the design-choice factors in Table 8. The intake variant (section 5.13) addresses this only in one direction. Even on FAO's eaten shares the per-person need stays well above 8.0 MJ, and 20 to 37% of food energy has no waste factor (section S3.10). Further: the model's meat window is 2020-21 to 2024-25, its land is the 2020-21 holdings, and its population is 27.6 million against FAO's 26.2. Henry and Godden's run carries forward 1990-91 sown pasture, which section S3.1 says "moves the result by 31%". For Henry and Godden, the "input factor" in Table 5 (0.69 to 1.11) is mostly the choice of Nix's 70 Mha scale-up, which is as much part of their method as world-average hectares are of the footprint. The paper picks one input (productivity) to classify two ways. A reader will ask why only that one, when the energy basis, the land scale-up and the waste treatment have equal claim. *What would resolve it:* a table with one row per input and one column per method, showing for each harmonisable input the value as run and as harmonised, with a note on whether the author treats it as input or method. Then report the closure statistic under a third line, on which energy per person is also put on one basis (intake, with each method's waste treatment made explicit). Run the model on the FAOSTAT layer as a further row, so that "common data" is literally true for at least one model reading.

**M3. The closure statistic is anchored on the author's model, and the headline figures are driven by two methods.** The distance in Table 6 is the mean absolute log ratio to 41.5 million, the corrected model. Against the median or geometric mean of the method centres, line (a) gives -4.9% and -6.3%, and line (b) gives 16.0% and 17.2%, well below 38.5%. Per method (section S3.11), the 38.5% on line (b) comes almost entirely from the footprint (61%) and HALF (68%); the other three methods give 26%, 8% and -15% on both lines. So the line (b) result says that expressing two land-based indices at Australian rather than world productivity moves them toward the model. That is expected, given that Australian pasture is far less productive than the world average, and it is not a finding about inputs in general. "Method centre" is not defined in the main text. The footprint contributes many readings to the 19 while weighing as one method, and the meaning of "five methods weighted equally" depends on how the centre of a multi-reading method is formed. *What would resolve it:* make the reference-free spread measures (standard deviation of log centres, highest over lowest) the primary statistics, with distance to the model secondary. Define the method centre in the text. Report Table 6 with the footprint and HALF removed, so readers can see what equalising does for the other methods. Remove 4.1% and 38.5% from the highlights unless both are explained there.

**M4. Attributing the residual gap to the pasture-crop pairing is not yet demonstrated.** Section 5.14 says what remains above the model "is consistent with" the pairing. The test is one relaxation (variant 1), which reads 81.7 million: 173% of the gap to footprint build 1, 117% to HALF and 93% to build 3. A cause that overshoots two of three targets is not a close attribution; it shows that something else in the model pushes the other way and is not identified. Variant 1 also lowers protein from 93 to 81 g a day, so the diet is not held constant. Variant 2 changes the diet outright, as the paper concedes. The paper notes that "the test cannot show it is the only cause". A modeller would want a structural bridge. Start from HALF at Australian productivity, add the model's features one at a time (Australian yields and pasture productivity by class; fixed diet with protein held; waste charged separately; reserve and non-food land; pairing), and report each step in both orders, or with a Shapley-type average, so the order does not decide the answer. Section S3.12 lists three tests "proposed and not run", including land pooled at world-average hectares. That is precisely the bridge step that would separate productivity from pairing; it should be run.

**M5. The binding constraint is pasture, but pasture productivity and its variability are outside the uncertainty analysis.** Table S2 shows the default diet needs 5.93 ha of pasture a person against 0.30 ha of cropland. Capacity is therefore close to the pasture area divided by pasture need, and section 6.4 confirms that doubling cropland moves the answer by only 1.09. Yet pasture productivity is one of the 23 undrawn choices (section S5), as are thin-zone yield error and nutrient composition. The two largest drawn drivers are the land source (32%), a binary switch between ABS holdings and the ABARES raster, and the energy requirement (27%). Both are judgement-weighted. The resulting 1.58 is a spread over the settings the author chose to vary, as section 6.9 says. Its use in sections 6.10, 6.11 and 9.2 to rank settings below definitions ("narrower than that span") is therefore partly built in by the design. Separately, the drought allowance scales the harvest by a 150-year record of wheat, oats and barley yields, treated as "one factor on every yield" (section S3.6). Applying cereal-yield variability to a pasture-bound, herd-based system is a strong assumption. Australian livestock output responds to drought with lags and herd liquidation, which the paper itself says no method captures (section 8.1). The failure probability at the 2025 population (5.8 to 6.1% a year, section 5.8) depends on that assumption. It also moves to 1.5 to 2% on the other tiers (section S3.6), and it is the kind of figure a policy reader will lift out of context. *What would resolve it:* draw pasture productivity (for example, carrying capacity in dry sheep equivalents by land class, over the range the ABARES or state sources give) and the livestock conversion factors, and report the new range and Sobol shares. Test the drought allowance against a livestock or pasture-growth series, or state plainly that the failure view does not cover the binding constraint. Remove the per-year failure probability from section 5.8 unless it carries those caveats in the same sentence.

**M6. Fidelity: the readings that carry the headline rest on implementations that do not reproduce.** By the paper's own grading, the footprint's plant-food cropland is 66 to 76% low, its crop-plus-pasture total 10 to 12% low and its wool pasture 85% low. Cassidy's people per hectare are 22 to 42% high, and the United States feed share is 41% against 67% (Table S6). The footprint's primary reading (64.8 to 117.0 million) is the low end of the aggregate range that the abstract and highlights report as "1.6 to 2.8 times the model". When the component that fails worst is plant-food cropland, a cropland-bound footprint reading is not well identified. Section 5.2 says "the authors were not consulted". For HALF and Cassidy this matters, because both groups published supplementary data that the paper says it did not read (Cassidy's supplement, section 5.6). The Global Footprint Network's national accounts would also give a current, reproducible footprint for Australia in place of a reconstruction of a 1991-92 paper. *What would resolve it:* contact the authors, or use their published data or code; for the footprint, add a reading from the current national footprint accounts as a check. Where an implementation stays unreproduced, mark the reading as the author's implementation "after" the method in every table and in the abstract, not as the method's reading.

**M7. The author's model is subject, reference, instrument and rated method at once; the safeguards described are not enough.** Section 9.5 and the competing-interests declaration state this openly, which is welcome. But the design still turns on it. The closure statistic is measured to the model (M3). Section 6 measures every design choice in the model alone, so the ordering in Table 8 is an ordering in this model's structure. A pasture-bound fixed-diet model will rate land as unimportant and diet as decisive by construction. Table 9's ratings were made by the author, and the only second rating came from "a blind checker session of an AI model, not a person" (section 7.1). Every disagreement was resolved toward the other methods. The model's own column is rated well suited for all four purposes, including purpose 2, which is explained by section 6, itself run on the model. *What would resolve it:* repeat at least the cheapest design choices of section 6 (exports, waste, land base) in one other method, such as the footprint or HALF at Australian productivity, so the ordering is shown not to depend on one model's structure. Have Table 9 rated independently by at least two people outside the project, and report their agreement (for example, Cohen's kappa). Alternatively, present Table 9 explicitly as the author's judgement and remove the rating claims from the abstract.

**M8. Presentation is not yet at a submittable standard.** Figures 1 to 3 and Table 2 are placeholders; Table 2 is "to be built from a catalogue csv that does not exist". Captions of Tables 3, 8, S5 and others say that a source file "still prints the earlier 13-setting improved range and needs regenerating" or "Both files need regenerating". Table S9 cites an internal QA report path. Table 4 mixes a 13-setting, 1,000-draw range (mirror) with 19-setting, 2,000-draw ranges (corrected, improved) in one column. Twenty-nine reference entries carry "[CHECK]" markers, and the reference list carries HTML comments. The AI declaration has bracketed model names, and the CRediT statement has no author name. The data statement (section 9.4) says the archive has no DOI. For an Ecological Economics readership the captions are also overloaded with file paths and build provenance that belong in the repository README, not in the article. *What would resolve it:* regenerate all source files so that captions no longer need to reconcile them; draw the figures; give the archive a DOI; strip build provenance from captions into one data-availability note.

**M9. Missing literature the field would expect.** The paper cites Alexander et al. 2016, Cassidy et al. 2013, Erb et al. 2016 and Gerten et al. 2020 (the last only second-hand, through Beltran-Peña et al.). It does not engage with the literature that has done this kind of comparison or decomposition at scale. That includes work on feed-food competition and the role of grazing land (Mottet et al. 2017), and on loss and waste along the chain as a share of land (Kummu et al. 2012; Alexander et al. 2017, "Losses, inefficiencies and waste in the global food system"). It also includes the planetary-boundary food-system studies that vary diet, waste and yield together and report the resulting population fed (Springmann et al. 2018; Gerten et al. 2020 read directly), and the "livestock on leftovers" work beyond van Zanten et al. 2016 (van Zanten et al. 2018; Schader et al. 2015). The trade dependence work (Fader et al. 2013, again second-hand; Porkka et al. 2013; Kummu et al. 2020) bears directly on Cohen's point about trade. Within Ecological Economics, the 1995 forum on carrying capacity (Arrow et al. 1995 and replies) and the footprint critiques beyond Giampietro and Saltelli would place the paper in the journal's own debate. *What would resolve it:* engage with these in sections 2, 4.6 and 8, and say where the decompositions in this literature agree or disagree with the ordering of Table 8.

## 4. Minor issues

1. Section 1 and the highlights frame the span as "about 9 to 100 million". The low end is the author's own long-term figure before its errors were corrected, and Table 1's Henry and Godden 109 sits above the top. A span that starts at a superseded, erroneous figure is an odd anchor. Consider giving 15.6 (corrected long term) or stating the span as "10 to 110".
2. Section 6.3: removing 12% avoidable waste raises the corrected model by a factor of 1.33. If waste were 12% of each food, the ceiling would be about 1.14. Please explain what else the waste setting carries (recycling credit, waste on the binding food group, cascade through feed).
3. Table S2: pasture per person doubles from 2.29 ha at 5% meat and eggs to 4.58 ha at 7.5%, while the step from 10% to 15% adds only 1.35 ha. Please explain the non-linearity (change of red-meat share across the guideline diets?), since the "cliff" in section 6.5 depends on it.
4. Optimised diet C (422.3 million on 0.06 ha of cropland and 0.13 ha of pasture a person) is fifteen times the population. Please state what it assumes about crop mix, markets and agronomic feasibility, or present it as an upper bound only. It sets the top of Table 8.
5. Section 5.1 gives the land base as 363.5 Mha; section 5.12 and section S3.7 use 396.1 Mha for 2011. Say once in the main text that these are different years.
6. Table 3: the people-per-hectare cell gives "145 to 147 million (about 190 on all cropland)", Table 5 gives 145 to 190 as run, and harvest matching gives 183. Use one convention.
7. Table 5 column (e) cites "(p. 78)" in its heading; put the citation in the caption.
8. Section 6.9 gives the 1.58 factor; section 6.11 adds "from lowest to highest draw the factor is 2.22, which passes the guideline diets and the drought allowance". Comparing an extreme-draw range with one-at-a-time factors is not like with like; drop it or qualify it.
9. Section 6.7: "The SA2 point sits on 2020-21 land and the others on the release land" is unclear to an outside reader. Say what the release land is.
10. Sections 5.8 and 6.6 relate the Jones committee's 30 to 35 million to the model's 30.8 at 1 in 10 and then say they "cannot be placed on the model's curve". Keep one of the two statements.
11. "Failure probability (Fearnside)" appears as a method column in Tables 3, 5 and 9, but it is the model's drought allowance renamed (section 5.8: "a test of the idea, not as an independent method"). Label it "the model, failure view" throughout, and do not count it among the methods tested.
12. Section 3.1 says works from 2011 onward were eligible, but the search strings, dates and counts are not given. A narrative review need not be systematic, but the selection rule should be reproducible.
13. Terminology: "balance" is used for the energy and protein balance, the commodity balance and the author's land-and-diet balance. Use distinct names in section 7.
14. Units: "kcal" and "MJ" alternate (8.0 MJ, 3,525 kcal, 15.6 MJ = 3,718 kcal). Pick one primary unit with the other in brackets.
15. Section 8.2: "Production within 100 km can meet the demand ... for only 11 to 28% of the world's people" should name the crop set to avoid misreading.
16. The abstract is 249 words, at the limit. Several sentences carry two qualifications each, and could be cut (see section 6 below).
17. Reference list: "Buzby, 2010. (cited in Lane, 2014)" is not a reference; cite the source or the thesis. Wackernagel and Rees 1996 needs place and edition.

## 5. Questions to the author

1. How exactly is "Australia's own productivity" computed for pasture in the footprint and HALF: from the output of meat and milk per hectare of all 331.87 Mha, or by land class? How sensitive is the 64.8 to 117.0 range to including the arid rangelands?
2. How is a method's "centre" formed when it has several readings, and how many of the 19 readings belong to each method?
3. In the model, what is pasture productivity per hectare by zone, where does it come from, and how far would a ±20% change move the short-term capacity?
4. Does the drought allowance scale pasture-fed output by the cereal yield factor? If so, how does that compare with observed falls in beef and sheep-meat output in 1982-83, 2002-03 and 2019-20?
5. Why is the model not run on the FAOSTAT 2021-23 layer as one reading, given that section S3 says the 2025 layer is taken as the same vintage?
6. Can variant 1 be run with protein held as well as energy, for example by substituting legumes, and what does it read?
7. Why were the authors of HALF and of Cassidy et al. not contacted, given that the reproduction failures are in components their supplements would settle?
8. Which of the six judgement-based settings in section S5 would you defend to a reviewer outside the project, and what would the range be if each were set to the widest published range (for example PAL 1.2 to 2.2)?
9. Is the 7.88 factor for the optimised diet a property of the method or of the solver's freedom? How does it change with a cap on any one food's share?

## 6. Note on the abstract and highlights

The abstract is accurate in its numbers and, unusually, admits the classification dependence of its main result. Three points overstate or understate.

First, "Five published methods ... were applied to Australian data" implies that each method's own output was obtained. Three of the readings are the author's capacity conversions (M1), and two rest on implementations graded not reproduced (M6). "The implementations reproduce them in part" is too soft for Cassidy's 22 to 42% and Simpson's plant-food cropland.

Second, "This narrative critical review finds that no single method serves every purpose" presents the author's own ratings, checked only by an AI session, as a finding.

Third, the sentence on weather and waste (18.9 to 55.3) and the 1.58 factor, set side by side, invite the reading that definitions outweigh settings. That ordering depends on which settings were drawn (M5).

The highlights are within the length limit, but two of them do not stand alone. "Closes 4.1% or 38.5% ..., by line drawn" means nothing to a reader who has not read section 5.11. "Settings, 1.58-fold" is shorthand. The highlight on pairing ("lifts it from 41.5 to 81.7 million") is accurate. It should not be read as attribution, and the main text rightly says it is not. I would replace the two cryptic highlights with one plain statement, for example: "Converting footprint and HALF to Australian productivity removes most of their gap to the model."

## 7. Five points I would most want answered in the response letter

1. **Harmonisation:** run a third classification on which energy per person and waste treatment are also on one basis, and run the model on the FAOSTAT layer, so that "common data" holds literally. Report how much of the 38.5% survives (M2).
2. **Reference and drivers of closure:** make reference-free spread measures primary, define the method centre, and show Table 6 without the footprint and HALF. Say plainly that the line (b) closure is the productivity conversion of two land indices (M3).
3. **Attribution of the residual gap:** run the structural bridge from HALF at Australian productivity to the model, feature by feature with order effects, including the land-pooled-at-world-average test already proposed (M4).
4. **The binding parameter:** draw pasture productivity and livestock conversion in the Monte Carlo, test the drought allowance against pasture-fed output, and restate the definitions-versus-settings comparison and the failure probability on that basis (M5).
5. **Independence and fidelity:** obtain an outside rating of Table 9, or recast it as the author's judgement; repeat at least two design choices in a second method; and resolve or clearly label the non-reproduced footprint and Cassidy readings, ideally after contacting their authors (M6, M7).

If these five are answered substantively and the presentation issues in M8 are fixed, I would expect one further round to be sufficient.
