# Referee report: "Australia's carrying capacity 2011 to 2025 by region on the corrected releases" (Paper A, results), submitted to Population and Environment

Reviewer standpoint: population and environment research, with a focus on demographic projections, population data and resource constraints.

## 1. Summary of the submission

The author updates his own land-use carrying capacity model, first published in this journal (Lane et al. 2015; Lane 2017), from 2011 data to 2025 data while holding the method fixed. Capacity is defined as the number of people a region's land could feed on a stated diet with no trade. The model is run under two presets: a "short term" preset (the data year's diet and production in an average year) and a "long term" preset (a 100-year self-sufficiency plan with a low-meat diet, organic farming, cut fuel use and a 1-in-100 weather allowance). Nationally, short-term capacity rose from 33.3 to 41.5 million while the population rose from 22.3 to 27.6 million, so the ratio held at about 1.5; long-term capacity barely moved (15.35 to 15.6 million), so a 100-year plan covers a falling share of the population (69% to 57%). A sequential decomposition attributes the short-term rise mainly to livestock output, consumption data and the red-meat mix, partly offset by a loss of land. Across 53 regions, the count over capacity fell from 22 to 16 in the short term, but after three screening tests (land-definition, boundary and 2025 Monte Carlo range) only two status changes are judged "clean and robust". A scale curve shows that the sum of unit capacities falls as units shrink, to 37% of national capacity at SA2 level on a state-yield basis, which the author traces to a model rule that cropland can carry pasture products but not the reverse. The paper then examines shocks (the nation is above balance in a 1-in-10 year and below it in a 1-in-20 year), method sensitivity, five regions in depth, and a set of one-at-a-time measures (livestock productivity, diet, waste, grain stores, drought-proofing) said to widen the margin.

## 2. Overall assessment

The question is squarely within the scope of Population and Environment, and a careful, transparent update of a model the journal has published before has genuine value. Several features deserve credit: the paper is candid about artefacts (the land-definition and boundary flags, the North East Victoria population error, the untested cereal-based drought factor), the population-weighted measure "population living in regions over capacity" is a good demographic summary, and the decomposition tables reconcile exactly (I checked Table 2 in both presets). The originality is moderate: the core contribution is a re-run of an existing model with new inputs, plus a finer scale curve and a regional robustness screen.

My recommendation is **major revision**. The headline comparison of 2011 with 2025 rests on inputs of different vintages and definitions, on a 2011 baseline with no uncertainty at all, and on an order-dependent decomposition, yet the abstract and highlights state the change and its drivers without those qualifications. From the standpoint of this journal's readers, the population side of the ratio is the weakest-documented part of the paper: sources, reference dates, vintages and the method of allocating population to NRM regions are not stated, and the paper sets out official population projections in its introduction and then never uses them. These are fixable, but they need new analysis, not only rewording.

## 3. Major issues

**1. The population inputs are undocumented and inconsistent across the two years.**
Where: section 2, section 3, section 10, Supplementary Section S5, and the North East Victoria and Swan discussions.
Problem: The paper never states the source, reference date or vintage of either population. The national 22.3 million (2011) and 27.6 million (2025) are presented without saying whether they are ABS estimated resident population at 30 June, which release, or whether the 2011 figure is the preliminary or the census-rebased estimate. The introduction quotes the Centre for Population's 27.5 million "in the March quarter of 2025", while the paper uses 27.6 million, without reconciling the two. At the regional level the two years were built differently: for SA Arid Lands "the 2011 population is the model's own figure, while the 2025 population is allocated from estimated resident population by residential land" (S5), and the region's population falls from 31,361 to 8,049, a 74% fall that no demographer would accept as real. North East Victoria's 2011 population turned out to be a copy of another region's, and for 2011 "each state's population" was set "to the sum of its regions (ERRATA error 6)".
Why it matters: Every status result, every flip and the population-weighted shares in Tables 4 and 6 are ratios with population as the numerator. The flip tests check only capacity: the paper itself says "the artefact test is made on capacity, not on whether the population change is real". Two discovered population errors in a small set of regions suggest others may exist.
What would resolve it: A data section (or supplementary table) giving, for each year, the population source, reference date, ABS release and geography; the concordance method from SA2 (or mesh block) to NRM region, and the ASGS edition; and the same allocation method applied to both years. Rebuild the 2011 regional populations by the 2025 method, or show the difference. Add a population test to the flip screen (for example, flag regions where the 2011 to 2025 population change differs from the ABS regional series by more than a stated margin). The unpublished "ERRATA" document should not be cited as a source; its content belongs in the supplement.

**2. "Change since 2011" conflates real change with changes in data sources and definitions.**
Where: section 2 ("within one method tier the change between 2011 and 2025 is a data change"), sections 3 and 4, Table 2, section 5.4.
Problem: A data change is not the same as a change on the ground. The land layer, the largest negative effect nationally (-2.6 million short term, -1.2 million long term), is taken from land-use mapping of different vintages; the paper flags only regions where land alone moves capacity by more than 2 times or less than 0.5 times, so a definitional shift of, say, 30% passes silently, and the national land effect is not examined for this at all. Hawkesbury-Nepean-Sydney's cropping land halves "on an unchanged boundary", which the author reads, rightly, as definition. The 2025 "data year" is itself a mix of vintages: livestock over 2020-21 to 2024-25, land of 2020-21, and the SA-level 2011 cells use 2020-21 land against a different reference (30.7 million). Table S1, which would give the settings of both presets in both years, is named but not supplied.
Why it matters: Readers will take "capacity rose by 8.2 million" as a statement about Australia's food system. Part of it is a statement about which datasets were used.
What would resolve it: A table of every input layer, its source, its reference period and its definition in each year, with a judgement on comparability. Supply Table S1. Quantify, or at least bound, the definitional part of the land effect nationally (for example by re-running the 2011 land on the 2020-21 classification where both exist). Say plainly in the abstract that the change is between two input sets of different vintages.

**3. The livestock-driven rise may reflect the herd cycle rather than a trend.**
Where: section 4 (last paragraph), Supplementary Section S6 ("The herd is also a store").
Problem: Livestock is the largest positive layer (+4.7 million short term). The paper concedes the livestock window "ends in two years of heavy slaughter", and S6 reports that "the single-year capacity runs from 43.5 to 55.4 million across 2016 to 2025 with the herd cycle". That range, 11.9 million, is larger than the whole 2011 to 2025 change of 8.2 million. It is reported on a different release (`2025-organic`) and kept out of the main text.
Why it matters: Heavy slaughter inflates output while a herd is being run down, so a destocking window overstates sustainable output. The headline "Livestock, diet and consumption drove the rise" may in large part be a statement about where the 2025 window fell in the cattle cycle. It also matters which single-year or window basis was used for 2011, which the paper does not say.
What would resolve it: Report the herd-cycle range on the corrected release in the main text; use a window long enough to span a full cycle, or present the 2011 to 2025 change across several window choices for both years; state the 2011 livestock window explicitly.

**4. The decomposition is order-dependent and the paper's causal language goes beyond it.**
Where: section 4, Table 2, abstract, highlights, conclusions.
Problem: The cumulative walk makes "each effect depend on its place in the order", as the author notes, and the table's "Interaction 0" row is zero by construction, not by finding. Livestock and consumption enter after land and before the red-meat mix; with a binding pasture constraint (section 6), the size of each later layer depends on what came before. The paper reports one order only. The sentence that consumption "would carry 3.8 million and the organic step 0.4 million" on the chain as built already shows that the attribution moves with the bookkeeping.
Why it matters: Demographers will recognise this as the standard problem of sequential decomposition (Kitagawa and Das Gupta style methods address it). The abstract's "Livestock, consumption and the red meat mix of the diet drove the rise" is one ordering's answer.
What would resolve it: Report a symmetric decomposition (Shapley or the average over all orderings of the nine layers, which is computationally trivial here), or at least the forward and reverse orders, and show the range for each layer. Remove the interaction row or explain that it is zero by design. Explain what the "consumption" layer is: a rise in capacity from consumption data implies lower per-person food requirements or a changed data source, and readers need to know which.

**5. Uncertainty is asymmetric and omitted from the headline statements.**
Where: sections 2, 3, 7.1, 8.1 and 9.1; abstract; highlights.
Problem: The 2011 results have no ranges, so the paper cannot say whether any 2011 to 2025 change exceeds its uncertainty; it says so for the long term but still headlines "57%" and "69%". The 2025 short-term range is 36.3 to 45.8 million, roughly plus or minus 12% around 41.5. The 1-in-10 margin is 1.12 and the 1-in-20 shortfall is 0.95; if the range scaled roughly proportionally under shocks (my rough reading, not a result in the paper), the 1-in-10 capacity at the 5th percentile would be about 26.9 million, below the 27.6 million population, and the 1-in-20 capacity at the 95th percentile about 29.0 million, above it. Neither the abstract's "above balance in a 1-in-10 year and below it in a 1-in-20 year" nor the third highlight carries any range. Further, the Monte Carlo is not an uncertainty analysis in the usual sense: five of 13 range ends "rest on the author's judgement", and a three-way judgement choice of meat window with equal weights alone explains 81% of short-term variance (S4). No uncertainty is carried for population, land-use mapping, yields or the drought factor.
What would resolve it: Run the same sampling on 2011 inputs, with common random numbers, and report a range for the change itself. Report ranges for the shocked national figures (Table 6) and for the 57% and 69% shares. Call the Monte Carlo ranges "sensitivity ranges over sampled method and input choices" rather than uncertainty, and state in the abstract that the 1-in-10 and 1-in-20 findings fall within them, if they do.

**6. The shock and long-term results rest on the least tested assumption in the model.**
Where: section 2 ("Applying a factor derived from cereal yields to pasture-fed output has not been tested"), sections 7.2 and 8, section 12.
Problem: The short-term capacity is "bound by pasture-fed meat and dairy" (section 6), yet the drought allowance scales every yield, including pasture products and livestock, by a factor derived from wheat, oats and barley. The weather allowance is the single largest long-term setting (+119%, Table 5), and it alone generates the 1-in-10 and 1-in-20 findings in the abstract. Livestock output in a single drought year often rises (forced turn-off) before it falls, so a proportional scaling is not obviously conservative or liberal. The factor is also taken from a 150-year record, while section 1.5 cites evidence that the climate since 1990 has reduced yield potential by 27% (Hochman et al. 2017); a stationary return period sits uneasily with that.
Why it matters: The paper's most quotable result depends on an assumption the author flags as untested in three places.
What would resolve it: Test the factor against pasture and livestock series (for example, pasture growth indices or livestock output in known drought years such as 2006-07 and 2018-19), or present the shock results under at least one alternative treatment of livestock. Show the return periods computed on a recent sub-period (for example, since 1990) beside the 150-year record.

**7. The regional long-term result depends on the method tier, and the reported tier is the outlier.**
Where: section 7.3, section 9.2, Supplementary Table S4.
Problem: Regions over capacity in the long term in 2025 are 36 (published), 32 (corrected) and 36 (improved). The paper reports the corrected tier, which is the only one of the three giving 32. The 2011 corrected count is 36. Therefore the long-term "net fall of 4" may be largely a tier effect, and the six regions that switch between corrected and improved (Mallee, Kangaroo Island, SA Arid Lands, SA Murray Darling Basin, South East SA, South West WA) are the regions where the long-term map is decided. Module interactions are large (-2.88 million short term, +3.20 million long term), so one-at-a-time module effects cannot be read additively.
Why it matters: Section 7.3's counts and the narrative that "the cropping belts and inland plains feed themselves" are presented as findings on one tier.
What would resolve it: Either justify, in this paper and not only by reference to an unpublished companion, why the corrected tier is the reporting basis, or report the long-term regional results on all three tiers. Add the method tier as a fourth flip test, which section 3 says has not been done.

**8. The population projections in the introduction are set up and then not used.**
Where: section 1.1; nothing after.
Problem: The introduction quotes the 2021 and 2023 Intergenerational Reports (38.8 million in 2060-61; 40.5 million in 2062-63) and the 2025 Population Statement (41 million by 2065-66). These are the obvious comparators for a capacity estimate, yet the paper never sets capacity against projected population. That is a notable gap for this journal: the projected 2065-66 population is almost exactly the 2025 short-term capacity (41.5 million), the 1-in-10 capacity (30.8 million) would be reached within roughly a decade on current growth, and the long-term capacity (15.6 million) is already well below the population. The quotations from Davies (2024) and McDonald (2017) on the management of growth and on employment are not connected to any result.
Why it matters: The policy relevance of the paper is the relation between where population is heading and what the land can carry. Without it, section 1.1 reads as background that does not earn its place.
What would resolve it: Either add a short analysis setting national and regional capacity (with ranges) against official projection series (low, medium, high, and their regional distribution where available, noting that regional projections are less reliable), including the date at which each threshold is crossed; or cut section 1.1 to what the paper uses. If projections are used, state their vintage and assumptions and acknowledge projection uncertainty (see the literature in item 10).

**9. The scale curve is largely a consequence of a model rule, and its bases are hard to follow.**
Where: sections 5 and 6, Table 3, Supplementary Section S2, abstract.
Problem: The supplement shows "capacity was exactly proportional to land", so any loss at finer scales comes entirely from the land mix in each unit interacting with the asymmetric substitution rule (cropland can carry pasture products; pasture cannot carry crops). The national test that capacity is unchanged "for every cropland share from 10% to 99%" sits oddly beside the cited thesis finding that "the share of cropping land [is] the most significant determinant of capacity" (Lane 2014, p. 3); the paper should reconcile them. Three different shares are quoted for the 53 regions in 2025 short term: 44% (state-yield), 63% (local yields) and 33% (national yield vector, S2). The random-grouping comparison rests on five draws. The abstract quotes "37%" without naming the basis; on the local basis it is 54%.
Why it matters: Readers will take the 37% as an empirical property of Australian land. It is a property of the model's fixed-proportion diet and its substitution rule applied to a partition, and its size depends heavily on which yield basis is used.
What would resolve it: State the mechanism in the abstract or qualify the number with its basis; test sensitivity to the substitution rule (for example, allowing some pasture to carry some crops, or allowing livestock to be fed from regions with surplus cropland); reconcile with Lane (2014, p. 3); use many more random draws (hundreds) if the comparison is kept; and consider reporting one basis throughout the main text with the others in the supplement.

**10. Missing literature and reliance on unpublished companion work.**
Where: sections 1, 8.1, 9, 11.2 and 11.6.
Problem: Several results are taken from "Paper B" or "the companion methods paper", which is not in the reference list and is not available to readers: the failure probability of 5.8 to 6.1% a year, the method choice, and the description and testing of the Monte Carlo. "CSIRO and University of Queensland 2025" is cited in section 1.1 but absent from the reference list. The paper engages little with the carrying capacity literature a Population and Environment reader would expect, beyond the author's own papers: for example Cohen (1995) on the many meanings of "how many people can the Earth support", Price (1999) and Seidl and Tisdell (1999) on the concept of carrying capacity, and the ecological footprint literature. It also cites no ABS population source at all, and no literature on population projection or small-area population estimation error (for example Wilson and Rees 2005), which bears directly on items 1 and 8.
What would resolve it: Make the paper self-contained on any result it reports (state the method and result, or cite a published or deposited preprint); add the missing references; add a short paragraph locating the paper in the carrying capacity debate; cite the ABS data series used.

**11. Presentation: the paper is long, dense with release codes, and its terminology obscures the results.**
Where: throughout.
Problem: Release identifiers (`2011-fixed-2`, `2025-mirror`, `2025-store-1`, `2025-organic`) and repository file paths appear in nearly every caption and many sentences. The status measure is called "people over capacity" but is a ratio of population to capacity ("people over capacity was 0.670"), which reads as a count. "Short term" and "long term" are presets with different diets and technologies, not time horizons. The paper answers five questions over twelve sections, and sections 10 to 12 mix results, literature summaries and policy suggestions. The figures were not available to me beyond their descriptions, so I could not assess them.
What would resolve it: Replace release codes in the main text with plain names (for example "2011, corrected method") defined once in a table; move file paths to the data availability statement; call the status measure "population-to-capacity ratio"; describe the presets as "current-conditions" and "100-year planning" scenarios or similar; consider whether sections 10 to 12 can be shortened substantially, with section 11 framed clearly as one-at-a-time model scenarios rather than as measures.

## 4. Minor issues

1. Section numbering skips 1.2 (1.1 is followed by 1.3).
2. Section 1.1: "a measure of employment, not population" is a confusing aside; either explain why McDonald (2017) is cited or drop it.
3. Section 1.1: the claim that the 2021 Intergenerational Report made "the first downward revision in such a report" needs its page reference to support that specific statement.
4. Section 2: "Status is reported as people over capacity, so a value above 1 is over capacity" should define the ratio algebraically.
5. Section 3 and Table 1: the long-term row shows 3 artefacts plus 2 not robust plus 1 clean for 4 flips; say in the caption that categories overlap (the caption says a flip can fail more than one test, but the arithmetic is still hard to follow). A per-region table of flips and test outcomes would be clearer.
6. Table 2: drop the "Interaction" row (zero by construction) and give the walk order in the caption.
7. Table 4: "National long-term capacity (million)" mixes two and three significant figures (15.35, 15.6). Use one precision throughout; the same applies to 1.5 against 1.49 and 1.50 in Table 6.
8. Table 6: the 2011 rows use `2011-fixed-2` in the caption, but section 3 says the national 2011 figure is from `2011-fixed`; state which supplies which column.
9. Section 5: give the ASGS edition and explain the unit counts (89 SA4s, 336 SA3s, 2,450 SA2s), which do not match the published counts for recent ASGS editions as I recall them; presumably some units were merged or excluded.
10. Section 6: "a diet of 2.5% meat and eggs fed 118.0 million" while 5% feeds 76.8 million (section 11.2) and 7.5% feeds 48.2 million (S6): the strong non-linearity deserves one sentence of explanation.
11. Section 8.1: "1 in 20 is not a threshold" and the failure probability rely on Paper B; see major issue 10.
12. Section 8.4: the export-inclusive count paragraph (22, 40 and 60 million) is hard to follow and the figures are from sources with no base year; consider moving it to the discussion or supplement.
13. Section 10: the opening says five regions were chosen "one of each type, from the largest movers", but Murray and Murrumbidgee-ACT are two regions, and the selection rule is not stated precisely enough to be reproduced.
14. Section 11.4: "The store is free in the model" is important and should be in the abstract's description of measures, since the abstract presents grain stores as a measure that widens the margin.
15. Section 11.6: the coincidence that Paper B's 15.5 million is a different quantity from this paper's 15.5 million invites confusion; rename or drop the cross-reference.
16. Section 12.4: "PDF p. 11" is not a citation form; use the printed page.

Reference list: entries cited in the text but missing (CSIRO and University of Queensland 2025); entries listed but, as far as I can find, not cited in the text or supplement (Ananda et al. 2023; Badami and Ramankutty 2015; Clinton et al. 2018; Grundy et al. 2016; Han et al. 2024; Langemeyer et al. 2021; Malau-Aduli et al. 2022; Mirosa et al. 2020; Payen et al. 2022; Productivity Commission 2011; Reutter et al. 2017; Rosa et al. 2020; Spang et al. 2019; Thompson and Penm 2011; Timmer 2024; Wiedemann et al. 2017; Wood et al. 2018).

Citation form mismatches: "Hughes et al. 2022" in the text against "2022a" in the list (no 2022b); "Grafton et al. 2018b" with no 2018a; "Roos et al. 2017" against "Röös"; "USDA undated" against "n.d."; "FAO food balance" against "FAOSTAT, 2025"; "DCCEEW" in the text against the full department name in the list.
19. Several reference entries are incomplete (no publisher, place, editors or initials), for example AUS-MEAT 2005, USDA n.d., Thompson and Penm 2011, and the book chapters; Bolleter (2018) carries a 2019 DOI and should be checked.
20. Data availability: the archive DOI is missing.
21. Units: write km² rather than "km2", and use thin spacing or consistent thousands separators in tables.

## 5. Questions to the author

1. What exactly are the 2011 and 2025 populations: which ABS series, reference date and release, and was the 2011 figure rebased after the 2016 or 2021 census?
2. How were populations allocated from SA2 to NRM regions in each year, and why does the 2011 method differ from the 2025 method? How large would the regional status changes be if both years used the same method?
3. How much of the national land effect (-2.6 million) survives if both years use the same land-use classification?
4. What livestock window was used for 2011, and where did it fall in the herd cycle? What is the 2011 to 2025 change if both years use windows of the same cycle phase?
5. What do the layer effects look like under a Shapley (all-orderings average) decomposition?
6. What does the "consumption" layer change: per-person intakes, the data source, or the treatment of waste? Did Australians' measured food requirements fall between the two years?
7. Do the 1-in-10 and 1-in-20 national findings hold at the 5th and 95th percentiles of the 2025 range?
8. Is there any evidence that pasture-fed output falls in drought years by the cereal-based factor?
9. Why is the corrected tier, rather than the improved tier, the reporting basis, given that the improved tier is described as an improvement?
10. When, on the official medium projection, does the population reach the 1-in-10 short-term capacity and the average-year short-term capacity, and how do regional projections change the count of regions over capacity?
11. Can the 2011 inputs be sampled with the same Monte Carlo, so that the change has a range?

## 6. The abstract and highlights

The abstract states the main national figures accurately as computed (I checked the 1.49 and 1.50 ratios, the 69% and 57% shares and the 1-in-20 status), but it states more than the evidence supports in four places. First, "Livestock, consumption and the red meat mix of the diet drove the rise" is the answer of one ordering of a sequential decomposition with no allowance for the herd cycle (major issues 3 and 4). Second, "it is above balance in a 1-in-10 year and below it in a 1-in-20 year" is given without the 2025 range, which on a rough reading spans balance at both return periods, and it rests on an untested cereal-based factor applied to livestock (major issues 5 and 6). Third, "their capacities sum to 37%" omits its yield basis; on each unit's own yields the figure is 54% (major issue 9). Fourth, "The closed region is therefore a bound, not a policy goal" does not follow from the 37% figure: it is a conceptual point the paper argues from literature in section 12.1. The abstract also presents measures that "widen the margin" without saying they are one-at-a-time, cost-free model scenarios. The abstract should name the basis of the population figures and say that the 2011 results carry no uncertainty range.

The highlights compress these problems further. "Livestock, diet and consumption drove the rise; population changes status only" restates an order-dependent attribution as a finding, and the second clause is true by construction of the model rather than a result. "Capacity is above the population in a 1-in-10 year and below it in a 1-in-20 year" omits that this is national, short-term and within the range of the method choices. The first highlight should say "on the corrected method" and define capacity as people fed from Australia's own land on a stated diet. The highlights should also carry at least one regional result, since the regional analysis is the paper's main addition to the earlier work.
