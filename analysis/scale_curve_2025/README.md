# Scale curve (section of the results paper; Murray 2026-11-02: run it on 2011 and 2025 data and include it generally)

Long-term shares corrected 2026-11-07: units had been evaluated under the national climate zone; each unit takes the region record, and so the climate zone, of the zone whose yields it uses (its state on the state basis), and the state level equals the sum of the release's own state capacities in every cell.

How capacity falls as the unit of self-sufficiency shrinks: the sum of capacities of units of a given size, each feeding itself from its own land, as a share of the national capacity. `web/scripts/make-scale-curve.mjs` writes `results/scale_curve.csv` for the six cells of the comparison grid (`npm run results`); `plot_scale_curve.py` draws the figure (`scale_curve.png` and `.svg`; `--log` spaces the levels by the logarithm of the number of units; `--local` draws the local-yield basis). Corrected method, share of national capacity:

| Level | Units | 2011 short | 2011 long | 2025 short | 2025 long | 2025 short, local yields | 2025 long, local yields |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Australia | 1 | 100% | 100% | 100% | 100% | 100% | 100% |
| State | 7 | 77% | 44% | 75% | 49% | 75% | 49% |
| NRM region | 53 | 44% | 20% | 44% | 23% | 63% | 38% |
| SA4 | 89 | 60% | 32% | 58% | 35% | 80% | 52% |
| SA3 | 336 | 46% | 23% | 44% | 24% | 64% | 40% |
| SA2 | 2,450 | 40% | 18% | 37% | 20% | 54% | 32% |

The first four columns are the figure of the paper (**state yield basis**: every unit takes the yields, the climate zone and the irrigation and nature-reserve settings of its state's zone). The last two are the sensitivity (**local basis**: a unit takes the yields, climate zone and settings of its own NRM zone, or of the NRM zone holding most of its cropland for SA units).

## Reading it

* **The loss is large at every scale and monotone along the administrative levels.** On the 2025 data the states sum to 75% of the national short-term capacity, SA4 to 58%, SA3 to 44% and SA2 to 37%; the long term falls further (49%, 35%, 24%, 20%); in the long term the states alone keep only half the national capacity (7.7 against 15.6 million, the release's own state results). The same shape holds in 2011 (77%, 60%, 46%, 40% short term), so the 2015 finding (national 40, states 28, regions 23 million) survives the corrections and extends to SA2.
* **The NRM regions (catchments) lose as much as the 336 SA3s with only 53 units** (44% against 44%, short term, 2025), and far more than the 89 SA4s (58%). Why: see "Why the NRM regions do so badly" below. (An earlier note blamed NRM catchments for cutting cities from their hinterlands; that was a guess and is wrong: the model's capacity does not depend on population at all, only on a unit's land, yields and settings.)
* **Why an earlier version rose at SA4 (an artefact, now explained).** The first figure gave each SA unit the yields of its dominant NRM zone but used the state's own (state-wide average) yields at the state level, so the SA4 level, whose units are larger than most zones, got better yields than the states and rose from 75% to 80%. Tested on 2025-fixed (`yield_assignment_test.mjs`): with the parent state's yields at every level the curve is 75, 58, 44, 37%; with the national yield vector 76, 54, 35, 28%; with each unit's dominant-zone yields, even the states exceed the nation (109%). A whole state does not have the yields of its best zone, so that basis overstates large units and is a sensitivity only.
* **Yield differences within states are worth about 17 points at SA2 level (37% to 54%, short term).** That is the gap between the two bases and is a bound: the state basis ignores local yield differences (conservative) and the dominant-zone basis gives each SA unit the yields of one zone (generous for units that span several). The truth for SA2 lies between.
* **The sum of capacities is not guaranteed monotone.** The ASGS levels are nested (SA2 within SA3 within SA4 within the state), but the model's capacity is not additive: it depends on the unit's land mix and its yield vector, so only a consistent yield basis gives a clean curve.

## Method and limits

* Zone levels (Australia, state) use the release's own zones; the NRM level uses the NRM zones (own yields on the local basis, the parent state's on the state basis). SA4, SA3 and SA2 use land by the 2011 method on 2020-21 data (`data/sa2/land_2011_method_by_sa2.csv`, which sums to the national zone's land) and the ERP of the release's vintage year. The SA group has its own national reference ("Australia (SA land)"), equal to the national capacity for the 2025 cells; for the 2011 cells the 2020-21 land differs from the 2011 land (30.7 against 33.3 million short term), so the 2011 SA shares are against their own reference and are not exactly comparable with the zone levels (there is no 2011 land by SA2).
* In `results/scale_curve.csv`, the 2011-corrected State rows carry the population of the sum of the seven states (20,686,860) where the Australia and NRM rows carry the national 22,326,388; capacities are unaffected; only the over-capacity count is. The release is frozen, so this is a note only.
* Units are administrative (ABS) boundaries, so scale and boundary effects are together; cities are units with almost no land, which is the point, but they dominate the SA2 level (2,136 to 2,311 of 2,450 SA2s are over capacity across the six cells, both terms and both yield bases). The other settings (diet, waste, resilience) are the national defaults in every unit, as in the 2015 paper.
* Rejected on the way: the national yield vector for every unit (understates the regional sum, 10.8 against 21.0 million on the 2011 zones) and the cropland-weighted mean of the zone yield vectors (inflates the yields of crops grown in a few zones; the national unit reached 77 million, not 33).

## Why the NRM regions do so badly (diagnosis, 2025-fixed, short term; `nrm_vs_sa_diagnosis.mjs`)

* **It is the partition, not the data.** Rebuilding the NRM regions from whole SA2s gives the same 44% as the NRM zones as built (so no inconsistency between the zone land and the SA2 land).
* **The gap is concentrated where rangeland meets cropland.** By state, the NRM zones against the SA4s (share of national capacity): NSW 17.6 against 25.3 (the state itself 25.5), SA 1.2 against 4.8 (state 5.0), WA 2.0 against 5.1 (state 7.5). Victoria, Queensland, Tasmania and the Northern Territory are about equal.
* **The mechanism is complementarity of crop and pasture land.** The model's diet needs crops and pasture-fed food in roughly fixed proportion, so a unit's capacity is set by whichever land type runs short. Rangeland (pasture with almost no cropping land) cannot feed anyone on its own: South Australia's Arid Lands NRM zone has 326,000 km2 of pasture and 221 km2 of cropping and a capacity of 41 thousand people. Paired with cropping land the same pasture is worth far more: SA4 406 (which, by its land, joins that arid country with the Eyre Peninsula zone's 17,221 km2 of cropping; SA4 406 has 17,587 km2 of cropping in all) has a capacity of 1.7 million, against 41 thousand for the arid zone plus 132 thousand for the Eyre Peninsula zone (which has the cropping but runs out of pasture). In Western Australia the Rangelands zone (646,000 km2 of pasture, 937 km2 of cropping) has a capacity of 241 thousand; SA4 511, which joins 356,000 km2 of pasture with 26,400 km2 of cropping, has 1.5 million.
* **Stranded pasture.** Count the units with more than 1,000 km2 of pasture and cropping land under 2% of that pasture (the floor leaves out the near-empty city units): in the NRM zones eight such units hold 71% of the nation's pasture, in the SA3s 18 units hold 72%, in the SA4s three units hold 51%. The SA4 outback units pair about 20 points more of the nation's pasture with cropland. The unused land (excess) the partitions leave: national 1.96 million km2, SA4s 3.67, NRM zones 4.50.
* **So the finding is about pairing rangeland with crops.** Nearly all of Australia's pasture is rangeland, and its value to the model depends on whether the unit that holds it also holds cropland. A catchment boundary separates the arid pastoral country from the cropping districts on its edge; the ABS outback SA4s do not. The same is why the thesis found that the cropping land proportion is the most significant determinant of capacity.
* **Limits.** One release and preset; yields are the parent state's. The NRM-against-SA4 gap is a property of these two partitions and of the model's fixed diet proportions: it is nearly as large in the long term (12 points: NRM 23%, SA4 35%, state basis, 2025, against 14 points short term), although that diet has only 7.5% meat and eggs; whether a still less pasture-dependent diet would close it has not been tested.

## How good could a split be? (`ideal_split_test.mjs`, 2025-fixed, short term, national yield vector for every unit)

* **Capacity is exactly proportional to land** (0.5, 0.1 and 0.01 times the national land give 100.0% of 0.5, 0.1 and 0.01 times the national capacity). So any split in which every unit has the national land mix sums to 100%: the loss from a split is entirely the mismatch of crop and pasture land between units, and the best possible split, with no geography, loses nothing.
* **Random groupings of the SA2s into K groups** (five draws each): K=7 about 100%, K=53 68 to 81%, K=89 56 to 69%, K=336 34 to 44%. Against these, the real partitions on the same basis: NRM zones (53 units) **33%**, SA4 (89) 54%, SA3 (336) 35%, SA2 (2,450) 28%. The NRM zones are far worse than a random grouping of the same size; the SA4s are slightly worse; the SA3s are about equal to random.
* **Reading.** Grouping like with like geographically (rangeland with rangeland, cropping district with cropping district) is what costs capacity, and the NRM catchments do it most. At fine scales (SA3, SA2) the loss comes instead from indivisible lumps: a few hundred huge pastoral SA2s are pasture-only whatever they are grouped with, so a random grouping does no better than the real SA3s.
* **An ideal contiguous split** (merge adjacent SA2s into K regions to maximise the sum of capacities) can be found by region-growing or annealing; it needs to know which SA2s touch, and the repository holds that in `data/sa2/adjacency.csv`. The search has been run (`analysis/regionalise_2025/`, results in `analysis/regionalise_2025/summary.csv`): at 53 regions with each region capped at 650,000 km2 of land, the best contiguous split found keeps 76% of the national capacity in the short term (75.7%) and 69% in the long term (69.4%) (claim A124; other caps and region counts A347 to A353). The search is a heuristic, so these are lower bounds on the true best split. The unconstrained ideal (100%) and the random result (68 to 81% at K=53) remain the references. It overlaps the display-region design (`SCOPE.md` B0a: a population centre with its productive hinterland).

## Would smaller units find more cropland, and adapt? (`urban_gardens_test.mjs`, 2025-fixed, short term, state yields; Murray 2026-11-03)

The scale curve holds land use and diet at today's values in every unit, so it is a conservative picture of forced local self-sufficiency. Two adaptations tested:

* **Gardens.** A share of a unit's infrastructure land (the urban land of the 2011 method: houses, gardens, roads) turned to cropping at the yields of the unit's state. The 33 metropolitan SA4s (more than 300 people a km2) hold 14.7 million people, 10,700 km2 of the nation's 20,150 km2 of infrastructure land and only 563 km2 of cropping. Turning half of that urban land to crops (about 5,400 km2, 1.7% of national cropland) lets them feed 171 thousand people on today's diet (1.2% of their population) or 408 thousand on a 2.5% meat-and-egg diet (2.8%); the national capacity rises 0.3% and 3.2%. Cities hold about 730 m2 of such land a person against about 3,000 m2 of cropland (and 71,000 m2 of pasture) a person needed on today's diet. To feed the cities from half their urban land on the low-meat diet the yields would have to be about 35 times the commercial yields used here (intensive garden yields are higher than commercial ones, but not tested).
* **Diet.** On a 2.5% meat-and-egg diet the sums at fine scale rise a great deal because the pasture that strands at fine scale matters less. Share of the national capacity on today's diet (41.5 million): states 75% today, 179% low-meat (185% with half the urban land as gardens); SA4 58%, 136% (139%); SA3 44%, 108% (111%); SA2 37%, 95% (97%). So at the finest scale a low-meat diet recovers nearly all that the smaller unit lost, while gardens add about two points. Against the national capacity on the same low-meat diet (118 million) the SA2 sum is still about a third, so the scale effect remains large in relative terms.
* **Reading.** The adaptation that matters for forced local self-sufficiency is diet, not backyard cropland; gardens are a small, local contribution. Land that a small community could re-purpose beyond gardens (lawns, parks, sports grounds, road verges, peri-urban land) is not separately classified in the land-use data and so is not tested.

## Could small-scale fruit and vegetable growing cover part of the need? (`food_land_by_category.py`, 2025-fixed, today's diet)

The model's land need is dominated by animal products and their feed, not by fruit and vegetables. Per person per year, of 0.33 ha of cropland: **white meat and eggs (poultry and pig feed) 1,610 m2 (49%)**, fats and oils 506 m2 (15%), cereals 433 m2 (13%), red meat's feed crops 279 m2 (8%), and **fruit and non-starchy vegetables 246 m2 (7.4%) for 330 g a day**; the pasture (7.96 ha) is 99% red meat and the rest dairy. Cities (the metropolitan SA4s) hold about 730 m2 of urban land a person.

* So a garden share of the urban land of 10%, 25% or 50% (73, 183, 365 m2 a person) would at commercial yields cover about 30%, 74% or 148% of a person's fruit and vegetable land; fruit and vegetables are the one major food group whose land need is small enough to be met on urban land. (The garden share of urban land is a scenario, not a measured figure.)
* But covering all of it would free only about 7% of the cropland a person needs, so it moves the capacity little (the garden test in this README: half the urban land as crops lifts the metropolitan SA4s from 0.3% to 1.2% of their population on today's diet). Grains (13%), oils (15%) and animal products (the rest) are the bulk of the land and are not back-yard crops.
* Small-scale fruit and vegetable growing is therefore plausible and nutritionally useful, and a modest land saving; it does not change the scale effect, which is driven by the pasture and the animal-feed cropland.

## To what extent can diet improve it? (`diet_scale_test.mjs`, `diet_scale.csv`, 2025-fixed, short term, state yields)

Sum of capacities at each level, as a share of the national capacity on today's diet (41.5 million), with the meat-and-egg share set at every unit (red meat 57.8% of the meat), and with every unit choosing its best diet from a grid (meat and eggs 0 to 15%, red meat 0 to 100% of the meat):

| Level | Units | 0% meat and eggs | 2.5% | 5% | 7.5% | 12% (today) | Each unit's best diet |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Australia | 1 | 271% | 284% | 185% | 116% | 100% | 284% |
| States | 7 | 177% | 179% | 113% | 81% | 75% | 187% |
| SA4 | 89 | 158% | 136% | 86% | 65% | 58% | 165% |
| SA3 | 336 | 132% | 108% | 67% | 48% | 44% | 138% |
| SA2 | 2,450 | 121% | 95% | 56% | 41% | 37% | 126% |

* **Diet matters more than scale.** The finest units on a plant-based diet (121%) feed more than the nation does on today's diet (100%); a 2.5% meat-and-egg diet at every SA3 is worth about the same (108%). Moving from today's diet to a 2.5% diet multiplies capacity by 2.8 nationally and 2.6 at SA2; to no meat or eggs by 2.7 and 3.3.
* **The relative loss from small scale is not removed.** Against the national capacity on the same diet the SA2 sum is 37% today, 33% at 2.5% meat and eggs, 45% with none, 44% when each unit picks its best diet.
* **The best diet changes with scale.** The best uniform meat share is 2.5% for the nation and the states and 0% from SA4 down (SA4: 158% against 136%): small units cannot pair their pasture with cropland, so the less they depend on pasture the better. Letting each unit choose adds about five points over a uniform 0% at SA2 (126% against 121%) because some pasture-rich units gain by keeping meat.
* **Limits.** The diets are the NHMRC food-modelling diets of the model's Diet sheet (adequate by the NHMRC's reference values), changed only in meat and egg share and red-meat share; the nutrition-limited optimisation (`analysis/nutrition_lp_2025`) was not run at fine scale. A large shift in diet is a behavioural change the model does not price. Within-group swaps that keep the food groups (variant A of the nutrition model) raise the national capacity by about half (45.7 to 69 million, improved method), a smaller and more realistic change than these.

## How the best diet uses cropland and pasture (`diet_binding_test.mjs`, 2025-fixed, short term)

* **The rule of the model.** A person's diet needs cropland (plant foods, oils, and the feed of poultry, pigs and eggs: white meat 1,610 m2 a year, red meat only 279 m2) and pasture (red meat 67,200 m2 a year, dairy 12,400 m2). Capacity is the population at which the land runs out. Cropland can graze animals when pasture falls short; pasture cannot grow crops. At today's national diet the pasture need (3.49 million km2) is more than the pasture held (3.32 million km2), so about 175,000 km2 of cropland grazes animals and only 141,000 km2 grows food crops.
* **The nation's best diet fills both.** Over meat and eggs 0 to 15% and red meat 0 to 100% of the meat, the best is **118.4 million people at 3.0% meat and eggs, all of it red meat, using 100% of the cropland and 92% of the pasture** (41.5 million today). Red meat replaces plant protein that would need cropland and uses pasture that has no other use, so capacity rises as meat is added until the pasture is used up; beyond that cropland is drawn into grazing and capacity falls steeply (2.5% meat and eggs 118 million, 4% 101 million, 5% 77 million).
* **A rangeland unit cannot use its pasture.** SA Arid Lands (326,000 km2 of pasture, 221 km2 of cropping): cropland binds at every diet. The best diet (15% meat and eggs, all red) gives 75 thousand people against 41 thousand today and still uses only 7% of the pasture (23,500 of 326,000 km2), because each person must also eat at least 85% non-meat foods, which need cropland. Pasture can be turned into people only up to the diet's meat limit; more pasture than that feeds no one unless the meat is exported, which the model, self-sufficient by design, does not allow.
* **A cropping unit is limited by the pasture its dairy and meat need.** Eyre Peninsula (17,200 km2 of cropping, 10,600 of pasture): pasture binds even with no meat, because dairy is pasture-fed and fixed in the diet family; capacity 375 thousand at 0% meat against 132 thousand today, and most of its cropland grazes animals. A diet with less dairy than the NHMRC family would be needed to use its cropland for crops (not in the grid).
* **So the best diet is the one at which the land of both types is just used up:** the cropland-based foods set to what the cropland supports, and the pasture-fed foods (red meat first) added until the pasture is exhausted, up to the diet's meat limit. The units differ in which end of that range they sit at: rangelands at the meat limit with pasture to spare, cropping districts with no meat and still short of pasture.

## Is pasture quality in the model? (`dairy_pasture_test.mjs`, 2025-fixed, short term, state yields; Murray 2026-11-03)

* **Only through regional averages.** The model has one pasture land type. Each animal product (sheep meat, wool, dairy milk, cattle meat) has a yield per m2 of pasture for each zone, found from the ABS numbers of animals and production by dividing the zone's total grazing land among its animals by Dry Sheep Equivalent (thesis, yield method step 10, p. 183). So within a zone dairy cattle and beef cattle are credited with the same average pasture; dairy gets a share of all the pasture, rangeland included, in proportion to its feed need, not the good (sown, irrigated or arable) pasture it actually occupies. Quality shows only where a zone's average is high (a dairy district has a high milk yield per m2). The ABS splits grazing land into improved pasture (45.2 of 331.9 million ha, 14%) and other land, but the model combines them (thesis p. 186). The one asymmetry the model has is that cropland can graze animals when pasture falls short (at the pasture's average yield, not the higher yield of sown pasture).
* **What this does to the results.** Dairy is about 12,400 m2 of the roughly 80,000 m2 of pasture a person needs a year, and in cropping districts dairy is what keeps pasture the binding land type even with no meat. Multiplying the dairy milk yield in every unit by 3 and by 5 (a stand-in for dairy on better land) gives, as a share of the national capacity on today's diet: the nation +11% and +13%; on a 2.5% meat-and-egg diet the nation +3% and +4%, but the SA4s from 136% to 168% and 172% (+24 to +26%) and the SA2s from 95% to 117% and 122% (+23 to +28%). Today's diet SA4 58% to 63% and 64%, SA2 37% to 40% and 41%.
* **Reading.** Treating dairy as grazing on average pasture is conservative for the cropping districts and the fine scales, where good-quality pasture and arable land are interchangeable, and it is the main reason cropping districts look pasture-limited even without meat; it matters little to the national low-meat optimum, where cropland binds. The reverse error exists for rangelands, which are credited with dairy at the zone's or state's average yield though dairy could not run on their poor pasture; that effect is not tested here. A land-quality split (improved against other pasture, with dairy confined to the improved share) would need the ABS improved-pasture area by SA2 and is not built.
