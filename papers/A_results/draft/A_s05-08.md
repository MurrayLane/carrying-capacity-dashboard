## 5. Self-sufficiency by unit: the partition effect

This section asks how much of the national capacity is left when every unit feeds itself from its own land: the sum of the units' capacities as a share of the national capacity, the scale curve of Lane et al. (2015) extended to SA2. It was computed for 7 states, 53 regions, 89 SA4s, 336 SA3s and 2,450 SA2s. Every unit kept the national settings for diet and waste. The curve in Figure 4 is on the state-yield basis: every unit took the yields, climate zone and settings of its state. This is the conservative bound and the only basis used for the curve in the main text; section 5.4 gives the other bound in one sentence. The loss is a partition effect, not an effect of size. It follows from the model's substitution rule (section 6): cropland can carry pasture-fed products but pasture cannot carry crops, so a unit whose land mix differs from the nation's loses capacity to the sum.

### 5.1 The curve and its stability

On the 2025 data the states summed to 75% of the national short-term capacity, the regions to 44%, the SA4s to 58%, the SA3s to 44% and the SA2s to 37% (Table 4). On 2011 data the states gave 77%, the regions 44%, the SA4s 60%, the SA3s 46% and the SA2s 40%. The long-term curve fell further at every level, to 20% for the SA2s in 2025 (Table 4).

[Figure 4: the scale curve, the sum of capacities as a share of national capacity at each level from the nation to SA2, 2011 and 2025, short and long term, state-yield basis]

The finding of Lane et al. (2015) that capacity falls as the unit shrinks (section 1.2) held on the corrected method. On 2011 data the national short-term capacity was 33.3 million, the states summed to 25.7 million and the 53 regions to 14.7 million, and the curve extends the finding to SA2.

### 5.2 The boundary effect and its cause

The 53 regions lost as much as the 336 SA3s, 44% against 44% in the short term in 2025. They lost far more than the 89 SA4s, which kept 58%. Rebuilding the regions from whole SA2s gave the same 44%, so the gap is a property of the partition, not of the land data. The gap lay where rangeland meets cropland, in New South Wales, South Australia and Western Australia (Supplementary Section S2).

Section 6 gives the cause. South Australia's Arid Lands region holds 326,000 km2 of pasture and 221 km2 of cropland. Its capacity was 41 thousand people on the state-yield basis (28,609 on its own yields, Table 8), far less than SA4 406, which joins that arid country with the cropland of the Eyre Peninsula.

### 5.3 How much a better drawing of regions keeps

Capacity is exactly proportional to land, so a split in which every unit has the national land mix loses nothing. In 200 random groupings of the 53 regions into seven groups, on the national yield vector (which removes the yield assignment of the state basis), the median sum is 91% of national capacity. The seven states keep 76% and the 53 regions 33% on the same basis, and the states keep less than 179 to 182 of the 200 random groupings. An ideal contiguous split bounds how much a better drawing of regions would keep (Supplementary Section S2). The best contiguous split found at 53 regions kept 76% of national capacity in the short term and 69% in the long term. The search was heuristic, so these are lower bounds on the best split.

### 5.4 Reading the curve

The curve is read for its shape, not for any single administrative SA2. For the 2011 cells the SA levels used land of 2020-21, against a reference of 30.7 million rather than the 33.3 million of the 2011 zones, so the 2011 SA shares are not exactly comparable with the zone levels (marked in Table 4). The other bound lets each unit keep its own yields: the 2025 short-term share is then 75% for the states and 54% for the SA2s, so yield differences within states are worth about 17 points at SA2 level, and the truth for SA2s lies between 37% and 54% (Supplementary Table S6). Letting dairy use cropland at observed dairy-farm yields moves the SA2 share from 37% to 40% of the unchanged national figure (Supplementary Section S2). The regions' sums in sections 7, 8 and 11 are on own yields, which is why they differ from the curve's 44%.

## 6. The mechanism

One rule drives most of these results. A diet in the model needs cropland and pasture in nearly fixed proportion, so a unit's capacity is set by whichever land type runs short. Cropland can carry pasture-fed products when pasture falls short, but pasture is not necessarily fit for crops (Lane 2014, p. 266), and in the model it cannot carry them. Lane (2014, p. 3) found the share of cropping land to be the most significant determinant of capacity; that holds where cropland is scarce, as in a rangeland region such as SA Arid Lands, with 221 km2 of cropland, where cropland decides capacity (section 5). Nationally the diet needs only 141,000 km2 of food cropland (below), so cropland binds only at a share well below the 10% at which the national test starts; in that test, on the 2025 corrected method, capacity was unchanged for every cropland share from 10% to 99% of the combined area. The test was run for Australia only, so the rule is not shown for regions.

On the reference diet of the 2025 corrected method, pasture binds nationally. The diet needs 3.49 million km2 of pasture against 3.32 million km2 held. About 175,000 km2 of cropland therefore grazes animals, and 141,000 km2 grows food crops. Cropland that grazes animals is credited square metre for square metre at pasture's own output, and pasture never grows crops. This is why measures aimed at field crops barely move the 2025 short term while livestock output does (section 11). Field-crop yields 24% lower took 0.2% off the short term, against 26% off at a 1-in-10 drought, which scales every yield.

Short-term capacity therefore reads as a statement of how far meat production covers meat eaten, at the herd and diet of each year. Meat production over meat eaten as food, all meats at FAO's per-person food supply, was 1.57 in 2021-23 against the model's capacity over population of 1.50, and 1.51 in 2010-12 against 1.49. This is a consistency check, not a validation of pasture-fed food: the ratio that matches is all meat, including pork and poultry, and the agreement is of ratios, not of counts of people (at the model's own meat requirement the same production feeds 33 and 28 million people against the model's 41.5 and 33.3 million). It does not test cropland, regions or the split between red meat and dairy.

Grain-fed cattle output is in the cattle-meat numerator, and the land that grows the feedlot grain and the feedlot's own land are in its denominator with the beef share of grazing land, so grain-fed output is not credited to pasture alone. Because idle cropland is ceded to pasture where pasture binds, moving that land to the cropland account would leave the pooled national land need unchanged; this is argued from the land rule, not computed, and no regional test has been run.

The model has one pasture land type, and within a zone dairy and beef cattle are credited with the same average pasture yield. The dairy-on-good-land improvement moves dairy's land to the cropping column, at the observed yield of dairy farms, and it moves regions both ways (section 9). Livestock on land unsuited to crops use land that crops cannot (Poore and Nemecek 2018, p. 990; van Zanten et al. 2016; Röös et al. 2017).

## 7. The long term: a self-sufficiency preset

The long-term preset follows Lane (2014) and Lane (2017), who called it a 100-year plan; it is one scenario, not a time path. It plans for the worst year in 100 of weather, through the cereal factor of section 2. It sets a low-meat diet, with meat and eggs at 7.5% of the diet against 12% in the short term. It assumes no avoidable waste, full recycling of food waste, organic farming and liquid fuel cut from 2,360 to 210 litres a person. It also sets activity level 3, three fibre settings, timber, infrastructure area and a 30% reserve. The land for the remaining fuel rests on a biodiesel yield of 400 litres per tonne of oilseeds (Geoscience Australia and ABARE 2010, p. 331).

### 7.1 National capacity in both years

On the corrected method the long-term capacity was 15.35 million on 2011 data and 15.6 million on 2025 data (Table 5). The preset covers 69% of the 2011 population and 57% of the 2025 population. Capacity rose by 0.27 million while the population grew by 5.3 million. The long-term change is not established: 26% of the paired draws of section 9 show a fall. The weather and fuel settings set most of the figure (section 7.2). With the weather factor of a 1-in-20 year in place of 1 in 100, the 2025 figure would be 21.7 million, 79% of the population: the long-term capacity without the weather allowance, 34.3 million, times the factor 0.634, since capacity is linear in the factor.

### 7.3 The regions

The cropping belts and inland plains feed themselves in the long term: in 2025 the strongest, South Coast WA, Avon and four others, had four to eight times their population in long-term capacity. Northern Gulf (which carries a land-definition flag), South West Qld and Lachlan also fed themselves. ## 8. Shocks and food security by region

The weather allowance of section 2 scales every yield, crops, pasture products and livestock alike, by a factor taken from wheat, oats and barley yields. The 53 regions each take their state's factor. Regions of one state therefore share a factor, but they do not all tip together: the five regions that tip at 1 in 10 in 2025 are in three states.

### 8.1 The national margin

National short-term capacity was about 1.5 times the population in an average year in both years (Table 7; section 3), 1.11 and 1.12 times in a 1-in-10 year and 0.68 and 0.69 times in a 1-in-100 year. In a 1-in-20 year capacity was below the population: 0.95 times in 2025, and in 2011 0.89 on the thesis weather table but 0.95 on the 2025 table. On one table the two years are nearly the same, so almost all of the apparent improvement from 0.89 to 0.95 is the table, not the data; the 1-in-10 margin is the same on either table. At the base settings the margin is above 1 at 1 in 10 and below 1 at 1 in 20 in both data years, but across the spread of settings both outcomes lie inside the range. The 5th to 95th percentile of 2025 capacity on the 19 choices of section 9 is 23.1 to 36.5 million at 1 in 10 (central 30.8; 0.84 to 1.32 times the population) and 19.7 to 31.2 million at 1 in 20 (central 26.3; 0.71 to 1.13 times). The population of 27.6 million lies inside both: 37% of the draws are below it at 1 in 10 and 78% at 1 in 20.

The factor itself depends on the record. Starting the cereal record in 1950-51 or 1970-71, or weighting the three cereals by area, gives a 1-in-10 capacity of 29.2 to 31.6 million, above the population in every variant, and a 1-in-20 capacity below it in every variant with enough seasons to support it (Supplementary Table S13). At the 2025 population the failure probability is 2.3% to 19.4% a year (5th to 95th percentile of the 2,000 widened draws, median 7.6%; linear in the return period between the weather table's anchors at 1, 3, 5, 10, 20, 30, 50 and 150 years; Supplementary Section S8).

Held at 2025 data, capacity of 41.5 million in an average year is not reached by either official baseline within its printed years (40.5 million in 2062-63 in the Intergenerational Report, 41.0 million in 2065-66 in the Population Statement). On straight lines between printed points their last growth reaches it in 2066 and 2068, and both baselines pass the 1-in-10 capacity of 30.8 million in 2034. The 2025 population is 1.8 times the long-term capacity of 15.6 million and the 2062-63 projection 2.6 times it. Regional projections are out of scope: no official series is published for these 53 regions.

The allowance is a cereal factor (section 2). It has not been tested on pasture-fed output, and almost all (about 97%) of the short-term drought effect at 1 in 10 falls on pasture-fed food. Applied to crops and grain-fed animals alone, with pasture-fed output at its average-year level, national short-term capacity is 41.2 million at 1 in 10 against 30.8 million with the factor on all output, and long-term capacity at 1 in 100 is 24.7 million against 15.6 million. The two bounds are given equal weight.

### 8.2 The regions by return period

On own yields, about 82% of the population of the 53 regions lived in regions over capacity even in an average year in 2011, and 81% in 2025 (Table 7). The count of regions over capacity rose from 16 in an average year to 21 at 1 in 10 in 2025, and from 22 to 25 in 2011. Taking each region at its own 95th or 5th percentile of the 19-choice range, which no single draw reaches, gives 19 and 26 regions over capacity at 1 in 10; in a single draw the number is 21 to 24 (median 23). Seven regions have their population inside their range at 1 in 10 and four at 1 in 20 (Supplementary Section S4 names them).

In 2025 five regions were under capacity in an average year and over it at 1 in 10: Wet Tropics, North East Victoria, Rangelands, Mackay Whitsunday and Burnett Mary. In 2011 three were, and at 1 in 20 four, on either weather table. Wet Tropics, Rangelands and Mackay Whitsunday flipped between 2011 and 2025, but their 2025 average-year status does not hold across the 19-choice range; nor does North East Victoria's, and Burnett Mary's does.

**What the bad-year results can bear.** The 1-in-20 year is not a threshold. The rise from three tipping regions to five is not a finding on its own, because the flip tests were not applied to the 1-in-10 status itself and seven regions lie inside their range at 1 in 10.

Regional self-sufficiency fails sooner than national: on own yields the regions together held 63% of national capacity in an average year in 2025 and 55% at 1 in 10 (16.9 million against 27.6 million people). The 44% of section 5 is on the state-yield basis, a different measure.

### 8.3 Named scenarios

The named scenarios were run on 2025 data, against a reference of 41.5 million in the short term and 15.6 million in the long term (Supplementary Section S3 and Table S2). Waste at 6% (Supplementary Section S6) raised the short term by 16% and lowered the long term by 8.2%, because the long-term settings assume no avoidable waste. A store of one year of grain raised the long term by 48% and a store of two years by 64%, with no short-term effect: more than any other named scenario in the long term, but less than the weather allowance itself (section 7).

### 8.4 What a single-year shock does not describe

A single-year shock does not describe a drought of several years. The 2017 to 2019 drought was exceptional as a three-year deficit (section 1.4), while its single years mostly fell within the expected range (Falster et al. 2024, pp. 6, 9). Bad wheat years share climate drivers: ENSO and the Indian Ocean Dipole together explain 30 to 41% of wheat production variability across Australia and East Africa, a figure the source does not give for Australia alone (Anderson et al. 2019), so a widespread drought can hit several states at once. An export-inclusive count sits beside the model's (Supplementary Section S9).
