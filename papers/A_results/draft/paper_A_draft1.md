First draft, assembled 2026-11-13 from A_s01-04.md, A_s05-08.md, A_s09-12.md. Every number traces to the claims csv beside each section file; sentences marked [gate 4] in those csvs use values awaiting Murray's gate 4 approval. Not for circulation.

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 Australian population debate and planning

How many people Australia can plan to feed from its own land is an old question. It returned as the population grew between 2011 and 2025. The government's framing of that growth is set by the Intergenerational Reports and the Population Statement. The 2021 report projected 38.8 million people in 2060-61, the first downward revision of the projection in an intergenerational report (Treasury 2021, p. 13). The 2023 report projected growth from 26.5 million people in 2022-23 to 40.5 million in 2062-63 (Treasury 2023, p. 39). It put average growth over the next 40 years at 1.1% a year, against 1.4% over the past 40 years (Treasury 2023, p. 37). The 2025 Population Statement put the population at 27.5 million in the March quarter of 2025 and forecast 41 million by 2065-66 (Centre for Population 2025, p. 1).

Davies (2024) sets out both readings of this growth. Growth of 5 million over two decades "has been described as unexpected, unprecedented, and unplanned" (Davies 2024, p. 149). In Davies's own account, "Australia's population growth is highly managed" (Davies 2024, p. 150). Parr (2018, p. 16) notes that growth concentrated in Sydney, Melbourne and Brisbane. McDonald (2017, p. 3) finds that most of the rise in employment from July 2011 to July 2016 came from immigrants; his measure is employment, not population.

This paper sets capacity against each data year's own population. That population was 22.3 million in 2011 and 27.6 million in 2025, a rise of about 24%. Published estimates of how many people Australia can feed scatter widely, because they answer different questions by different methods (Henry and Godden 1995; Foran and Poldy 2002; CSIRO and University of Queensland 2025). A companion methods paper compares those methods.

### 1.4 Food security in Australian planning and policy

Food security and regional food self-sufficiency appear in Australian planning and policy only in patches. The government's expert panel framed Australian food security at the national scale (PMSEIC 2010). Of 102 food-related policy documents from Queensland, Western Australia, Victoria and the federal government, nine set food security as a specific goal, and none of the nine was federal or from Queensland (Summerhayes and Baker 2026, pp. 963-964). In the same set of documents, food-related urban planning received little attention, and policy was weakly integrated across levels of government (Summerhayes and Baker 2024, p. 1). Of 25 senior planners and policymakers interviewed, 21 agreed that food is a stranger to urban planning (Summerhayes and Baker 2025, p. 534). For the Sydney Basin, the farmland data were too inconsistent to say how much land is farmed (James and O'Neill 2016, p. 187). Lawrence, Richards and Lyons (2013, p. 30) argue that productivist, export-oriented farming cannot deliver the long-term production that national food security needs. The federal committee's 2023 inquiry made a National Food Plan its key recommendation (House of Representatives Standing Committee on Agriculture 2023, p. iii). A regional capacity result answers the question a regional food plan would ask: which regions could feed their own people from their own land.

### 1.5 Observed drought and climate effects on Australian farming

Since 1990 the climate has lowered the simulated water-limited yield potential of Australian wheat, which fell by 27% over 1990 to 2015 (Hochman 2017, p. 2071). National actual yields did not decline over the same period (Hochman 2017, p. 2078). Simulated farm profit has also fallen since 2001: a farm model put average broadacre profit under the 2001 to 2020 climate 22.6% below profit under the 1950 to 2000 climate (Hughes 2022, p. 11). ABARES modelling put the effect of the 2001 to 2023 seasons, against 1951 to 2000, at 18% of average broadacre profit (ABARES undated, p. 10). Both are simulated profit, not observed output. The 2017 to 2019 drought was a three-year rainfall deficit across the Murray-Darling Basin, unprecedented in the instrumental record (Falster 2024, p. 1). A regional economic model put the drought's national welfare loss at a present value of $53 billion, the authors' model estimate (Wittwer 2021, p. 932). The national series read for this paper give the value of production, an export share of 71% of production by volume in the three years to 2024-25 (ABARES undated, p. 5), and farm incomes from 2005-06 to 2024-25 (ABARES 2025, p. 2). None of them is an annual series of farm output, and no source read here measures farm output across 2011 to 2025 itself. The drought allowance of section 8 tests the margin against this dependence on the weather.

### 1.3 The model, what was left open, and the questions

The model is the author's own. It was built for a doctoral thesis (Lane 2014), and its essential parameters were published (Lane, Dawes and Grace 2014). Lane, Dawes and Grace (2015) found that capacity shrinks as the unit of self-sufficiency shrinks from the nation to states and regions. Lane (2017) set a short and a long time frame side by side. A planning study has applied Lane (2017) to scenarios for settling northern Australia (Bolleter 2018, p. 123). The earlier papers gave one data year and, for the long term, one national and regional picture. They left open how the picture changes over time, how robust the regional results are, and how the long term and shocks play out region by region.

This paper brings the whole picture forward from 2011 to 2025 with the method frozen. It asks five questions. First, which regions moved between under and over capacity, and how much of the change in capacity is land, yields, livestock and diet, given that population changes status, not capacity? Second, what would a 100-year self-sufficiency plan ask of each region, and how has that changed? Third, how do the regional results hold under shocks? Fourth, how sure are the regional results? Fifth, how much of the national margin survives as the unit of self-sufficiency shrinks from the nation to the smallest statistical areas, and has that changed since 2011? Sections 3 and 4 answer the first question, section 7 the second, section 8 the third, section 9 the fourth, and sections 5 and 6 the fifth. Sections 10 to 12 take selected regions in depth, the measures that would widen the margin, and the limits. The companion methods paper carries the comparison of methods and the choice of method.

## 2. Method and data in brief

Capacity here is the number of people a region's land could feed on a stated diet and settings, with no trade into or out of the region. A region is over capacity when its population exceeds that number. Status is reported as people over capacity, so a value above 1 is over capacity.

The model is the author's own. It was built for the thesis and published from 2014 to 2017 (Lane 2014; Lane, Dawes and Grace 2014; Lane, Dawes and Grace 2015; Lane 2017). The full model, its constants and its construction method are in the supporting information.

The model is run under two presets. The short term takes the diet and production of the data year in an average year. The long term takes the settings of a 100-year self-sufficiency plan, defined in section 7. The settings of both presets in both years are tabled in the supporting information (Table S1).

The model has 60 zones: the nation, seven state zones and 52 NRM regions. The regional results use 53 regions (52 NRM regions and the Northern Territory).

The 2011 inputs are those of the thesis. The 2025 inputs were brought forward layer by layer: land, population, crop yields, livestock, consumption, the organic share, the resilience table, liquid fuel and the diet. Each layer is a release whose inputs are recorded by hash. The method is frozen, so within one method tier the change between 2011 and 2025 is a data change.

The method has three tiers: published, corrected and improved. The corrected tier applies two corrections to the published method. One is the biodiesel yield per tonne of oilseed (Geoscience Australia and ABARE 2010, p. 331). The other is the carcase-to-retail yield of beef (USDA undated; AUS-MEAT 2005). The paper reports the corrected releases, `2011-fixed` and `2025-fixed`, unless a figure names another release. The 2011 results appear as corrected only.[^1] The regional 2011 results use `2011-fixed-2`, which corrects the 2011 population of North East Victoria and leaves every capacity unchanged (section 3). The method effects of section 9 are measured on the published base, `2025-mirror`. The diet swaps of section 12 are on the improved release, `2025-improved`. The herd-cycle range of section 11 is on `2025-organic`. The named scenarios and grain stores of sections 8 and 11 are on `2025-fixed`, with each store taking the resilience table of the frozen release `2025-store-1` or `2025-store-2`. The scale curve of section 5 evaluates every unit on its state's yields, the conservative bound; the zone results of sections 7 to 11 use each zone's own yields.

The drought allowance scales yields by one factor per state, taken from the 150-year record of wheat, oats and barley (Lane 2014). The national figure takes the Australian factor. Regions take their state's factor, so the regions of one state share it. The five regions that tip over capacity at a 1-in-10 year in 2025 lie in three states. Applying a factor derived from cereal yields to pasture-fed output has not been tested, and section 8 says so.

The 2025 results carry Monte Carlo ranges. The Monte Carlo samples 13 choices, of which 12 vary in the 2025 corrected and published releases. The method is described and tested in section 6 of the companion methods paper and only used here. The 2011 results have no ranges.

[^1]: The 2011 values published in Lane, Dawes and Grace (2015) and Lane (2017) were computed before the two corrections; the values in this paper are the corrected ones, and correction notes to those papers set out the change.

## 3. The short-term picture, 2011 to 2025

On the corrected releases, Australia's short-term capacity was 33.3 million people in 2011 (`2011-fixed`) against a population of 22.3 million. In 2025 (`2025-fixed`) it was 41.5 million against 27.6 million (Figure 1). Capacity was about 1.5 times the population in both years: 1.49 times in 2011 and 1.50 times in 2025. Between the two years capacity grew by 24.6% and the population by 23.7%, so the national margin held its multiple.

The regional picture moved more than the national one. Of the 53 regions (52 NRM regions and the Northern Territory), 22 were over capacity in the short term in 2011 (`2011-fixed-2`) and 16 in 2025 (`2025-fixed`). In the long term, 36 and 32 regions were over capacity. The net fall of six regions in the short term is larger than the two flips that pass the tests below. The count of regions over capacity is therefore not a finding on its own.

Eight regions changed status in the short term: seven moved from over to under capacity and one, Corangamite, from under to over. Each flip was tested three ways (Table 2). The first test asks whether the land layer alone moves the region's capacity by more than 2 times or less than 0.5 times, which marks a change in how land is defined rather than in how it is used. The second asks whether the 2011 zone was drawn on an older boundary. The third asks whether the 2025 status holds across the 5th to 95th percentile of the 2025 Monte Carlo range. Three flips are land-definition artefacts: Desert Channels, Southern Gulf and SA Arid Lands. Three are not robust to the 2025 range: Wet Tropics, Rangelands (Western Australia) and Corangamite. Two are clean and robust: Mackay Whitsunday and North Tasmania, both from over to under capacity.

The two clean flips carry qualifications. Mackay Whitsunday is under capacity in 2011 on the improved release (`2011-improved`), so it does not flip on that release. North Tasmania's 2011 status sits close to balance, at 144,582 people against a capacity of 139,165 (`2011-fixed-2`), and the 2011 inputs have no range. North East Victoria counted as a third clean flip until its 2011 population was found to be a copy of Goulburn Broken's 204,651. With the ABS figure of 98,604 it is under capacity in 2011 as well as 2025, so it does not flip. In the long term four regions flip, all from over to under, and one is clean and robust, South West Queensland; section 7 returns to them.

Capacity grew most in the inland cropping and basin regions. The largest short-term gains were in Central West NSW (464 thousand people), Lachlan (456 thousand) and Namoi (441 thousand), all under capacity at both dates (`2011-fixed` to `2025-fixed`). Part of these gains comes from diet settings, consumption and the red meat mix, that apply in every region (section 4). People grew most on the coast and in the capitals: by 1.4 million in Port Phillip and Westernport, 1.3 million in Hawkesbury-Nepean-Sydney and 1.0 million in South East Queensland. Davies (2024, p. 153) follows growth into regional cities and towns; among the model's regions, the largest gains in people were in the capital-city regions. Murrumbidgee-ACT, which holds Canberra, gained both, 348 thousand in capacity and 209 thousand in people. Figure 2 maps the change by region and marks the flips.

[Figure 2: change in short-term capacity and population by region, 2011 (`2011-fixed-2`) to 2025 (`2025-fixed`), with the status flips marked by test, from `analysis/paper_a/regional_flips.csv` and `analysis/comparison_2025/movers.csv`]

The capital-city regions are over capacity in both years and both presets, except Murrumbidgee-ACT, which is under capacity in the short term in both years. Swan, which holds Perth, is the clearest boundary case. Its short-term capacity falls from 170 thousand to 6 thousand while its population rises from 1.70 to 2.27 million. The land layer alone takes its capacity to 0.01 of the 2011 value, because the 2011 zone was drawn before the Perth boundary change. Swan's 2025 status can be used, but its 2011 status cannot. Elsewhere on the coast the picture is mixed: Wet Tropics, Mackay Whitsunday, North Tasmania and Corangamite flip in the short term, while South Coast WA, Eyre Peninsula and Glenelg Hopkins are under capacity in both years.

The cautions are these. Five zones carry older 2011 boundaries. The land-definition flag is a fixed threshold at 2 and 0.5 times. Robustness is tested on the 2025 range only, because the 2011 inputs were not sampled. The artefact test is made on capacity, not on whether the population change is real, and the North East Victoria correction shows that a population input can make or unmake a flip. The flips have not been tested against the method tier, which the Mackay Whitsunday case shows can matter.

## 4. What drove the change

The change in capacity between 2011 and 2025 was taken apart on the corrected releases by a cumulative walk. The walk starts at `2011-fixed` and adds the 2025 data layers one at a time, in the order of the release chain: land, population, crop yields, livestock, consumption, the organic update, the resilience table, long-term liquid fuel and the red meat mix of the diet. It ends at `2025-fixed`. Each layer's effect is the capacity after it minus the capacity before it. The effects therefore sum exactly to the change, with no interaction term, and each effect depends on its place in the order. Kastner (2012) split a change in land need into population, yield and diet effects; here population is not one of the causes.

Population does not enter capacity in this model; it changes whether a zone is over or under its capacity. Nationally, people over capacity was 0.670 in the short term and 1.454 in the long term in 2011, and 0.665 and 1.767 in 2025 (Table 3). The short-term status held, while the long-term status worsened because the population grew and long-term capacity barely moved. The population layer moves capacity only through the infrastructure land that follows population, which changes capacity by 0 people nationally.

In the short term capacity rose by 8.2 million. Land took 2.6 million off. Livestock added 4.7 million, consumption 4.2 million and the red meat mix 1.8 million. Crop yields added 0.1 million, because short-term capacity is bound by pasture-fed meat and dairy rather than by field crops (section 6). The organic share is counted in the organic step, not in consumption; on the release chain as built, consumption would carry 3.8 million and the organic step 0.4 million, with the same sum.

In the long term capacity rose by 0.27 million. Land is the largest effect, at -1.2 million. Livestock added 0.6 million, the red meat mix 0.4 million, crop yields 0.2 million, the organic update 0.17 million and liquid fuel 0.09 million. The resilience table moved capacity by -7 people.

Figure 3 gives the effect of each layer in each of the 53 regions (52 NRM regions and the Northern Territory), in both presets. In the inland cropping regions the short-term gain is mostly livestock: in Central West NSW livestock added 356,807 people to a capacity that rose from 1,333,553 to 1,798,009. In Swan the land layer took 167,976 off a 2011 capacity of 170,101, the boundary case of section 3. The diet layers that raise the national figure can lower a region's figure. In Fitzroy consumption took 62,789 and the red meat mix 38,290 off short-term capacity, and in the long term crop yields added 34,818 there against 658 from livestock.

## 5. The scale curve: where the margin goes

This section asked how much of the national capacity is left when every unit feeds itself from its own land, measured as the sum of the units' capacities as a share of the national capacity. It was computed for 7 states, 53 regions (52 NRM regions and the Northern Territory), 89 SA4s, 336 SA3s and 2,450 SA2s. Every unit kept the national settings for diet and waste. The curve in Figure 4 is on the state-yield basis: every unit took the yields, climate zone and settings of its state. This is the conservative bound; the zone results of sections 7 to 11 are on each zone's own yields, the local basis, and every regional sum below names its basis. Only a consistent yield basis gives a clean curve, because capacity depends on a unit's land mix and yields.

### 5.1 The curve and its stability

On the 2025 corrected release the states summed to 75% of the national short-term capacity, the regions to 44%, the SA4s to 58%, the SA3s to 44% and the SA2s to 37% (Table 4). On 2011 data the states gave 77%, the regions 44%, the SA4s 60%, the SA3s 46% and the SA2s 40%. The shares barely changed between the two data years. The long-term curve fell further at every level. In 2025 the states kept 49% of the long-term capacity, the regions 23%, the SA4s 35%, the SA3s 24% and the SA2s 20%. The 2011 long-term shares were 44%, 20%, 32%, 23% and 18%.

[Figure 4: the scale curve, the sum of capacities as a share of national capacity at each level from the nation to SA2, 2011 and 2025, short and long term, state-yield basis, corrected releases (2011-fixed, 2025-fixed); from `results/scale_curve.csv`, drawn by `analysis/scale_curve_2025/plot_scale_curve.py` (`scale_curve.png`)]

Lane, Dawes and Grace 2015 found that capacity falls as the unit of self-sufficiency shrinks. That finding held on the corrected method. On 2011 data the national short-term capacity was 33.3 million, the states summed to 25.7 million and the 53 regions to 21.0 million. The regions figure is on each zone's own yields, not the curve's state basis, and the curve extends the finding to SA2. In Iran, capacity summed over local units fell below the national figure (Khorsandi 2022), and a meta-model of those results related capacity to area with diminishing returns (Khorsandi 2023). Existing US cropland could meet as much as 90% of national food demand within 100 miles on the standard diet (Zumkehr and Campbell 2015, p. 246). New York State could supply 34% of its food needs within an average distance of 49 km (Peters 2009, p. 72). Worldwide, 11 to 28% of people could meet their demand for a single crop type within 100 km (Kinnunen 2020, p. 229). Capacity studies are the most frequent type of foodshed study (Schreiber 2021, p. 9), and foodshed reach varies with diet and land type (Kurtz 2020).

### 5.2 The boundary effect and its cause

The 53 regions lost as much as the 336 SA3s, 44% against 44% in the short term in 2025. They lost far more than the 89 SA4s, which kept 58%. Rebuilding the regions from whole SA2s gave the same 44%, so the gap is a property of the partition, not of the land data. The gap lay where rangeland meets cropland. As shares of national capacity, the regions of New South Wales summed to 17.6% against 25.3% for its SA4s. In South Australia the figures were 1.2% against 4.8%, and in Western Australia 2.0% against 5.1%. The other states were about equal on the two partitions.

Section 6 gives the cause. South Australia's Arid Lands region holds 326,000 km² of pasture and 221 km² of cropland. On the state-yield basis its capacity was 41 thousand people. SA4 406 joins that arid country with the cropland of the Eyre Peninsula and had a capacity of 1.7 million. The two regions apart had 41 thousand and 132 thousand. On its own yields SA Arid Lands had a capacity of 28.6 thousand in 2025 (section 10). In Western Australia the Rangelands region, with 646,000 km² of pasture and 937 km² of cropland, had a capacity of 241 thousand. SA4 511 had 1.5 million. Units with more than 1,000 km² of pasture and cropland under 2% of that pasture held 71% of the nation's pasture among the regions (8 units), 72% among the SA3s (18) and 51% among the SA4s (3). The land the partitions left unused rose from 1.96 million km² for the nation to 3.67 million for the SA4s and 4.50 million for the regions. In the long term the gap between the regions and the SA4s was 12 points, 23% against 35%.

### 5.3 How much a better drawing of regions keeps

Random groupings and an ideal contiguous split bound how much a better drawing of regions would keep. Capacity was exactly proportional to land: 0.5, 0.1 and 0.01 times the national land gave 100.0% of the same fraction of national capacity. A split in which every unit had the national land mix would therefore lose nothing. On the national yield vector, random groupings of the SA2s into 53 groups kept 68 to 81% of national capacity, against 33% for the 53 regions as drawn. These are spreads across five draws, not uncertainty ranges. The SA4s, at 54%, were slightly worse than random groupings of their number (56 to 69%), and the SA3s, at 35%, were about equal to random (34 to 44%). The best contiguous split found at 53 regions, each capped at 650,000 km² of land, kept 76% of national capacity in the short term and 69% in the long term (Supplementary Figure S1). These come from two splits, each optimised for its own term; the short-term split kept 66.1% in the long term. The search was heuristic, so 76% and 69% are lower bounds on the best split.

## 6. The mechanism

One rule drives most of the results above. A diet in the model needs cropland and pasture in nearly fixed proportion, so a unit's capacity is set by whichever land type runs short. Cropland can carry pasture-fed products when pasture falls short, but pasture cannot grow crops (Lane 2014, p. 266). Lane 2014 found the share of cropping land to be the most significant determinant of capacity (p. 3). In a national test on the 2025 corrected release, capacity was unchanged for every cropland share from 10% to 100% of the combined cropland and pasture area. The test was run for Australia only, so the rule is shown nationally and not for regions.

On the reference diet of the 2025 corrected release, pasture binds nationally. The diet needs 3.49 million km² of pasture against 3.32 million km² held. About 175,000 km² of cropland therefore grazes animals, and 141,000 km² grows food crops. No pasture is left unused; the 1.96 million km² of unused land is non-agricultural land. This is why measures aimed at field crops moved the 2025 short term by 0 to 2.4%, while livestock output 10% higher raised it by 9.7%. It is also why field-crop yields 24% lower took 0.2% off the short term, against 26% off at a 1-in-10 drought, which scales every yield (both on the 2025 corrected release).

On the 2025 corrected release, pasture-fed meat raises capacity until the pasture is used up, and lowers it once cropland is drawn into grazing. There a diet of 2.5% meat and eggs fed 118.0 million, against 112.4 million with none. On the 2025 improved release the diet with no meat or eggs was the highest point, at 103.6 million. The rise and fall therefore holds on the corrected release only.

A rangeland region cannot use its pasture. In SA Arid Lands cropland binds at every diet tested, and its capacity on the state-yield basis was 41 thousand people.

The model has one pasture land type, and within a zone dairy and beef cattle are credited with the same average pasture yield. The dairy-on-good-land improvement moves dairy's land to the cropping column, at the observed yield of dairy farms. Measured against the published-method release (2025-mirror), it moved regions both ways in the short term. SA Arid Lands fell by 27%, while Wet Tropics, Rangelands and Lower Murray Darling rose by 22%. Grazing on land unsuited to crops is common ground with the wider literature: 65% of the grass ruminants eat grows on such land (Poore and Nemecek 2018, p. 990), and livestock on it use land that crops cannot (van Zanten 2016; Roos 2017).

## 7. The long term: a 100-year plan

This section asked what a plan to feed Australia from its own land for 100 years would cover. The long-term settings follow Lane 2014 and Lane 2017. They plan for the worst year in 100 of weather. They set a low-meat diet, with meat and eggs at 7.5% of the diet against 12% in the short term. They assume no avoidable waste, full recycling of food waste, organic farming and liquid fuel cut from 2,360 to 210 litres a person. They also set activity level 3, three fibre settings, timber, infrastructure area and a 30% reserve. The land for the remaining fuel rests on a biodiesel yield of 400 litres per tonne of oilseeds (Geoscience Australia and ABARE 2010, p. 331). Australia's emergency oil stocks equalled 61 days of net imports against an obligation of 90 days (Soliman Hunter 2021, PDF p. 11).

### 7.1 National capacity in both years

On the corrected method the long-term capacity was 15.35 million on 2011 data and 15.6 million on 2025 data (Table 5). A plan on that basis would cover 69% of the 2011 population of 22.3 million and 57% of the 2025 population of 27.6 million. Capacity rose by 0.27 million while the population grew by 5.28 million. ## 8. Shocks and food security by region

The model's drought allowance scales every yield, crops, pasture products and livestock alike, by one factor per state. The factor for each return period comes from the record of wheat, oats and barley yields over 150 years (Lane 2014, p. 193). The 53 regions (52 NRM regions and the Northern Territory) each take their state's factor. Regions of one state therefore share a factor, but they do not all tip together: the five regions that tip at 1 in 10 in 2025 are in three states. Applying a grain-based factor to pasture-fed output is untested, and no source read gives it.

### 8.1 The national margin

In an average year the national short-term capacity was 1.49 times the population in 2011 and 1.5 times in 2025 (Table 7). In a 1-in-10 year it was 1.11 and 1.12 times, or 30.8 million against 27.6 million in 2025. In a 1-in-20 year the population exceeded capacity: people over capacity was 1.12 in 2011 and 1.05 in 2025. In a 1-in-100 year capacity fell to 0.68 and 0.69 times the population, 18.9 million in 2025. The margin is above balance at 1 in 10 and below it at 1 in 20 in both data years. 1 in 20 is not a threshold. At the 2025 population the failure probability is 5.8 to 6.1% a year, about 1 in 16 to 17, on the corrected method (Paper B). National totals smooth out local shocks in a large country such as Australia (Cottrell 2019, p. 135).

### 8.4 What a single-year shock does not describe

A single-year shock does not describe a drought of several years: the 2017 to 2019 drought was exceptional as a three-year rainfall deficit across the Murray-Darling Basin, while its single years mostly fell within the expected range (Falster 2024, pp. 6, 9). Bad wheat years are also partly correlated across Australia: ENSO and the Indian Ocean Dipole together explain 30 to 41% of wheat variability in Australia and East Africa (Anderson 2019). The climate effect on cropping productivity differs by state (Hughes 2017, p. 189).

An export-inclusive count sits beside the model's. Lawrence, Richards and Lyons 2013 give about 22 million people fed at home and 40 million fed abroad, with no base year stated (p. 30). PMSEIC 2010 is the source of the 40 million abroad (p. 15). PMSEIC's own figure is 60 million (p. 1), stated without source, and the report does not add the two. This count includes exports and the diet as eaten, so its basis differs from the model's self-sufficient capacity.

## 9. How sure are the regional results

### 9.1 The ranges and the status flips

This section tests the regional results for the 53 regions (52 NRM regions and the Northern Territory), each on its own yields (the local basis). The ranges come from a Monte Carlo of 1,000 Latin hypercube draws over 13 sampled input choices. Twelve of the 13 vary in the corrected and published 2025 releases (2025-fixed and 2025-mirror). The thirteenth, the long-term feeding benchmark, has no effect in those releases. Paper B (section 6) describes and tests the method; this paper only uses it. The 2011 inputs were not sampled, so the 2011 results have no range.

On the corrected 2025 release (2025-fixed), the 5th to 95th percentile of national capacity is 36.3 to 45.8 million people in the short term and 13.4 to 16.5 million in the long term. The corrected long-term capacity was 15.35 million on 2011 data (2011-fixed) and 15.6 million on 2025 data (2025-fixed). Both figures lie inside the 2025 long-term range. The change between the two years is therefore smaller than the spread across input choices for 2025. That comparison is not a test of whether the change is real, because the 2011 figure has no range of its own.

Each status flip was tested against its region's 2025 range on 2025-fixed. A flip holds when the 2025 population lies outside the region's 5th to 95th percentile of capacity. The test covers the 2025 status only; a flip also needs a robust 2011 status, and the 2011 inputs have no draws. In the short term eight regions changed status between 2011 and 2025 on the corrected method: seven from over to under capacity and one from under to over. Three of the eight are not robust to the 2025 range: Wet Tropics, Rangelands (WA) and Corangamite, the one region that moved from under to over capacity. Three others are land-definition artefacts: Desert Channels, Southern Gulf and SA Arid Lands. Two pass every test in hand: Mackay Whitsunday and North Tasmania. In the long term four regions changed status, all from over to under capacity. Three of them carry an artefact flag, and two are not robust to the range. One, South West Queensland, passes every test.

The method tier is not a flip test. It does move single regions: Corangamite changes short-term status between the corrected and improved 2025 releases (2025-fixed and 2025-improved), and Mackay Whitsunday does so on 2011 data.

Five range ends rest on the author's judgement and have no outside source. They are the three-way choice of the meat data window, with equal chances; the low end of the meat-and-egg share of the diet, 0.115; the low end of the red-meat share of meat, 0.528; the equal weight given to the leave-one-out resilience table; and the low end of the long-term feeding benchmark, 0.30. The last has no effect in the corrected and published 2025 releases. It is drawn in the improved release (2025-improved), with a negligible national effect. The judgement ends drive most of the width of the ranges. On 2025-fixed the meat window alone explains 81% of the variance of the short-term national capacity and 36% of the long-term. The resilience table alone explains 52% of the long-term variance, and its weight is one of the judgement ends.

## 10. Regions in depth

Five regions were chosen, one of each type, from the largest movers among the 53 regions (52 NRM regions and the Northern Territory): a capital with hinterland, a Murray-Darling Basin pair, a south-west Western Australian cropping region, Tasmania, and an arid region set beside a cropping neighbour. Its short-term capacity fell from 66,198 to 46,572 people while its population grew from 4,073,137 to 5,401,609. Its long-term capacity is 7,186 in 2011 and 6,106 in 2025. ## 11. Discussion: what would widen the margin

A measure is worth what it does to the constraint that binds. Each measure below was applied alone to the corrected 2025 release (2025-fixed), against a reference of 41.5 million in the short term and 15.6 million in the long term (Table 5). Short-term capacity is bound by pasture-fed meat and dairy. Measures aimed at field crops therefore do almost nothing: field-crop yields, irrigation and cropland taken from pasture move the short term by 0 to 2.4%. In the model, cropland can carry pasture-fed products but pasture cannot carry crops, so capacity is the same for any cropland share from 10% to 99% of the combined area. Pasture is fully used: the short term needs 3.49 million km2 of pasture against 3.32 million held. The 1.96 million km2 of unused land is non-agricultural land (section 6). Doubling cropland from that land, the 1975 CSIRO case (Gifford and others 1975, as reported in Henry and Godden 1995), raises both terms by 8.7%, to 45.1 and 17.0 million.

### 11.1 Livestock productivity

Livestock output 10% higher raises the short term to 45.5 million (+9.7%) and the long term to 17.1 million (+9.4%), on 2025-fixed. Output 20% higher gives 49.6 million (+19.4%) and 18.6 million (+18.8%). Wiedemann and others 2015 give five-year averages for the national beef herd from 1981-85 to 2006-10. The average daily gain of steers rose from 0.39 to 0.72 kg across those periods (Table 1, p. 111). Beef produced per breeding cow mated rose 32%, from 301 to 396 kg, mainly from heavier steers (p. 113). The industry forecasts in Mayberry and others 2019 and Ridoutt 2024 concern animal numbers, not output per animal.

### 11.2 Diet composition

Setting the meat-and-egg share of the diet to 5%, with the other diet settings at their base values, raises the short term to 76.8 million (+85.0%) and the long term to 19.6 million (+25.7%), on 2025-fixed. A share of 7.5% gives 48.2 million (+16.2%). A red-meat share of 30% of meat, with more poultry and pork, gives 64.6 million (+55.6%) and 18.5 million (+18.6%). Paper B gives a different figure under the same label: 103.3 million on the improved release (2025-improved), for the guideline diet with 5% meat and eggs. The two differ in release and in what else the diet changes. Studies of Australian and global diets give the land and emissions of observed and recommended diets (Hendrie and others 2014; Tilman and Clark 2014; Ridoutt and others 2020).

### 11.3 Food waste

The model's 6% setting halves the model's retail-and-consumer loss share, a choice that mirrors UN Target 12.3. Australia's own goal is to halve food waste by 2030 across the whole supply and consumption chain (DCCEEW 2017, pp. 3, 8). The National Food Waste Baseline estimated 7.3 Mt of food waste for 2016-17 (DCCEEW 2019, p. 3). The 2021 Feasibility Study estimated about 7.6 Mt a year (DCCEEW n.d.). The two are estimates from different projects and are not combined here. On 2025-fixed the 6% setting raises the short term to 48.3 million (+16.4%) and lowers the long term to 14.3 million (-8.2%). The long-term settings assume no avoidable waste, so the 6% setting is a step back there. No avoidable waste raises the short term to 55.3 million (+33.2%), and full recycling of food waste adds 2.2%. The waste scenario of section 8, rerun on 2025-fixed, gives the same result.

### 11.5 Drought resilience

Drought-proofing the worst year, so that it loses half as much, moves the 100-year factor for Australia from 0.46 to 0.73 of trend. On 2025-fixed it raises the long term by 60%, to 24.9 million, and leaves the short term unchanged. National drought policy has moved towards self-reliance and preparedness (Botterill 2021; Productivity Commission 2023, p. 3). The Future Drought Fund makes $100 million a year available for drought resilience (Productivity Commission 2023, p. v).

### 11.4 Grain reserves

The store sizes of one and two years are the model's own scenarios; no Australian source sets them. With the store resilience tables applied to 2025-fixed, a one-year grain store raises the long term by 48%, to 23.2 million, and a two-year store by 64%, to 25.7 million. Neither changes the short term. A one-year store is worth the same as planning for a drought of about 16 years instead of 100, and a two-year store about 10 years. Both equivalents are read by interpolation between rows of the planning table. On one release, a two-year store (+64%) and drought-proofing (+60%) are the two largest long-term measures, and both act on the worst year. The store is free in the model.

A one-year store for domestic use is about 11.5 Mt: 3.1 Mt for direct use at 112 kg a person, and 8.4 Mt for the feed grain embodied in meat at 303 kg a person. The 303 kg is a 2050 base-case figure of Foran and Poldy 2002, applied here to the 2025 population. The store is about a fifth of an average national cereal crop of 52.6 Mt. Wright and Cafiero 2011 discuss one or two years of supply for a dry importer, a different case (p. S72). Reserves and trade together set exposure to supply shocks (Marchand and others 2016).

### 11.6 The regions as a pool

On each zone's own yields (the local basis) and on 2025-fixed, the national short-term capacity is 1.60 times the sum of the capacities of the 53 regions (52 NRM regions and the Northern Territory) in an average year. The factor is 1.82 at a 1-in-10 year. In the long term it is 2.60, and 3.00 on 2011 data (2011-fixed). The regions' pasture and cropland complement each other. In an average year the regions over capacity are short by 20.7 million people-equivalents, and the regions under capacity have 19.1 million to spare. At a 1-in-10 year the shortfall is 21.5 million and the spare 10.8 million. Trade in products between regions recovers at most about 15.5 million people-equivalents in the short term: the national 41.5 million less the regional sum of 26.0 million. In the long term it recovers 9.6 million. This is an upper bound, because it assumes products move without loss or cost. It is not the 15.5 million of Paper B, which is the sum of SA2 capacities.

Moving food has a cost. Road and rail transport of agriculture and forestry cost A$6.8 billion, 9.8% of farm gross value of production, in 2015-16 (Higgins and others 2018a, p. 196). Northern supply chains run over 1000 km on roads cut by seasonal flooding (Higgins and others 2018b, p. 341). Few studies follow shocks along supply chains (Davis and others 2020), and no Australian study of food flows between regions was found for this paper. Bad wheat years are partly correlated across the southern states (Anderson and others 2019), so a pool of regions does not escape a widespread drought.

### 11.7 Measures the model cannot carry

The capacities assume that all production is eaten at home. In 2021-23 Australia produced 4.66 Mt of meat and ate 2.96 Mt, a surplus of 57% (FAO food balance). This is a different measure from the model's capacity of 1.50 times the population, and the two are not compared directly. If meat output fell by the national capacity factor, it would be about 3.45 Mt at a 1-in-10 year and 2.95 Mt at a 1-in-20 year; that assumption is untested. Farmers produce some 93 per cent of the food eaten at home (Lawrence, Richards and Lyons 2013, p. 32).

Fish imports, 593 thousand tonnes, are about as large as the food use of fish, 641 thousand tonnes. In the commodity balance fish is the item that runs out first, at 12 to 17 million people. Trade raises supply diversity but also import dependence (Kummu and others 2020).

Pulses, 4.4 Mt produced and mostly exported, keep for a long time. The herd is also a store: the single-year capacity runs from 43.5 to 55.4 million across 2016 to 2025 with the herd cycle, on the 2025-organic release as built, not on 2025-fixed.

The national 100-year worst-year factor, 0.46 of trend, is milder than those of New South Wales (0.30), Victoria (0.22), Queensland (0.35) and South Australia (0.35). It is not milder than those of Western Australia (0.47) or Tasmania (0.67). Spreading production across states and varieties therefore lowers national variability.

In the long term 94% of the population lives in regions over capacity (2025-fixed). Settlement policy changes where food must travel, not the national capacity. Aquaculture, seasonal forecasting, household and community stocks, and finance and insurance are further measures the model cannot carry; water and fuel are limitations (section 12).

Four cautions apply. Each measure was applied alone, so the effects are not additive. Cost, time and feasibility are not modelled. Settings such as the 5% meat share bound what is possible, not what is likely. The drought, store and livestock results rest on one weather factor applied to every yield.

## 12. Limitations and conclusions

The model is a closed land-use account for each region. It carries no trade, disease or biosecurity, and no limits from nutrients, soil or labour. It leaves out Indigenous food systems and wild harvest. Its climate scenarios scale field-crop yields only, not pasture. The 2011 inputs were not sampled, and five zones carry older 2011 boundaries. These limits bear most on the closed-region reading, which the four points below take in turn.

### 12.1 Self-sufficiency as a policy concept

Self-sufficiency means production relative to consumption, so it does not rule out trade (Clapp 2017, p. 89). Local self-sufficiency is rarely possible: only 11 to 28% of the world's people could meet their demand for a given crop from within 100 km (Kinnunen and others 2020, p. 229). Most foodshed studies measure capacity, and one hybrid study is Australian (Schreiber and others 2021, p. 9). Australia is a surplus producer in some of the paths modelled by Beltran-Peña and others 2020 (p. 4). The closed region of this paper is therefore a bound, not a policy goal.

### 12.2 Urban agriculture and other adaptations

Under forced local self-sufficiency, diet raises capacity at every scale but does not close the scale loss. On 2025-fixed and the state-yield basis, the sum of SA2 capacities is 37% of the national capacity on the base diet. It is 33% on a 2.5% meat-and-egg diet and 45% with no meat or eggs, each against the national capacity on the same diet. Against the national figure on the base diet, the SA2 sum on the 2.5% diet reaches 95%. Swaps within food groups raise the national capacity by about three fifths (+62%), from 53.6 to 86.9 million, on the improved release (2025-improved). Gardens add little: covering all fruit and vegetable land from gardens would free only about 7% of the cropland a person needs (2025-fixed). Urban agriculture could supply 15.0% of food energy for the residents of the Sydney blocks modelled by Mcdougall and others 2020 (p. 8).

### 12.3 Water for food

The model has no water limit, so its irrigation results are an upper bound. Raising the irrigated share of cropland to 5% adds 2.4% to the 2025 short term (2025-fixed). In the Murray-Darling Basin, median projected streamflow falls about 20% under 2 °C of warming (Prosser 2021, p. 4). Higher irrigation efficiency rarely lowers water use across a basin (Grafton and others 2018).

### 12.4 Fuel and energy in food production

The model carries fuel only through its liquid fuel setting. Without the low-fuel setting of the long term, the 2025 long-term capacity falls from 15.6 to 3.7 million (2025-fixed). With the biofuel share at 1 and the other settings at their short-term values, the 2025 short term falls from 41.5 to 9.7 million. Australia's refining and fuel stocks leave it exposed to imports of liquid fuel (Soliman Hunter and Taylor 2021). Energy used after the farm gate is outside the model (Ladha-Sabur and others 2019; D'Odorico and others 2018).

**Conclusions.** The five questions of section 1 are answered in turn. First, national short-term capacity rose from 33.3 to 41.5 million between 2011 and 2025 on the corrected method. Livestock added 4.7 million people, food consumption data 4.2 million and the red-meat mix of the diet 1.8 million, while land took away 2.6 million and crop yields added 0.1 million. Population changes status, not capacity: people over capacity moved from 0.670 to 0.665 in the short term and from 1.454 to 1.767 in the long term. Second, a 100-year self-sufficient plan covered 69% of the population in 2011 and 57% in 2025, with capacity of 15.35 and 15.6 million. Third, the worst of ten years leaves 30.8 million against 27.6 million people in 2025. At a 1-in-20 year the population is 1.05 times capacity, and the failure probability a year is 5.8 to 6.1%, about 1 in 16 to 17. Fourth, two short-term flips pass the tests in hand (Mackay Whitsunday and North Tasmania), and one long-term flip (South West Queensland). The short-term count of regions over capacity is robust to the method tier, while the long-term count is not. Fifth, on the state-yield basis the sum of SA2 capacities is 37% of the national short-term capacity in 2025.
