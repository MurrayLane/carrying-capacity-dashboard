# Paper A, results: Australia's carrying capacity 2011 to 2025 by region on the corrected releases

First draft, assembled 2026-11-13 from A_s01-04.md, A_s05-08.md, A_s09-12.md. Every number traces to the claims csv beside each section file; sentences marked [gate 4] in those csvs use values awaiting Murray's gate 4 approval. Not for circulation.

## Abstract

How many people can Australia's regions feed from their own land, and has that changed since 2011? This paper brings the author's land-use model forward from 2011 to 2025 with the method frozen. It compares capacity with population in 53 regions (52 NRM regions and the Northern Territory), takes the change apart layer by layer, sums capacity from the nation down to 2,450 SA2s, and tests the regional results against bad years, Monte Carlo ranges and method choices. On the corrected releases, national short-term capacity rose from 33.3 million to 41.5 million people while the population grew from 22.3 million to 27.6 million, so capacity stayed about 1.5 times the population. Livestock, consumption and the red meat mix of the diet drove the rise; population changes status, not capacity. The margin depends on the weather: it is above balance in a 1-in-10 year and below it in a 1-in-20 year. A 100-year self-sufficiency plan would cover 57% of the 2025 population. When every SA2 must feed itself, their capacities sum to 37% of the national short-term capacity. The closed region is therefore a bound, not a policy goal. Measures widen the margin by acting on what binds: livestock output, diet and waste in the short term, drought-proofing and grain stores in the long term.

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 Australian population debate and planning

How many people Australia can feed from its own land is an old question. It returned as the population grew between 2011 and 2025. The 2021 Intergenerational Report projected 38.8 million people in 2060-61, the first downward revision in such a report (Treasury 2021, p. 13). The 2023 report projected 26.5 million people in 2022-23 rising to 40.5 million in 2062-63 (Treasury 2023, p. 39). It put average growth over the next 40 years at 1.1% a year, against 1.4% over the past 40 years (Treasury 2023, p. 37). The 2025 Population Statement put the population at 27.5 million in the March quarter of 2025 and forecast 41 million by 2065-66 (Centre for Population 2025, p. 1).

Growth of 5 million over two decades "has been described as unexpected, unprecedented, and unplanned" (Davies 2024, p. 149). In Davies's own account, "Australia's population growth is highly managed" (Davies 2024, p. 150). McDonald (2017, p. 3) finds that immigrants supplied most of the rise in employment from July 2011 to July 2016, a measure of employment, not population.

This paper sets capacity against each data year's own population, which rose by about 24% (section 3). Published estimates of how many people Australia can feed scatter widely, mostly because they answer different questions (Henry and Godden 1995; Foran and Poldy 2002; CSIRO and University of Queensland 2025).

### 1.3 The model, what was left open, and the questions

The model is the author's own. It was built for a doctoral thesis (Lane 2014), and its essential parameters were published (Lane et al. 2014). Lane et al. (2015) found that capacity shrinks as the unit of self-sufficiency shrinks from the nation to states and regions. Lane (2017) set a short and a long time frame side by side, and a planning study has applied it to scenarios for settling northern Australia (Bolleter 2018, p. 123). The earlier papers gave one data year and, for the long term, one national and regional picture; later work citing them was not checked.

The paper asks five questions. First, which regions moved between under and over capacity, and how much of the change in capacity is land, yields, livestock and diet, given that population changes status, not capacity? Second, what would a 100-year self-sufficiency plan ask of each region, and how has that changed? Third, how do the regional results hold under shocks? Fourth, how sure are the regional results? Fifth, how much of the national margin survives as the unit of self-sufficiency shrinks to the smallest statistical areas, and has that changed since 2011? Sections 3 and 4 answer the first, 7 the second, 8 the third, 9 the fourth, and 5 and 6 the fifth. Sections 10 to 12 cover selected regions, the measures that would widen the margin, and the limits. A companion methods paper compares the published estimates and carries the choice of method.

### 1.4 Food security in Australian planning and policy

Food security and regional self-sufficiency appear in Australian planning and policy only in patches. The government's expert panel framed food security at the national scale (PMSEIC 2010). Of 102 food-related policy documents from Queensland, Western Australia, Victoria and the federal government, nine set food security as a specific goal, and none of the nine was federal or from Queensland (Summerhayes and Baker 2026, pp. 963-964). In the same documents food-related urban planning received little attention, and policy was weakly integrated across levels of government (Summerhayes and Baker 2024, p. 1). Of 25 senior planners and policymakers interviewed, 21 agreed that food is a stranger to urban planning (Summerhayes and Baker 2025, p. 534). Lawrence et al. (2013, p. 30) argue that productivist, export-oriented farming cannot deliver the long-term production that national food security needs. The federal committee's 2023 inquiry made a National Food Plan its key recommendation (House of Representatives Standing Committee on Agriculture 2023, p. iii). A regional capacity result could inform such a plan: which regions could feed their own people from their own land.

### 1.5 Observed drought and climate effects on Australian farming

Since 1990 the climate has lowered the simulated water-limited yield potential of Australian wheat, by 27% to 2015 (Hochman et al. 2017, p. 2071). National actual yields did not decline over the same period (Hochman et al. 2017, p. 2078). Simulated farm profit has also fallen since 2001: a farm model put average broadacre profit under the 2001 to 2020 climate 22.6% below that under the 1950 to 2000 climate (Hughes et al. 2022, p. 11). ABARES modelling put the effect of the 2001 to 2023 seasons, against 1951 to 2000, at 18% of average broadacre profit (ABARES undated, p. 10). Both are simulated profit, not observed output. The 2017 to 2019 drought was a three-year rainfall deficit across the Murray-Darling Basin, unprecedented in the instrumental record (Falster et al. 2024, p. 1). A regional economic model put the drought's national welfare loss at a present value of $53 billion (Wittwer and Waschik 2021, p. 932). The national series read here give the value of production, an export share of 71% of production by volume in the three years to 2024-25 (ABARES undated, p. 5), and farm incomes from 2005-06 to 2024-25 (ABARES 2025, p. 2). None is an annual series of farm output, so no source read here measures output across 2011 to 2025. Section 8 tests the margin against this dependence on the weather.

## 2. Method and data in brief

Capacity here is the number of people a region's land could feed on a stated diet and settings, with no trade in or out. A region is over capacity when its population exceeds that number. Status is reported as people over capacity, so a value above 1 is over capacity.

The full model, its constants and its construction method, published from 2014 to 2017 (section 1.3), are in the supplementary information.

The model is run under two presets. The short term takes the diet and production of the data year in an average year. The long term takes the settings of a 100-year self-sufficiency plan, defined in section 7. Table S1 gives the settings of both presets in both years.

The model has 60 zones: the nation, seven state zones and 52 NRM regions. The regional results use 53 regions (52 NRM regions and the Northern Territory).

The 2011 inputs are those of the thesis. The 2025 inputs were brought forward layer by layer, in the order of section 4. Each layer is a release whose inputs are recorded by hash. The method is frozen, so within one method tier the change between 2011 and 2025 is a data change.

The method has three tiers: published, corrected and improved. The corrected tier applies two corrections to the published method, the biodiesel yield per tonne of oilseed (Geoscience Australia and ABARE 2010, p. 331) and the carcase-to-retail yield of beef (USDA undated; AUS-MEAT 2005), and for 2011 sets each state's population to the sum of its regions (ERRATA error 6), which moves the state ratios but no capacity. The paper reports the corrected releases, `2011-fixed` and `2025-fixed`, unless a figure names another release. The 2011 results appear as corrected only.[^1] The regional 2011 results use `2011-fixed-2`, which corrects the 2011 population of North East Victoria and leaves every capacity unchanged (section 3). Other releases are named at their figures: the published base, `2025-mirror`, for the method effects of section 9; the improved release, `2025-improved`, for the diet swaps of section 12; and `2025-organic` for the herd-cycle range of the supplementary information. The named scenarios and grain stores of sections 8 and 11 are on `2025-fixed`, with each store taking the resilience table of the frozen release `2025-store-1` or `2025-store-2`.

The drought allowance scales yields by one factor per state, taken from the 150-year record of wheat, oats and barley (Lane 2014, p. 193). The national figure takes the Australian factor, and each region takes its state's factor (section 8). Applying a factor derived from cereal yields to pasture-fed output has not been tested.

The 2025 results carry Monte Carlo ranges over 13 sampled choices, of which 12 vary in the 2025 corrected and published releases. Section 6 of the companion methods paper describes and tests the method. The 2011 results have no ranges.

[^1]: The 2011 values published in Lane et al. (2015) and Lane (2017) were computed before the two corrections to the method and before each 2011 state's population was set to the sum of its regions; the values in this paper are the corrected ones, and correction notes to those papers set out the change.

## 3. The short-term picture, 2011 to 2025

On the corrected releases, Australia's short-term capacity was 33.3 million people in 2011 (`2011-fixed`) against a population of 22.3 million. In 2025 (`2025-fixed`) it was 41.5 million against 27.6 million (Figure 1). Capacity was about 1.5 times the population in both years: 1.49 times in 2011 and 1.50 times in 2025. Capacity grew by 24.6% and the population by 23.7%, so the national margin held its multiple.

The regional picture moved more. Of the 53 regions (52 NRM regions and the Northern Territory), 22 were over capacity in the short term in 2011 (`2011-fixed-2`) and 16 in 2025 (`2025-fixed`). In the long term, 36 and 32 regions were over capacity. The net fall of six regions in the short term is larger than the two flips that pass the tests below. The count of regions over capacity is therefore not a finding on its own.

Eight regions changed status in the short term: seven moved from over to under capacity and one, Corangamite, from under to over. Each flip was tested three ways (Table 1). The first asks whether the land layer alone moves the region's capacity by more than 2 times or less than 0.5 times, a sign that land was defined differently, not used differently. The second asks whether the 2011 zone was drawn on an older boundary. The third asks whether the 2025 status holds across the 5th to 95th percentile of the 2025 Monte Carlo range. Three flips are land-definition artefacts: Desert Channels, Southern Gulf and SA Arid Lands. Three are not robust to the 2025 range: Wet Tropics, Rangelands (Western Australia) and Corangamite. Two are clean and robust, both from over to under capacity: Mackay Whitsunday and North Tasmania.

The two clean flips carry qualifications. Mackay Whitsunday is under capacity in 2011 on the improved release (`2011-improved`), so it does not flip on that release. North East Victoria counted as a third clean flip until its 2011 population was found to be a copy of Goulburn Broken's 204,651. With the ABS figure of 98,604 it is under capacity in 2011 as well as 2025, so it does not flip. Section 7 takes up the four long-term flips.

Capacity grew most in the inland cropping and basin regions. The largest short-term gains were in Central West NSW (464 thousand people), Lachlan (456 thousand) and Namoi (441 thousand), all under capacity at both dates (`2011-fixed` to `2025-fixed`). Part of these gains comes from diet settings, consumption and the red meat mix, that apply in every region (section 4). People grew most on the coast and in the capitals: by 1.4 million in Port Phillip and Westernport, 1.3 million in Hawkesbury-Nepean-Sydney and 1.0 million in South East Queensland. Davies (2024, p. 153) also follows growth into regional cities and towns. Murrumbidgee-ACT, which holds Canberra, gained both, 348 thousand in capacity and 209 thousand in people. The capital-city regions are over capacity in both years and both presets, except Murrumbidgee-ACT, which is under capacity in the short term in both years. Swan, which holds Perth, is the clearest boundary case (Supplementary Section S1). Elsewhere on the coast the picture is mixed (Supplementary Section S1).

The cautions are these. Five zones carry older 2011 boundaries. The land-definition flag is a fixed threshold at 2 and 0.5 times. Robustness is tested on the 2025 range only (section 9). The artefact test is made on capacity, not on whether the population change is real; the North East Victoria correction shows that a population input can make or unmake a flip. The flips have not been tested against the method tier (section 9).

## 4. What drove the change

The change in capacity was taken apart on the corrected releases by a cumulative walk. The walk starts at `2011-fixed` and adds the 2025 data layers one at a time, in the order of the release chain: land, population, crop yields, livestock, consumption, the organic update, the resilience table, long-term liquid fuel and the red meat mix of the diet. It ends at `2025-fixed`. Each layer's effect is the capacity after it minus the capacity before it. The effects therefore sum exactly to the change, with no interaction term, and each effect depends on its place in the order. Kastner et al. (2012) split a change in land need into population, yield and diet effects; here population is not one of the causes.

Population does not enter capacity in this model; it changes whether a zone is over or under it. Nationally, people over capacity was 0.670 in the short term and 1.454 in the long term in 2011, and 0.665 and 1.767 in 2025 (Table 2). The short-term status held, while the long-term status worsened because the population grew and long-term capacity barely moved. The population layer moves capacity only through the infrastructure land that follows population, which changes capacity by 0 people nationally.

In the short term capacity rose by 8.2 million. Land took 2.6 million off. Livestock added 4.7 million, consumption 4.2 million and the red meat mix 1.8 million. Crop yields added 0.1 million, because short-term capacity is bound by pasture-fed meat and dairy rather than by field crops (section 6). The organic share is counted in the organic step, not in consumption; on the release chain as built, consumption would carry 3.8 million and the organic step 0.4 million, with the same sum.

In the long term capacity rose by 0.27 million. Land is the largest effect, at -1.2 million. Livestock added 0.6 million, the red meat mix 0.4 million, crop yields 0.2 million, the organic update 0.17 million and liquid fuel 0.09 million. The resilience table moved capacity by -7 people.

## 5. The scale curve: where the margin goes

This section asks how much of the national capacity is left when every unit feeds itself from its own land: the sum of the units' capacities as a share of the national capacity. It was computed for 7 states, 53 regions (52 NRM regions and the Northern Territory), 89 SA4s, 336 SA3s and 2,450 SA2s. Every unit kept the national settings for diet and waste. The curve in Figure 4 is on the state-yield basis: every unit took the yields, climate zone and settings of its state. This is the conservative bound; the zone results of sections 7 to 11 are on each zone's own yields, the local basis, and every regional sum below names its basis.

### 5.1 The curve and its stability

On the 2025 corrected release the states summed to 75% of the national short-term capacity, the regions to 44%, the SA4s to 58%, the SA3s to 44% and the SA2s to 37% (Table 3). On 2011 data the states gave 77%, the regions 44%, the SA4s 60%, the SA3s 46% and the SA2s 40%. The shares barely changed between the two data years. The long-term curve fell further at every level, to 20% for the SA2s in 2025 (Table 3).

[Figure 4: the scale curve, the sum of capacities as a share of national capacity at each level from the nation to SA2, 2011 and 2025, short and long term, state-yield basis, corrected releases (2011-fixed, 2025-fixed); from `results/scale_curve.csv`, drawn by `analysis/scale_curve_2025/plot_scale_curve.py` (`scale_curve.png`)]

The finding of Lane et al. (2015) that capacity falls as the unit shrinks (section 1.3) held on the corrected method. On 2011 data the national short-term capacity was 33.3 million, the states summed to 25.7 million and the 53 regions to 21.0 million. The regions figure is on each zone's own yields, not the curve's state basis, and the curve extends the finding to SA2. In Iran, capacity summed over local units fell below the national figure (Khorsandi et al. 2022), and a meta-model of those results related capacity to area with diminishing returns (Khorsandi et al. 2023). Existing US cropland could meet as much as 90% of national food demand within 100 miles on the standard diet (Zumkehr and Campbell 2015, p. 246).

### 5.2 The boundary effect and its cause

On the state-yield basis, the 53 regions lost as much as the 336 SA3s, 44% against 44% in the short term in 2025. They lost far more than the 89 SA4s, which kept 58%. Rebuilding the regions from whole SA2s gave the same 44%, so the gap is a property of the partition, not of the land data. The gap lay where rangeland meets cropland, in New South Wales, South Australia and Western Australia (Supplementary Section S2).

Section 6 gives the cause. South Australia's Arid Lands region holds 326,000 km2 of pasture and 221 km2 of cropland. On the state-yield basis its capacity was 41 thousand people, far less than SA4 406, which joins that arid country with the cropland of the Eyre Peninsula. On its own yields SA Arid Lands had a capacity of 28.6 thousand in 2025 (Table 7). Supplementary Section S2 gives the other stranded pasture. In the long term the gap between the regions and the SA4s was 12 points, 23% against 35%.

### 5.3 How much a better drawing of regions keeps

Random groupings and an ideal contiguous split bound how much a better drawing of regions would keep (Supplementary Section S2). The best contiguous split found at 53 regions kept 76% of national capacity in the short term and 69% in the long term. These come from two splits, each optimised for its own term; the short-term split kept 66.1% in the long term. The search was heuristic, so 76% and 69% are lower bounds on the best split.

### 5.4 Reading the curve

The curve is read for its shape, not for any single administrative SA2. For the 2011 cells the SA levels used land of 2020-21, against a reference of 30.7 million rather than the 33.3 million of the 2011 zones. The 2011 SA shares are therefore not exactly comparable with the zone levels. On the local basis the 2025 short-term share was 75% for the states and 54% for the SA2s. Yield differences within states were therefore worth about 17 points at SA2 level, 37% against 54%. The regions' local-basis share, used in sections 7, 8 and 11, differs from the curve's 44% for that reason.

## 6. The mechanism

One rule drives most of these results. A diet in the model needs cropland and pasture in nearly fixed proportion, so a unit's capacity is set by whichever land type runs short. Cropland can carry pasture-fed products when pasture falls short, but pasture is not necessarily fit for crops (Lane 2014, p. 266), and in the model it cannot carry them. Lane (2014, p. 3) found the share of cropping land to be the most significant determinant of capacity. In a national test on the 2025 corrected release, capacity was unchanged for every cropland share from 10% to 99% of the combined cropland and pasture area. The test was run for Australia only, so the rule is not shown for regions.

On the reference diet of the 2025 corrected release, pasture binds nationally. The diet needs 3.49 million km2 of pasture against 3.32 million km2 held. About 175,000 km2 of cropland therefore grazes animals, and 141,000 km2 grows food crops. No pasture is left unused; the 1.96 million km2 of unused land is non-agricultural land. This is why measures aimed at field crops barely move the 2025 short term while livestock output does (section 11). It is also why field-crop yields 24% lower took 0.2% off the short term, against 26% off at a 1-in-10 drought, which scales every yield (both on the 2025 corrected release).

On the 2025 corrected release a little pasture-fed meat beats none: a diet of 2.5% meat and eggs fed 118.0 million, against 112.4 million with none. On the 2025 improved release the diet with no meat or eggs was the highest point, at 103.6 million. The gain from a little meat therefore holds on the corrected release only.

A rangeland region cannot use its pasture without cropland, as SA Arid Lands shows (section 5).

The model has one pasture land type, and within a zone dairy and beef cattle are credited with the same average pasture yield. The dairy-on-good-land improvement moves dairy's land to the cropping column, at the observed yield of dairy farms, and it moves regions both ways (section 9). Grazing on land unsuited to crops is common ground with the wider literature: 65% of the grass ruminants eat grows on such land (Poore and Nemecek 2018, p. 990), and livestock on it use land that crops cannot (van Zanten et al. 2016; Roos et al. 2017).

## 7. The long term: a 100-year plan

The long-term settings follow Lane (2014) and Lane (2017). They plan for the worst year in 100 of weather. They set a low-meat diet, with meat and eggs at 7.5% of the diet against 12% in the short term. They assume no avoidable waste, full recycling of food waste, organic farming and liquid fuel cut from 2,360 to 210 litres a person. They also set activity level 3, three fibre settings, timber, infrastructure area and a 30% reserve. The land for the remaining fuel rests on a biodiesel yield of 400 litres per tonne of oilseeds (Geoscience Australia and ABARE 2010, p. 331).

### 7.1 National capacity in both years

On the corrected method the long-term capacity was 15.35 million on 2011 data and 15.6 million on 2025 data (Table 4). A plan on that basis would cover 69% of the 2011 population and 57% of the 2025 population. Capacity rose by 0.27 million while the population grew by 5.28 million. The change is smaller than the spread of the 2025 figure across input choices, which is not a test of whether it is real (section 9).

### 7.2 What each setting is worth

Each setting was put back to its short-term value with the others held (Table 5). The weather allowance was the largest. Without it the 2025 long-term figure would be 34.3 million rather than 15.6. Liquid fuel was the largest on the other side: with short-term fuel use the figure would be 3.7 million. The 2011 table has the same two at the top (33.7 and 2.6 million). The long-term figure is therefore mainly a statement about planning for bad years. No avoidable waste, the low meat-and-egg share, the lower red-meat share and the lower fibre need, from 23 to 11.5, each raise capacity. Organic farming and the natural-fibre and wool-fibre shares lower it. Timber, infrastructure area and the 30% reserve have no effect, because non-agricultural land absorbs them.

### 7.3 The regions

The cropping belts and inland plains feed themselves in the long term. In 2025 the strongest were South Coast WA, Avon, Border Rivers - Maranoa - Balonne, Western NSW, Border Rivers - Gwydir and Northern Agricultural. Their populations were 0.12 to 0.26 times their long-term capacity. Northern Gulf (which carries a land-definition flag), South West Qld and Lachlan also fed themselves. The long-term regional count also depends on the method tier (section 9).

### 8.3 Named scenarios

The named scenarios were run on the 2025 corrected release (2025-fixed), against a reference of 41.5 million in the short term and 15.6 million in the long term; Supplementary Section S3 and Table S2 give them all. The grain stores applied the resilience tables of the frozen store releases (2025-store-1, 2025-store-2). ### 8.4 What a single-year shock does not describe

A single-year shock does not describe a drought of several years. The 2017 to 2019 drought was exceptional as a three-year deficit (section 1.5), while its single years mostly fell within the expected range (Falster et al. 2024, pp. 6, 9). Bad wheat years are also partly correlated across the southern states (Anderson et al. 2019): ENSO and the Indian Ocean Dipole together explain 30 to 41% of wheat variability in Australia and East Africa.

An export-inclusive count sits beside the model's. Lawrence et al. (2013, p. 30) give about 22 million people fed at home and 40 million fed abroad, with no base year stated. PMSEIC (2010, p. 15) is the source of the 40 million abroad. PMSEIC's own figure is 60 million (p. 1), stated without source, and the report does not add the two. This count includes exports and the diet as eaten, so its basis differs from the model's self-sufficient capacity.

## 9. How sure are the regional results

### 9.1 The ranges and the status flips

This section tests the regional results for the 53 regions (52 NRM regions and the Northern Territory), each on its own yields (the local basis). The ranges come from the Monte Carlo of section 2, 1,000 Latin hypercube draws over 13 sampled input choices.

On the corrected 2025 release (2025-fixed), the 5th to 95th percentile of national capacity is 36.3 to 45.8 million people in the short term and 13.4 to 16.5 million in the long term. The long-term capacities of section 7, on 2011 and 2025 data, both lie inside the 2025 long-term range. The change between the two years is therefore smaller than the spread across input choices for 2025. That comparison is not a test of whether the change is real, because the 2011 figure has no range of its own.

Each status flip was tested against its region's 2025 range on 2025-fixed. A flip holds when the 2025 population lies outside the region's 5th to 95th percentile of capacity. The test covers the 2025 status only, because the 2011 inputs have no draws. Of the eight short-term flips of section 3, three are not robust to the 2025 range: Wet Tropics, Rangelands (WA) and Corangamite, the one region that moved from under to over capacity. Two pass every test in hand: Mackay Whitsunday and North Tasmania. Of the four long-term flips, one passes every test (section 7).

The method tier is not a flip test. It does move single regions: Corangamite changes short-term status between the corrected and improved 2025 releases (2025-fixed and 2025-improved), and Mackay Whitsunday does so on 2011 data.

Five range ends rest on the author's judgement and have no outside source. They are the three-way choice of the meat data window, with equal chances; the low end of the meat-and-egg share of the diet, 0.115; the low end of the red-meat share of meat, 0.528; the equal weight given to the leave-one-out resilience table; and the low end of the long-term feeding benchmark, 0.30. The last has no effect in the corrected and published 2025 releases. The choices whose ends or weights rest on judgement drive most of the width of the ranges; Supplementary Section S4 gives their shares of the variance.

### 9.2 What each method choice does, region by region

Each method choice was applied alone to the published-method release on 2025 data (2025-mirror), the published method of Lane (2014). Its effect is given as a share of the published capacity (Figure 7; Supplementary Section S4 and Table S3). Two modules are the corrections of section 2, the beef carcase yield and the biodiesel yield. The other six are improvements.

The two corrections act in different places. The beef carcase correction lowers the short-term national capacity by 16.0%, most in the beef and cropping regions, and leaves the long-term national figure unchanged. The biodiesel correction is a long-term correction, +20.7% nationally, and reaches +97.9% in South East Queensland. Dairy on land that can carry it is the largest improvement, +20.2% in the short term nationally, and it moves regions both ways.

## 10. Regions in depth

Five regions were chosen, one of each type, from the largest movers among the 53 regions (52 NRM regions and the Northern Territory): a capital with hinterland, a Murray-Darling Basin pair, a south-west Western Australian cropping region, Tasmania, and an arid region set beside a cropping neighbour. Each is read through the short term, the long term, drought and the 2025 range. All figures are on the corrected releases (2011-fixed and 2025-fixed) and on each region's own yields (Table 7). Supplementary Section S5 gives the full reading of Hawkesbury-Nepean-Sydney, the Murray with Murrumbidgee-ACT, Avon, and SA Arid Lands with the Eyre Peninsula.

Those four regions show what the counts mean on the ground. Hawkesbury-Nepean-Sydney is over capacity in both years and both terms. Even the 95th percentile of its 2025 short-term capacity is 48,520 people, against a population of 5,401,609. Its fall in capacity carries the land-definition flag: with only the 2025 land layer swapped in, capacity is 0.491 of the 2011 figure, just below the rule's lower bound of 0.5. Cropping land is 389 km2 on the 2011 layer and 191 km2 on the 2025 layer, on an unchanged boundary, so the fall is not read as a change in what the land can feed. James and O'Neill (2016, p. 183) found Sydney Basin farm data too uncertain to show a trend, with error margins above 25% in many ABS figures. The Murray is under capacity in both years and both terms. Murrumbidgee-ACT, which holds Canberra, is under capacity in an average year: its short-term capacity rose from 1,185,593 to 1,533,929, and its 2025 population is 0.482 times its capacity. It is over capacity in the long term (2.431 times) and at a 1-in-100 drought (1.604 times). The Basin accounts for about 40% of national farm output (Wittwer and Waschik 2021, p. 919), but the model carries no water limit (section 12). Avon is a cropping region under capacity throughout, and in the long term its 2025 population is 0.14 times its capacity. At a 1-in-100 drought its 2025 short-term capacity is 508,513 people, with yields scaled by Western Australia's worst-year factor. Hughes and Lawson (2017, p. 189) found a larger climate effect on cropping productivity in Western Australia than nationally: -7.63% against -4.71% for all cropping farms, 2000-01 to 2014-15 (their Table 8.3). SA Arid Lands moved from over to under capacity in the short term, but both changes are matters of definition. The land layer alone raises its capacity 6.5 times, and the 2025 population is allocated from estimated resident population by residential land. ## 11. Discussion: what would widen the margin

A measure is worth what it does to the constraint that binds. Each measure below was applied alone to the corrected 2025 release (2025-fixed), against a reference of 41.5 million in the short term and 15.6 million in the long term (Supplementary Table S5). Short-term capacity is bound by pasture-fed meat and dairy (section 6), so field-crop yields, irrigation and cropland taken from pasture move it by only 0 to 2.4%. Doubling cropland from non-agricultural land, the 1975 CSIRO case (Gifford et al. 1975, as reported in Henry and Godden 1995), raises both terms by 8.7%, to 45.1 and 17.0 million. Supplementary Section S6 gives these measures, which have no read source beyond the model; the five below have one.

### 11.1 Livestock productivity

Livestock output 10% higher raises the short term to 45.5 million (+9.7%) and the long term to 17.1 million (+9.4%), on 2025-fixed. Output 20% higher gives 49.6 million (+19.4%) and 18.6 million (+18.8%). Across five-year averages from 1981-85 to 2006-10, the average daily gain of steers in the national beef herd rose from 0.39 to 0.72 kg (Wiedemann et al. 2015, their Table 1, p. 111). Beef produced per breeding cow mated rose 32%, from 301 to 396 kg, mainly from heavier steers (p. 113). The industry forecasts in Mayberry et al. (2019) and Ridoutt (2024) concern animal numbers, not output per animal.

### 11.2 Diet composition

Setting the meat-and-egg share alone to 5% raises the short term to 76.8 million (+85.0%) and the long term to 19.6 million (+25.7%), on 2025-fixed. A red-meat share of 30% of meat, with more poultry and pork, gives 64.6 million (+55.6%) and 18.5 million (+18.6%). Paper B gives a different figure under the same label: 103.3 million on the improved release (2025-improved), for the guideline diet with 5% meat and eggs. The two differ in release and in what else the diet changes. Studies of Australian and global diets give the land and emissions of observed and recommended diets (Hendrie et al. 2014; Tilman and Clark 2014; Ridoutt et al. 2020).

### 11.3 Food waste

The model's 6% setting halves the model's retail-and-consumer loss share, a choice that mirrors UN Target 12.3. Australia's own goal is to halve food waste by 2030 across the whole supply and consumption chain (DCCEEW 2017, pp. 3, 8). The National Food Waste Baseline estimated 7.3 Mt of food waste for 2016-17 (DCCEEW 2019, p. 3). The 2021 Feasibility Study estimated about 7.6 Mt a year (DCCEEW n.d.). The two are estimates from different projects and are not combined here. Section 8 gives the effect of the 6% setting.

### 11.4 Grain reserves

The store sizes of one and two years are the model's own scenarios; no Australian source sets them. With the store resilience tables applied to 2025-fixed, a one-year grain store raises the long term by 48%, to 23.2 million, and a two-year store by 64%, to 25.7 million (section 8). A one-year store is worth the same as planning for a drought of about 16 years instead of 100, and a two-year store about 10 years. Both equivalents are read by interpolation between rows of the planning table. On one release, a two-year store (+64%) and drought-proofing (+60%, section 11.5) are the two largest long-term measures, and both act on the worst year. The store is free in the model.

A one-year store for domestic use is about 11.5 Mt, about a fifth of an average national cereal crop of 52.6 Mt (Supplementary Section S6). Most of it is feed grain embodied in meat, at 303 kg a person, a 2050 base-case figure of Foran and Poldy (2002) applied here to the 2025 population. Wright and Cafiero (2011, p. S72) discuss one or two years of supply for a dry importer, a different case. Reserves and trade together set exposure to supply shocks (Marchand et al. 2016).

### 11.5 Drought resilience

Drought-proofing the worst year, so that it loses half as much, moves the 100-year factor for Australia from 0.46 to 0.73 of trend. On 2025-fixed it raises the long term by 60%, to 24.9 million, and leaves the short term unchanged. The principles of farmer self-reliance and risk management have stood since the 1992 National Drought Policy (Botterill 2021). The Future Drought Fund embodies a shift to supporting preparedness and makes $100 million a year available for drought resilience (Productivity Commission 2023, pp. 3, v).

### 11.6 The regions as a pool

On each zone's own yields and on 2025-fixed, the national short-term capacity is 1.60 times the sum of the capacities of the 53 regions (52 NRM regions and the Northern Territory) in an average year; Supplementary Section S6 gives bad years and the long term. Trade in products between regions recovers at most about 15.5 million people-equivalents in the short term: the national 41.5 million less the regional sum of 26.0 million. In the long term it recovers 9.6 million. This is an upper bound, because it assumes products move without loss or cost. It is not the 15.5 million of Paper B, which is the sum of SA2 capacities.

Moving food has a cost. Road and rail transport of agriculture and forestry cost A$6.8 billion, 9.8% of farm gross value of production, in 2015-16 (Higgins et al. 2018a, p. 196). No Australian study of food flows between regions was found for this paper (Supplementary Section S6). Because bad wheat years are partly correlated across the southern states (Anderson et al. 2019; section 8), a pool of regions does not escape a widespread drought.

### 11.7 Measures the model cannot carry

The capacities assume that all production is eaten at home. In 2021-23 Australia produced 4.66 Mt of meat and ate 2.96 Mt, a surplus of 57% (FAO food balance). This is a different measure from the model's capacity of 1.50 times the population, and the two are not compared directly. Farmers produce some 93% of the food eaten at home (Lawrence et al. 2013, p. 32). Trade raises supply diversity but also import dependence (Kummu et al. 2020). Supplementary Section S6 gives the other measures the model cannot carry, among them the herd as a store and the state worst-year factors; water and fuel are limitations (section 12).

Three cautions apply. Each measure was applied alone, so the effects are not additive. Cost, time and feasibility are not modelled. Settings such as the 5% meat share bound what is possible, not what is likely.

## 12. Limitations and conclusions

The model is a closed land-use account for each region. It carries no trade, disease or biosecurity, and no limits from nutrients, soil or labour. It leaves out Indigenous food systems and wild harvest. Its named climate scenarios scale field-crop yields only; its drought allowance scales every yield by a grain-based factor that is untested for pasture. The 2011 inputs were not sampled, and five zones carry older 2011 boundaries.

### 12.1 Self-sufficiency as a policy concept

Self-sufficiency means production relative to consumption, so it does not rule out trade (Clapp 2017, p. 89). Local self-sufficiency is rarely possible: only 11 to 28% of the world's people could meet their demand for a given crop from within 100 km (Kinnunen et al. 2020, p. 229). Most foodshed studies measure capacity, and one hybrid study is Australian (Schreiber et al. 2021, p. 9). Australia is a surplus producer in some of the paths modelled by Beltran-Peña et al. (2020, p. 4). The closed region of this paper is therefore a bound, not a policy goal.

### 12.2 Urban agriculture and other adaptations

Under forced local self-sufficiency, diet raises capacity at every scale but does not close the scale loss. On 2025-fixed and the state-yield basis, the sum of SA2 capacities stays between 33% and 45% of the national capacity on the same diet (Supplementary Section S7). Swaps within food groups raise the national capacity by about three fifths (+62%) on the improved release (2025-improved). Gardens add little (2025-fixed). Mcdougall et al. (2020, p. 8) found that urban agriculture could supply 15.0% of food energy for the residents of the Sydney blocks they modelled, in their 25% land-use scenario, which adds street verges and vacant land to a quarter of yard space.

### 12.3 Water for food

The model has no water limit, so its irrigation results are an upper bound. Raising the irrigated share of cropland to 5% adds 2.4% to the 2025 short term (2025-fixed). In the Murray-Darling Basin, median projected streamflow falls about 20% under 2 °C of warming (Prosser et al. 2021, p. 4). Higher irrigation efficiency rarely lowers water use across a basin (Grafton et al. 2018b).

### 12.4 Fuel and energy in food production

The model carries fuel only through its liquid fuel setting, the second-largest long-term setting (section 7). With the biofuel share at 1 and the other settings at their short-term values, the 2025 short term falls from 41.5 to 9.7 million (2025-fixed). Australia's emergency oil stocks equalled 61 days of net imports against an obligation of 90 days (Soliman Hunter and Taylor 2021, PDF p. 11). Energy used after the farm gate is outside the model (Ladha-Sabur et al. 2019; D'Odorico et al. 2018).

**Conclusions.** First, national short-term capacity rose from 33.3 to 41.5 million on the corrected method. Livestock added 4.7 million people, food consumption data 4.2 million and the red-meat mix of the diet 1.8 million, while land took away 2.6 million and crop yields added 0.1 million. Population changes status, not capacity: people over capacity moved from 0.670 to 0.665 in the short term and from 1.454 to 1.767 in the long term. Second, a 100-year self-sufficient plan covered 69% of the population in 2011 and 57% in 2025. Third, the worst of ten years leaves 30.8 million against 27.6 million people in 2025. At a 1-in-20 year the population is 1.05 times capacity, and the failure probability a year is 5.8 to 6.1%, about 1 in 16 to 17. Fourth, two short-term flips pass the tests in hand (Mackay Whitsunday and North Tasmania), and one long-term flip (South West Queensland). The short-term count of regions over capacity is robust to the method tier, while the long-term count is not. Fifth, on the state-yield basis the sum of SA2 capacities is 37% of the national short-term capacity in 2025.
