## S1. Regional detail for sections 3 and 4

Swan, which holds Perth, is the clearest boundary case of section 3. Its short-term capacity falls from 170 thousand to 6 thousand while its population rises from 1.72 to 2.27 million (`2011-fixed-3` to `2025-fixed`).

North East Victoria counted as a flip until its thesis population, a copy of Goulburn Broken's 204,651, was replaced by the ABS figure of 98,604; it is then under capacity in 2011 as well as 2025. Mackay Whitsunday is under capacity in 2011 on the improved method, so it does not flip there.

Elsewhere on the coast the picture is mixed: Wet Tropics, Mackay Whitsunday, North Tasmania and Corangamite flip in the short term, while South Coast WA, Eyre Peninsula and Glenelg Hopkins are under capacity in both years in the short term.

The regional decomposition of section 4 (Figure 3) is on the corrected releases (`2011-fixed` to `2025-fixed`) and on the forward walk. In Central West NSW, Lachlan and Namoi most of the short-term gain is livestock: in Central West NSW livestock added 356,807 people to a capacity that rose from 1,333,553 to 1,798,009. Elsewhere inland the share differs: livestock carried 21% of the short-term gain in Murrumbidgee-ACT, and in the Murray it took 97,458 people off a gain of 153,025. In Swan the forward walk gives the land layer -167,976 against a 2011 capacity of 170,101, the boundary case of section 3, and the reverse walk -1,120,638. In Fitzroy consumption took 62,789 and the red meat mix 38,290 off short-term capacity, and in the long term crop yields added 34,818 there against 658 from livestock.

**The order of the layers.** Across the 53 regions the largest layer on the average of all orderings is land in 26 regions and livestock in 25 in the short term (consumption and crop yields in one each), and crop yields in 19, land in 17 and livestock in 16 in the long term (fuel in one). With land and livestock as one layer (eight layers), that layer is largest by absolute value in 42 regions in the short term and 31 in the long term (crop yields 7 and 20); the forward walk names a different largest layer from the average in 4 and 6 regions, the reverse walk in 2 and 2. The forward walk names a different largest layer from the average in 7 regions in the short term and 2 in the long term, and the reverse walk in 3 and 1. Infrastructure land moves capacity only in Northern and Yorke.

**The land and livestock overlap: Avon.** The land layer cuts Avon's pasture from 24,368 to 15,309 km2, a ratio of 1.592. The 2025 livestock yields divide output by the 2020-21 grazing area of the land layer plus hay, 15,950 km2, where the 2011 yields divided by 25,379 km2, the mean of the archive years 2006 to 2010: a ratio of 1.591. Avon's milk yield rises from 32.75 to 67.54 g/m2, 2.062 times, which is 1.591 times from the area and 1.296 times from output and the dairy share. Pasture times milk yield therefore changes only 1.296 times, and the area cancels. In the forward walk the land layer takes 134,628 people off Avon's short-term capacity and the livestock layer returns 125,905, together -8,723; in the long term the two are -68,404 and +52,121, together -16,283. For Australia the two ratios are 1.097 (pasture of the land layer, 3,639,794 to 3,318,689 km2) and 1.104 (the livestock denominator). Where feed grain is part of a yield's land (meat) or cropland binds, the cancelling is only partial.

## S2. The partition: boundary detail and the best contiguous split (section 5)

All figures in this section are on the 2025 corrected release (`2025-fixed`) and the state-yield basis unless named otherwise.

As shares of national capacity, the regions of New South Wales summed to 17.6% against 25.3% for its SA4s. In South Australia the figures were 1.2% against 4.8%, and in Western Australia 2.0% against 5.1%. The other states were about equal on the two partitions.

SA4 406 joins the arid country of SA Arid Lands with the cropland of the Eyre Peninsula and had a capacity of 1.7 million. On the state-yield basis the two regions apart had 41 thousand and 132 thousand (28,609 and 384,476 on their own yields, Table 7). In Western Australia the Rangelands region, with 646,000 km2 of pasture and 937 km2 of cropland, had a capacity of 241 thousand. SA4 511 had 1.5 million. Units with more than 1,000 km2 of pasture and cropland under 2% of that pasture held 71% of the nation's pasture among the regions (8 units), 72% among the SA3s (18) and 51% among the SA4s (3). The land the partitions left unused rose from 1.96 million km2 for the nation to 3.67 million for the SA4s and 4.50 million for the regions.

Capacity was exactly proportional to land: 0.5, 0.1 and 0.01 times the national land gave 100.0% of the same fraction of national capacity. A split in which every unit had the national land mix would therefore lose nothing. On the national yield vector, random groupings of the SA2s into 53 groups kept 68 to 81% of national capacity, against 33% for the 53 regions (52 NRM regions and the Northern Territory) as drawn. These are spreads across five draws, not uncertainty ranges. In 200 random groupings of the 53 regions into seven groups the median sum is 91% of national capacity on the national yield vector; the seven states keep 76% and the regions 33%, and the states keep less than 179 to 182 of the 200 groupings. On the state-yield basis a group takes one state's yields, so groups can exceed 100% of national capacity and the median depends on the rule for which state's yields a mixed group takes (85% by the state with most cropland, 58% by the state with most land); it is not quoted in the main text. Letting dairy use cropland at observed dairy-farm yields moves the SA2 share from 37.4% to 40.3% of the unchanged national figure (39.0% with the yield halved); against its own national figure the SA2 sum keeps 34.6%, so the loss from pooling is about two thirds under every treatment tested. The SA4s, at 54%, were slightly worse than random groupings of their number (56 to 69%), and the SA3s, at 35%, were about equal to random (34 to 44%). The best contiguous split found at 53 regions was searched with each region capped at 650,000 km2 of land (Supplementary Figure S1).

## S4. Ranges and method effects by region (section 9)

**The ranges.** The national and regional ranges come from 2,000 Latin hypercube draws of 19 sampled choices, run for all 60 zones; the earlier regional ranges, from 1,000 draws of 13 choices, are superseded for the flip tests. The 13 are as follows. Twelve of them vary in the corrected and published 2025 releases (2025-fixed and 2025-mirror); the thirteenth, the long-term feeding benchmark, has no effect in those releases and is drawn in the improved release (2025-improved), with a negligible national effect. On 2025-fixed the meat window alone explains 81% of the variance of the short-term national capacity and 36% of the long-term on these 13 choices. The national range of the main text comes from 2,000 draws of 19 choices: the 13, with six added. The six are avoidable waste (uniform from 12% to 14%), the retail and consumer loss factors (a weight from 0 to 1 between the thesis's US factors and Australian factors), the source of the land areas (ABS holdings or the ABARES land-use raster, with equal chances), the meat calibration (a weight from 0 to 1 between the ABS tables as published and the archive's 2010 level), the energy requirement per person (a food amount from 1.000 to 1.286 times the sedentary requirement, which is physical activity levels of 1.4 to 1.8 within a range of 1.2 to 2.2 in the source; the release's own diet has not been checked against a level of 1.4) and the crop-yield trend window (between the field-crop factors of 2022-25 and of 2016-21). Eighteen of the 19 vary, because the long-term feeding benchmark is fixed on this release. A definition changes what the number means (what is counted, the unit of self-sufficiency, the time frame, the diet standard, the weather allowed for) and is reported separately; a setting is a value within one meaning and is drawn. Where no source gives an end or a weight, it is the author's judgement.

**The weather factor.** The drought allowance is the resilience table of the model. For Australia and each state, each season's yield is the unweighted mean of the wheat, oat and barley yields in tonnes per hectare, from the ABS Historic Agricultural Commodities workbook (1860 to 2022) and the ABS 2022-23 to 2024-25 cubes: 153 seasons, those ending 1861, 1866 and 1871 and then every season from 1875-76 to 2024-25. Each season is divided by the mean of the ten observations from five before to four after it, held at the first or last ten at the ends. The 153 ratios are ranked from the worst, and rank k is read as a one-in-(n+1)/k year; the table takes anchors at 1, 3, 5, 10, 20, 30, 50 and 150 years and interpolates in a straight line between them. For Australia the 1-in-10 factor, 0.742, is the 15th-worst ratio (the season ending 1947), the 1-in-20 factor, 0.634, the 8th-worst (1896), and the 1-in-100 factor, 0.456, is the midpoint of the 50-year anchor (the 3rd-worst, 1941, 0.552) and the 150-year anchor (the worst, 1915, 0.359). The droughts of 2002-03, 2006-07 and 2019-20 appear as the seasons ending 2007 (0.567, the 4th-worst), 2003 (0.578) and 2020 (0.762, the 16th-worst). The model applies one factor to every yield of a zone, crops, pasture-fed and grain-fed animal products, fish, honey and timber alike. The 2011 releases use the thesis table (0.599 at 20 years).

## S5. The other four types of region in depth (section 10)

All figures except the method effects are on the corrected releases (2011-fixed-3 and 2025-fixed) and on each region's own yields (Table 7). The 2011 populations are the ABS figures of 2011-fixed-3.

**Hawkesbury-Nepean-Sydney.** The region is over capacity in both years and both terms. Its short-term capacity fell from 66,198 to 46,572 people while its population grew from 4,394,612 to 5,401,609. Its long-term capacity is 7,186 in 2011 and 6,106 in 2025. Even the 95th percentile of its 2025 short-term capacity, on the 19 choices, is 144,480 people. The fall in capacity carries the land-definition flag. With only the 2025 land layer swapped in, capacity is 0.491 of the 2011 figure, just below the rule's lower bound of 0.5. Cropping land is 389 km2 on the 2011 layer and 191 km2 on the 2025 layer, on an unchanged boundary. The change is therefore not read as a change in what the land can feed. James and O'Neill (2016, p. 183) found Sydney Basin farm data too uncertain to show a trend, with error margins above 25% in many ABS figures.

**The Murray with Murrumbidgee-ACT.** The Murray region is under capacity in both years and both terms. Its short-term capacity rose from 752,498 to 905,523 against a population of 105,924 to 121,808. Murrumbidgee-ACT, which holds Canberra, is under capacity in an average year: its capacity is 2.08 times its 2025 population. It is over capacity in the long term (capacity 0.41 times the population) and at a 1-in-100 drought (0.62 times). Its short-term capacity rose from 1,185,593 to 1,533,929 while its population grew from 597,750 to 738,895. In both regions the largest short-term method effects are measured on the published base (2025-mirror, section 9): dairy on good land (+17.8% and +17.9%) and the beef carcase correction (-15.0%). The Basin accounts for about 40% of national farm output (Wittwer and Waschik 2021, p. 919). Its median projected streamflow is about 20% lower under 2 °C of global warming (Prosser et al. 2021, p. 4). In northern Victoria, raw milk output fell by 1.3 billion litres between 2001-02 and 2009-10 (Sysak and Beilin 2017, p. 416). The model carries no water limit, so none of this enters the capacities (section 12).

**Avon.** Avon is a cropping region under capacity throughout. Its short-term capacity rose from 971,132 to 1,080,065 against a population of 60,913 to 69,727. In the long term it is among the strongest regions: its long-term capacity is 7.3 times its 2025 population. At a 1-in-100 drought its 2025 short-term capacity is 508,513 people. The model scales its yields by Western Australia's worst-year factor. Hughes and Lawson (2017, p. 189) found a larger climate effect on cropping productivity in Western Australia than nationally: -7.63% against -4.71% for all cropping farms, 2000-01 to 2014-15 (their Table 8.3).

**SA Arid Lands beside the Eyre Peninsula.** SA Arid Lands moved from over to under capacity in the short term. Its capacity on its own yields rose from 2,164 to 28,609 while its population fell from 10,613 to 8,049, both years on the same ABS estimated resident population and allocation (the thesis's 31,361 had no recorded source). The land layer alone raises its capacity 6.5 times, so the capacity change is a matter of definition and the flip is not read as a change on the ground. In the long term its 2025 status is not robust to the range: 3,122 to 8,814 people on the 19 choices against a population of 8,049. Its cropping neighbour, the Eyre Peninsula, carries no flag. There, capacity rose from 217,967 to 384,476 against a population of 57,595 to 59,255, and the region is under capacity in both years and both terms.

## S6. The measures of section 11, their sources and other detail

Each measure was applied alone to the corrected 2025 method unless Table S5 names another release.

**Livestock productivity.** Livestock output 10% higher raises the short term to 45.5 million (+9.7%) and the long term to 17.1 million (+9.4%). Output 20% higher gives 49.6 million (+19.4%) and 18.6 million (+18.8%). Across five-year averages from 1981-85 to 2006-10, the average daily gain of steers in the national beef herd rose from 0.39 to 0.72 kg (Wiedemann et al. 2015, their Table 1, p. 111), and beef produced per breeding cow mated rose 32%, from 301 to 396 kg (p. 113).

**Diet composition.** A meat-and-egg share of 5% gives 76.8 million (+85.0%) and 19.6 million (+25.7%). A red-meat share of 30% of meat gives 64.6 million (+55.6%) and 18.5 million (+18.6%). Studies of Australian and global diets give the land and emissions of observed and recommended diets (Hendrie et al. 2014; Tilman and Clark 2014; Ridoutt et al. 2020). On the 2025 corrected method a diet of 2.5% meat and eggs fed 118.0 million against 112.4 million with none; on the improved method the diet with no meat or eggs was the highest point, at 103.6 million.

**Food waste.** The model's 6% setting halves the model's retail-and-consumer loss share, mirroring UN Target 12.3. Australia's own goal is to halve food waste by 2030 across the whole chain (DCCEEW 2017, pp. 3, 8). The National Food Waste Baseline estimated 7.3 Mt for 2016-17 (DCCEEW 2019, p. 3) and the 2021 Feasibility Study about 7.6 Mt a year (DCCEEW n.d.); the two are from different projects and are not combined.

**Grain reserves.** The store sizes of one and two years are the model's own scenarios; no Australian source sets them. A one-year store raises the long term by 48%, to 23.2 million, and a two-year store by 64%, to 25.7 million. A one-year store is worth the same as planning for a drought of about 16 years instead of 100, and a two-year store about 10 years, by interpolation in the planning table. Reserves and trade together set exposure to supply shocks (Marchand et al. 2016).

**Drought resilience.** Drought-proofing the worst year, so that it loses half as much, moves the 100-year cereal factor for Australia from 0.46 to 0.73 of trend. It raises the long term by 60%, to 24.9 million. The Future Drought Fund makes $100 million a year available for drought resilience (Productivity Commission 2023, pp. 3, v).

**Cropland doubled.** Doubling cropland from non-agricultural land, the 1975 CSIRO case (Gifford et al. 1975, cited in Henry and Godden 1995), raises both terms by 8.7%, to 45.1 and 17.0 million.

**Field crops, irrigation and cropland.** Field-crop yields 10% and 20% higher raise the short term by 0.1% and the long term by 0.2% and 0.3%. Horticulture yields 10% higher leave the short term unchanged and raise the long term by 0.2%. All yields 10% higher raise both terms by 10.0%. An irrigated share of all farmland of 0.02 or 0.05, against a base of 0.007, raises both terms by 0.7% or 2.4%. Cropland 25% larger, taken from pasture, changes neither term. In the model, cropland can carry pasture-fed products but pasture cannot carry crops, so capacity is the same for any cropland share from 10% to 99% of the combined area (section 6).

**Diet, waste and fuel.** A meat-and-egg share of 7.5% gives 48.2 million (+16.2%) in the short term. No avoidable waste raises the short term to 55.3 million (+33.2%), and full recycling of food waste adds 2.2%. Liquid fuel of 156 litres a person, set in both terms, moves the short term by +0.1% and the long term by +0.8%.

**The grain store.** A one-year store for domestic use is about 11.5 Mt: 3.1 Mt for direct use at 112 kg a person, and 8.4 Mt for the feed grain embodied in meat at 303 kg a person.

**The regions as a pool.** On own yields the national capacity is 1.60 times the sum of the regional capacities in an average year. The factor is 1.82 at a 1-in-10 year. In the long term it is 2.60, and 3.00 on 2011 data (2011-fixed). The 15.5 million by which national capacity exceeds the regional sum is the pooling gain of the closed model, an upper bound because transport losses, costs and storage are absent. The regions' pasture and cropland complement each other. In an average year the regions over capacity are short by 20.7 million people, and the regions under capacity have 19.1 million to spare. At a 1-in-10 year the shortfall is 21.5 million and the spare 10.8 million. Northern supply chains run over 1000 km on roads cut by seasonal flooding (Higgins et al. 2018b, p. 341). Few studies follow shocks along supply chains (Davis et al. 2020).

**Measures the model cannot carry.** The capacities assume all production is eaten at home; trade raises supply diversity but also import dependence (Kummu et al. 2020). If meat output fell by the national capacity factor, it would be about 3.45 Mt at a 1-in-10 year and 2.95 Mt at a 1-in-20 year; that assumption is untested. Fish imports, 593 thousand tonnes, are about as large as the food use of fish, 641 thousand tonnes. In the commodity balance fish is the item that runs out first, at 12 to 17 million people. Pulses, 4.4 Mt produced and mostly exported, keep for a long time. The herd is also a store: the single-year capacity runs from 43.5 to 55.4 million across 2016 to 2025 with the herd cycle, on the 2025-organic release as built, not on 2025-fixed. The national 100-year worst-year factor, 0.46 of trend, is milder than those of New South Wales (0.30), Victoria (0.22), Queensland (0.35) and South Australia (0.35). It is not milder than those of Western Australia (0.47) or Tasmania (0.67). Spreading production across states therefore lowers national variability. In the long term 94% of the population lives in regions over capacity (2025-fixed; section 7). Settlement policy changes where food must travel, not the national capacity. Aquaculture, seasonal forecasting, household and community stocks, and finance and insurance are further measures the model cannot carry.

**Caution.** The drought, store and livestock results rest on one cereal weather factor applied to every yield.

## S8. The model, the data and the failure probability (sections 2 and 8)

**The model.** The model is the author's workbook (sheets Base, Yield, Diet, Recycle, Settings and resilience), set out in a doctoral thesis (Lane 2014) and three papers (Lane et al. 2014, 2015; Lane 2017). For each of its 60 zones it holds land areas (cropping, pasture, reserve, infrastructure and non-agricultural land), a population, yields in g/m2 for 135 rows (crops, animal products, fish, honey and timber) and the settings of Table S1. Capacity is the number of people whose diet the zone's own land can supply, once land for fuel, fibre, timber, infrastructure and a reserve is set aside. A diet needs cropland and pasture in nearly fixed proportion, so the land type that runs short sets capacity (section 6). Every yield row is the zone yield times one weather factor, so capacity is linear in the yields. Areas are in km2; yields are in g/m2 of product, the unit of the model's yield sheet.

**The data.** Table S11 names the source of each input in both years. The 2011 and 2025 populations are the same ABS series on the same allocation, so the two years differ by data, not by method.

**The failure probability.** At the base settings the model feeds the 2025 population of 27,608,200 when the weather factor is 27,608,200 divided by 41,503,537, which is 0.665. The table's factors for Australia are 0.742 at 10 years and 0.634 at 20 years, and it interpolates in a straight line in the return period between its anchors at 1, 3, 5, 10, 20, 30, 50 and 150 years, so 0.665 falls at 17.1 years: a base failure probability of 5.8% a year, about 1 in 17. Read linearly in the logarithm of the return period between the same anchors it is 16.4 years and 6.1%. The base value is one point in a range. Each of the 2,000 widened draws, with its average-year capacity mapped to a return period by the same rule, gives 2.3% to 19.4% a year (5th to 95th percentile, median 7.6%), or 2.4% to 19.5% (median 8.0%) in the logarithm; nine draws (0.45%) fail in an average year. The range rests on the cereal factor, the interpolation rule and the method tier.
