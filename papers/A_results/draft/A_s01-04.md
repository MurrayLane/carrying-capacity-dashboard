## Abstract

How many people can Australia's regions feed from their own land? The author's land-use model, method frozen, is run on 2011 and 2025 data for 53 regions, with no trade. At fixed settings short-term capacity rose from 33.3 to 41.5 million people and the population from 22.3 to 27.6 million; capacity stayed about 1.5 times the population. In all 2,000 paired draws on the seven choices both years share, 2025 capacity exceeds 2011; the long-term change is not established (26% of paired draws fall). Averaged over all orderings of eight data layers, the consumption and red-meat-mix layers add 5.6 million and land and livestock together 2.5 million; a ten-year diet window gives 39.1 million for 2025 (39.7 unrounded). A cereal-yield weather allowance on all output leaves 30.8 million in a 1-in-10 year; on crops and grain-fed animals only, 41.2 million. A long-term preset (low meat, organic farming, little fuel, worst year in 100) covers 57% of the 2025 population; weather and fuel settings explain most of the shortfall. Of eight short-term status flips none is both robust across 19 sampled choices and free of an artefact flag (SA Arid Lands keeps its status across the range and fails only on the land-definition flag); one long-term flip passes both. If every SA2 (ABS Statistical Area Level 2) must feed itself, capacities sum to 37% of the national figure on state yields (54% on own yields), a partition effect: cropland can carry pasture-fed food, but pasture cannot carry crops.

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 Australian population debate and planning

How many people Australia can feed from its own land is an old question. It returned as the population grew between 2011 and 2025. The 2023 Intergenerational Report projected 26.5 million people in 2022-23 rising to 40.5 million in 2062-63 (Treasury 2023, p. 39). The 2025 Population Statement put the population at 27.5 million in the March quarter of 2025 and forecast 41 million by 2065-66 (Centre for Population 2026, p. 1). Section 8.1 sets these projections against capacity.

Growth of 5 million over two decades "has been described as unexpected, unprecedented, and unplanned" (Davies 2024, p. 149), but "Australia's population growth is highly managed" (Davies 2024, p. 150). Those two decades are a longer span than the 14 years studied here, which added 5.3 million people. This paper sets capacity against each data year's own population, which rose by about 24% (section 3). Published estimates scatter widely, mostly because they answer different questions (Henry and Godden 1995; Foran and Poldy 2002; CSIRO and University of Queensland 2025).

### 1.2 The model, what was left open, and the questions

The model is the author's own. It was built for a doctoral thesis (Lane 2014), and its essential parameters were published (Lane et al. 2014). Lane et al. (2015) found that capacity shrinks as the unit of self-sufficiency shrinks from the nation to states and regions. Lane (2017) set a short and a long time frame side by side. The earlier papers gave one data year and, for the long term, one national and regional picture. Works citing them were listed, and one applies the model, a planning study of settling northern Australia (Bolleter 2018, p. 123).

The paper asks five questions. First, which regions moved between under and over capacity, and how much of the change in capacity is land, yields, livestock and diet, given that population changes status, not capacity? Second, what would the long-term preset, a low-meat, organic diet with little liquid fuel and the worst year in 100 of weather, ask of each region, and how has that changed? Third, how do the regional results hold under shocks? Fourth, how sure are the regional results? Fifth, how much of the national margin survives as the unit of self-sufficiency shrinks to the smallest statistical areas (SA2s), and has that changed since 2011? All results are for a closed model with no trade. A companion methods paper compares the published estimates and carries the choice of method.

### 1.3 Food security in Australian planning and policy

Food security and regional self-sufficiency appear in Australian planning and policy only in patches. The government's expert panel framed food security at the national scale (PMSEIC 2010). Of 102 food-related policy documents from Queensland, Western Australia, Victoria and the federal government, nine set food security as a specific goal, and none of the nine was federal or from Queensland (Summerhayes and Baker 2026b, pp. 963-964). The federal committee's 2023 inquiry made a National Food Plan its key recommendation (House of Representatives Standing Committee on Agriculture 2023, p. iii). A regional capacity result could inform such a plan: which regions could feed their own people from their own land.

### 1.4 Observed drought and climate effects on Australian farming

Since 1990 the climate has lowered the simulated water-limited yield potential of Australian wheat, by 27% to 2015 (Hochman et al. 2017, p. 2071). National actual yields did not decline over the same period (Hochman et al. 2017, p. 2078). The 2017 to 2019 drought was a three-year rainfall deficit across the Murray-Darling Basin, unprecedented in the instrumental record (Falster et al. 2024, p. 1). No annual national series of farm output across 2011 to 2025 was read; the national series read give an export share of 71% of production by volume (ABARES 2026b, p. 5) and farm incomes (ABARES 2026a, p. 2).

## 2. Method and data in brief

Capacity here is the number of people a region's land could feed on a stated diet and settings, with no trade in or out. One ratio is used throughout: capacity over population, the margin. Above 1 a region can feed itself; below 1 it is over capacity. The model, its constants and its construction, published from 2014 to 2017 (section 1.2), are summarised in Supplementary Section S8.

The model is run under two presets. The short term takes the diet and production of the data year in an average year. The long term, the long-term preset, takes the weather of the worst year in 100 with a low-meat diet, organic farming, no avoidable waste and 210 litres of liquid fuel a person; section 7 defines it. The model has 60 zones: the nation, seven state zones and 52 natural resource management (NRM) regions. The regional results use 53 regions (52 NRM regions and the Northern Territory), the 2011 NRM regions kept as a fixed partition in both years, not as the NRM regions of 2025.

The 2011 inputs are those of the thesis, except the populations; the 2025 inputs were brought forward layer by layer, in the order of section 4. The method is frozen, so within one method tier the change between 2011 and 2025 is a data change. A data year pairs the production data of its window with the population to be fed at 30 June of that year; Table 1 gives the reference periods and the distance from each window's centre to that date. Set against the population at the centres of the 2025 windows, capacity is 1.51 to 1.65 times the population (1.54 to 1.65 on the production windows; 1.58 on the livestock window), against 1.50 at 30 June 2025; the 2011 populations needed for the same check are not held. The diet inputs move by the change in FAO food supply for Australia between 2010-12 and the latest three years, 2021-23 (section 4 gives the reading on a ten-year window).

Both regional populations are the ABS estimated resident population at 30 June by 2021 SA2, one series for both years, shared among the zones by residential land where an SA2 straddles a boundary; the number of straddling SA2s and the population the allocation moves are not recorded. The 2025 national figure, 27,608,200, is the allocated SA2 sum, 304 below the ABS total for the same SA2s, not a rounding. The thesis's own 2011 regional figures had no recorded source and differed from the ABS figure by more than 10% of that figure in 24 of the 53 regions. The national 2011 population is the sum of the 53 regions, 22,336,514, which is 10,126 more than the thesis row, so national and regional figures share one base. Supplementary Section S8 lists the data sources by year.

The method has three tiers: published, corrected and improved. The corrected tier applies two corrections to the published method, the biodiesel yield per tonne of oilseed (Geoscience Australia and ABARE 2010, p. 331) and the carcase-to-retail yield of beef (USDA 1992; AUS-MEAT 2005), and for 2011 sets each state's population to the sum of its regions, which moves the state ratios but no capacity. The paper reports the corrected method on 2011 and 2025 data unless a figure names another tier;[^1] Supplementary Section S11 keys the release names of the data files. The named scenarios and grain stores of sections 8 and 11 are on 2025 data, each store taking its own resilience table.

The weather allowance scales every yield by one factor per state, taken from the record of wheat, oats and barley yields (Lane 2014, p. 193). It is a cereal factor. For Australia it is built from 153 seasons from 1861 to 2024-25, each season's yield over the mean of the ten observations around it, and its 1-in-10 and 1-in-20 values are 0.742 and 0.634 (the 2011 data use the thesis table, 0.599 at 20 years, except where section 8.1 puts both years on one table). Applying a cereal factor to pasture-fed output has not been tested; section 8.1 gives a crop-only bound.

The 2025 results carry Monte Carlo ranges over 19 sampled choices, of which 18 vary on the corrected method. An earlier set of 13 of them, without the land source, the energy requirement and four other data choices, is kept for comparison in Supplementary Section S4. A definition (what is counted, the unit, the time frame, the diet standard, the weather allowed for) changes what the number means and is reported separately; a setting is a value within one meaning, drawn over the range its source gives. Section 6 of the companion methods paper describes and tests the method; Supplementary Section S4 gives the shares. The 2011 results have no ranges of their own; section 9 pairs the two years on the seven choices they share.

[^1]: The 2011 values published in Lane et al. (2015) and Lane (2017) were computed before the two method corrections, before each 2011 state's population was set to the sum of its regions and before the regional populations were put on the ABS figures; correction notes to those papers set out the change. ERRATA.md in the repository lists ten errors in the thesis model and its papers; this paper corrects the biodiesel yield, which counted a seed's oil content twice (error 1), the beef carcase-to-retail yield of 1.0 (error 2) and the 2011 populations (errors 6 and 8 to 10).

## 3. The short-term picture, 2011 to 2025

On the corrected method, Australia's short-term capacity was 33.3 million people in 2011 against a population of 22.3 million. In 2025 it was 41.5 million against 27.6 million (Figure 1). Capacity was about 1.5 times the population in both years: 1.49 in 2011 and 1.50 in 2025. Capacity grew by 24.6% and the population by 23.6%.

[Figure 1: national short-term capacity against population, 2011 and 2025]

The regional picture moved more. Of the 53 regions, 22 were over capacity in the short term in 2011 and 16 in 2025. In the long term, 35 and 32 regions were over capacity. The net fall of six regions in the short term is the balance of seven flips to under capacity and one to over.

Eight regions changed status in the short term: seven moved from over to under capacity and one, Corangamite, from under to over. Each flip was tested three ways (Table 2). The first asks whether the land layer alone moves the region's capacity by more than 2 times or less than 0.5 times, a sign that land was defined differently, not used differently. The second asks whether the 2011 zone was drawn on an older boundary. The third asks whether the 2025 status holds across the 5th to 95th percentile of the region's capacity over all 19 sampled choices (section 9). On the full set of 19 choices none of the eight flips is both free of an artefact flag and robust to the range. Three are land-definition artefacts: Desert Channels, Southern Gulf and SA Arid Lands. Seven do not keep their 2025 status across the range: Wet Tropics, Desert Channels, Mackay Whitsunday, Rangelands (Western Australia), North Tasmania, Southern Gulf and Corangamite. SA Arid Lands keeps its status but is a land-definition case. "Robust" is used only for flips that pass on the full set.

The two flips that were clean and robust on 13 choices, Mackay Whitsunday and North Tasmania, have 5th-percentile capacities (136,000 and 149,000) below their 2025 populations (154,000 and 156,000), and 29% and 14% of the 2,000 draws put them over capacity (shares that depend on the equal chances given to the two land sources; section 9). The 2011 status is the base figure and carries no range. The long-term flip of South West Queensland is the one that passes: its 2025 status holds in every draw (lowest capacity 15,500 against a population of 7,110). Section 7 takes up the three long-term flips; Supplementary Section S1 notes the cases where the method tier or a corrected 2011 population removes a flip.

Capacity grew most in the inland cropping and basin regions. The largest short-term gains were in Central West NSW (464 thousand people), Lachlan (456 thousand) and Namoi (441 thousand), all under capacity at both dates. People grew most on the coast and in the capitals: by 1.3 million in Port Phillip and Westernport, 1.0 million in Hawkesbury-Nepean-Sydney and 1.0 million in South East Queensland. Figure 2 maps the change by region and marks the flips.

[Figure 2: change in short-term capacity and population by region, 2011 to 2025, with the status flips marked by test]

The capital-city regions are over capacity in both years and both presets, except Murrumbidgee-ACT, which is under capacity in the short term in both years. Swan, which holds Perth, is the clearest boundary case: the land layer alone takes its capacity to 0.01 of the 2011 value, because the 2011 zone was drawn before the Perth boundary change, so its 2025 status can be used but its 2011 status cannot (Supplementary Section S1).

Robustness is tested on the 2025 range only and on capacity, not on whether the population change is real, and the flips have not been tested against the method tier (section 9).

## 4. What drove the change

The change in capacity was taken apart layer by layer on the corrected method. The eight layers are the 2025 data added to the 2011 inputs one set at a time: land and livestock together, crop yields, consumption, the organic update, the resilience table, long-term liquid fuel, the red meat mix of the diet, and infrastructure land. Land and livestock are one layer because the 2025 livestock yields divide output by the 2020-21 grazing area of the land layer, so a smaller recorded grazing area lowers capacity in the land layer and raises the livestock yield by the same ratio; neither alone is read as a change in land use (Supplementary Section S1; Table S12 gives nine layers). A layer's effect depends on the order, so the walk was run forward (the 2025 layers added in the order in which the data were brought forward: land and livestock, crop yields, consumption, the organic share, the resilience table, fuel, the red meat mix, infrastructure land), in reverse (the opposite order) and in each of the 40,320 orderings. The main figures are the average over all orderings; the forward and reverse walks are the two ends of the chain, and the average lies between them in every row of Table 3. Each ordering's effects sum exactly to the change, so there is no interaction term.

Population does not enter capacity in this model; it changes whether a zone is over or under it. Nationally, capacity over population was 1.49 in the short term and 0.69 in the long term in 2011, and 1.50 and 0.57 in 2025 (Table 3). The short-term margin held, while the long-term margin fell because the population grew and long-term capacity barely moved.

In the short term capacity rose by 8.2 million. On the average of all orderings consumption added 4.15 million, land and livestock together 2.46 million, the red meat mix 1.43 million and crop yields 0.16 million. The consumption and red-meat-mix layers together add 5.6 million (68%). The red-meat share of meat fell from 0.64 to 0.578, and FAO food supply (availability, not intake) of beef and veal from 39.1 to 25.9 kg a person between 2010-12 and 2021-23; no second consumption series is held to corroborate the size of that fall. Fuel, biofuel and timber settings move the consumption layer by 0.2% or less, so it is almost wholly diet.

The diet layers rest on the 2021-23 window, the lowest-beef window of the FAO series, and beef supply falls over the series unevenly (Supplementary Section S10). On the ten-year window 2014 to 2023 national short-term capacity is 39.1 million (39.7 million on unrounded diet inputs) instead of 41.5. The choice of window is worth about 2.4 million, about 30% of the 8.2 million rise (on the release's rounded inputs; 22% unrounded), and 2025 capacity is still 17% to 19% above 2011's. This window effect is measured with the three diet inputs changed alone on 2025 data, and is not the same quantity as the 5.6 million of Table 3.

A change of definition could reach only a small part of the land and livestock layer. The ABS changed its land-use categories in 2016, and the grazing area it records for Australia fell from 3.64 million km2 (mean of four years to 2009-10) to 3.32 million in 2020-21; changes to its scope threshold and frame after 2008-09 are not documented in the sources read here (Supplementary Section S10). If the whole fall were definitional, then with output and diet held fixed it would account for about 0.19 million of the 2.46 million that land and livestock add (8%), an upper bound under those assumptions; the rest of the layer is mostly output. Crop yields add little, because short-term capacity is bound by pasture-fed meat and dairy, not field crops (section 6).

In the long term capacity rose by 0.27 million. Land and livestock together are the largest layer, taking 0.60 million off on the average. The red meat mix added 0.36 million, crop yields 0.24 million, the organic update 0.18 million and liquid fuel 0.10 million.

Figure 3 gives the effect of each layer in each of the 53 regions, in both presets, on the forward walk; Supplementary Section S1 gives the regional detail. On the average land and livestock together are the largest layer by absolute value in 42 of the 53 regions in the short term and 31 in the long term (crop yields in 7 and 20).

[Figure 3: effect of each layer on capacity by region, short and long term, forward walk, corrected method]

Two caveats apply. The livestock layer rests on a five-year window of herd and meat data, 2020-21 to 2024-25, that ends in two years of heavy slaughter (section 9 gives the range on full herd cycles). The 2011 yields of crops and animal products, rebuilt on each three-year subset of 2005-06 to 2009-10, give 2011 short-term capacity of 32.7 to 34.0 million, with 2025 above it by 22% to 27% on every subset; crops alone move it by under 0.1 million (Supplementary Section S10).
