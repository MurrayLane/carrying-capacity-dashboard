## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 Australian population debate and planning

How many people Australia can plan to feed from its own land is an old question. It returned as the population grew between 2011 and 2025. The government's framing of that growth is set by the Intergenerational Reports and the Population Statement. The 2021 report projected 38.8 million people in 2060-61, the first downward revision of the projection in an intergenerational report (Treasury 2021, p. 13). The 2023 report projected growth from 26.5 million people in 2022-23 to 40.5 million in 2062-63 (Treasury 2023, p. 39). It put average growth over the next 40 years at 1.1% a year, against 1.4% over the past 40 years (Treasury 2023, p. 37). The 2025 Population Statement put the population at 27.5 million in the March quarter of 2025 and forecast 41 million by 2065-66 (Centre for Population 2025, p. 1).

Davies (2024) sets out both readings of this growth. Growth of 5 million over two decades "has been described as unexpected, unprecedented, and unplanned" (Davies 2024, p. 149). In Davies's own account, "Australia's population growth is highly managed" (Davies 2024, p. 150). Parr (2018, p. 16) notes that growth concentrated in Sydney, Melbourne and Brisbane. McDonald (2017, p. 3) finds that most of the rise in employment from July 2011 to July 2016 came from immigrants; his measure is employment, not population.

This paper sets capacity against each data year's own population. That population was 22.3 million in 2011 and 27.6 million in 2025, a rise of about 24%. Published estimates of how many people Australia can feed scatter widely, because they answer different questions by different methods (Henry and Godden 1995; Foran and Poldy 2002; CSIRO and University of Queensland 2025). A companion methods paper compares those methods.

### 1.4 Food security in Australian planning and policy

Food security and regional food self-sufficiency appear in Australian planning and policy only in patches. The government's expert panel framed Australian food security at the national scale (PMSEIC 2010). Of 102 food-related policy documents from Queensland, Western Australia, Victoria and the federal government, nine set food security as a specific goal, and none of the nine was federal or from Queensland (Summerhayes and Baker 2026, pp. 963-964). In the same set of documents, food-related urban planning received little attention, and policy was weakly integrated across levels of government (Summerhayes and Baker 2024, p. 1). Of 25 senior planners and policymakers interviewed, 21 agreed that food is a stranger to urban planning (Summerhayes and Baker 2025, p. 534). For the Sydney Basin, the farmland data were too inconsistent to say how much land is farmed (James and O'Neill 2016, p. 187). Lawrence, Richards and Lyons (2013, p. 30) argue that productivist, export-oriented farming cannot deliver the long-term production that national food security needs. The federal committee's 2023 inquiry made a National Food Plan its key recommendation (House of Representatives Standing Committee on Agriculture 2023, p. iii). A regional capacity result answers the question a regional food plan would ask: which regions could feed their own people from their own land.

### 1.5 Observed drought and climate effects on Australian farming

Since 1990 the climate has lowered the simulated water-limited yield potential of Australian wheat, which fell by 27% over 1990 to 2015 (Hochman 2017, p. 2071). National actual yields did not decline over the same period (Hochman 2017, p. 2078). Simulated farm profit has also fallen since 2001: a farm model put average broadacre profit under the 2001 to 2020 climate 22.6% below profit under the 1950 to 2000 climate (Hughes 2022, p. 11). ABARES modelling put the effect of the 2001 to 2023 seasons, against 1951 to 2000, at 18% of average broadacre profit (ABARES undated, p. 10). Both are simulated profit, not observed output. The 2017 to 2019 drought was a three-year rainfall deficit across the Murray-Darling Basin, unprecedented in the instrumental record (Falster 2024, p. 1). A regional economic model put the drought's national welfare loss at a present value of $53 billion, the authors' model estimate (Wittwer 2021, p. 932). The national series read for this paper give the value of production, an export share of 71% of production by volume in the three years to 2024-25 (ABARES undated, p. 5), and farm incomes from 2005-06 to 2024-25 (ABARES 2025, p. 2). None of them is an annual series of farm output, and no source read here measures farm output across 2011 to 2025 itself. The drought allowance of section 8 tests the margin against this dependence on the weather.

### 1.3 The model, what was left open, and the questions

The model is the author's own. It was built for a doctoral thesis (Lane 2014), and its essential parameters were published (Lane, Dawes and Grace 2014). Lane, Dawes and Grace (2015) found that capacity shrinks as the unit of self-sufficiency shrinks from the nation to states and regions. Lane (2017) set a short and a long time frame side by side. A planning study has applied Lane (2017) to scenarios for settling northern Australia (Bolleter 2018, p. 123). The earlier papers gave one data year and, for the long term, one national and regional picture. They left open how the picture changes over time, how robust the regional results are, and how the long term and shocks play out region by region.

This paper brings the whole picture forward from 2011 to 2025 with the method frozen. It asks five questions. First, which regions moved between under and over capacity, and how much of the change in capacity is land, yields, livestock and diet, given that population changes status, not capacity? Second, what would a 100-year self-sufficiency plan ask of each region, and how has that changed? Third, how do the regional results hold under shocks? Fourth, how sure are the regional results? Fifth, how much of the national margin survives as the unit of self-sufficiency shrinks from the nation to the smallest statistical areas, and has that changed since 2011? Sections 3 and 4 answer the first question, section 7 the second, section 8 the third, section 9 the fourth, and sections 5 and 6 the fifth. Sections 10 to 12 take selected regions in depth, the measures that would widen the margin, and the limits. The companion methods paper carries the comparison of methods and the choice of method.

## 2. Method and data in brief

Capacity here is the number of people a region's land could feed on a stated diet and settings, with no trade into or out of the region. A region is over capacity when its population exceeds that number. Status is reported as people over capacity, so a value above 1 is over capacity.

The model is the author's own. It was built for the thesis and published from 2014 to 2017 (Lane 2014; Lane, Dawes and Grace 2014; Lane, Dawes and Grace 2015; Lane 2017). The full model, its constants and its construction method are in the supporting information.

The model is run under two presets. The short term takes the diet and production of the data year in an average year. The long term takes the settings of a 100-year self-sufficiency plan, defined in section 7. The settings of both presets in both years are tabled in the supporting information (Table S1).

The model has 60 zones: the nation, seven state zones and 52 NRM regions. The regional results use 53 regions (52 NRM regions and the Northern Territory).

The 2011 inputs are those of the thesis. The 2025 inputs were brought forward layer by layer: land, population, crop yields, livestock, consumption, the organic share, the resilience table, liquid fuel and the diet. Each layer is a release whose inputs are recorded by hash. The method is frozen, so within one method tier the change between 2011 and 2025 is a data change.

The method has three tiers: published, corrected and improved. The corrected tier applies two corrections to the published method. One is the biodiesel yield per tonne of oilseed (Geoscience Australia and ABARE 2010, p. 331). The other is the carcase-to-retail yield of beef (USDA undated; AUS-MEAT 2005). The paper reports the corrected releases, `2011-fixed` and `2025-fixed`, unless a figure names another release. The 2011 results appear as corrected only.[^1] The regional 2011 results use `2011-fixed-2`, which corrects the 2011 population of North East Victoria and leaves every capacity unchanged (section 3). The method effects of section 9 are measured on the published base, `2025-mirror`. The diet swaps of section 12 are on the improved release, `2025-improved`. The herd-cycle range of section 11 is on `2025-organic`. The named scenarios and grain stores of sections 8 and 11 are on `2025-fixed`, with each store taking the resilience table of the frozen release `2025-store-1` or `2025-store-2`. The scale curve of section 5 evaluates every unit on its state's yields, the conservative bound; the zone results of sections 7 to 11 use each zone's own yields.

The drought allowance scales yields by one factor per state, taken from the 150-year record of wheat, oats and barley (Lane 2014). The national figure takes the Australian factor. Regions take their state's factor, so the regions of one state share it. The five regions that tip over capacity at a 1-in-10 year in 2025 lie in three states. Applying a factor derived from cereal yields to pasture-fed output has not been tested, and section 8 says so.

The 2025 results carry Monte Carlo ranges. The Monte Carlo samples 13 choices, of which 12 vary in the 2025 corrected and published releases. The method is described and tested in section 6 of the companion methods paper and only used here. The 2011 results have no ranges.

[^1]: The 2011 values published in Lane, Dawes and Grace (2015) and Lane (2017) were computed before the two corrections; the values in this paper are the corrected ones, and correction notes to those papers set out the change.

## 3. The short-term picture, 2011 to 2025

On the corrected releases, Australia's short-term capacity was 33.3 million people in 2011 (`2011-fixed`) against a population of 22.3 million. In 2025 (`2025-fixed`) it was 41.5 million against 27.6 million (Figure 1). Capacity was about 1.5 times the population in both years: 1.49 times in 2011 and 1.50 times in 2025. Between the two years capacity grew by 24.6% and the population by 23.7%, so the national margin held its multiple.

[Figure 1: national short-term capacity against population, 2011 (`2011-fixed`) and 2025 (`2025-fixed`), from `results/comparison_cells.csv`, Australia rows, columns `capacity_2011_corrected`, `capacity_2025_corrected`, `population_2011`, `population_2025`]

The regional picture moved more than the national one. Of the 53 regions (52 NRM regions and the Northern Territory), 22 were over capacity in the short term in 2011 (`2011-fixed-2`) and 16 in 2025 (`2025-fixed`). In the long term, 36 and 32 regions were over capacity. The net fall of six regions in the short term is larger than the two flips that pass the tests below. The count of regions over capacity is therefore not a finding on its own.

Eight regions changed status in the short term: seven moved from over to under capacity and one, Corangamite, from under to over. Each flip was tested three ways (Table 2). The first test asks whether the land layer alone moves the region's capacity by more than 2 times or less than 0.5 times, which marks a change in how land is defined rather than in how it is used. The second asks whether the 2011 zone was drawn on an older boundary. The third asks whether the 2025 status holds across the 5th to 95th percentile of the 2025 Monte Carlo range. Three flips are land-definition artefacts: Desert Channels, Southern Gulf and SA Arid Lands. Three are not robust to the 2025 range: Wet Tropics, Rangelands (Western Australia) and Corangamite. Two are clean and robust: Mackay Whitsunday and North Tasmania, both from over to under capacity.

The two clean flips carry qualifications. Mackay Whitsunday is under capacity in 2011 on the improved release (`2011-improved`), so it does not flip on that release. North Tasmania's 2011 status sits close to balance, at 144,582 people against a capacity of 139,165 (`2011-fixed-2`), and the 2011 inputs have no range. North East Victoria counted as a third clean flip until its 2011 population was found to be a copy of Goulburn Broken's 204,651. With the ABS figure of 98,604 it is under capacity in 2011 as well as 2025, so it does not flip. In the long term four regions flip, all from over to under, and one is clean and robust, South West Queensland; section 7 returns to them.

Capacity grew most in the inland cropping and basin regions. The largest short-term gains were in Central West NSW (464 thousand people), Lachlan (456 thousand) and Namoi (441 thousand), all under capacity at both dates (`2011-fixed` to `2025-fixed`). Part of these gains comes from diet settings, consumption and the red meat mix, that apply in every region (section 4). People grew most on the coast and in the capitals: by 1.4 million in Port Phillip and Westernport, 1.3 million in Hawkesbury-Nepean-Sydney and 1.0 million in South East Queensland. Davies (2024, p. 153) follows growth into regional cities and towns; among the model's regions, the largest gains in people were in the capital-city regions. Murrumbidgee-ACT, which holds Canberra, gained both, 348 thousand in capacity and 209 thousand in people. Figure 2 maps the change by region and marks the flips.

[Figure 2: change in short-term capacity and population by region, 2011 (`2011-fixed-2`) to 2025 (`2025-fixed`), with the status flips marked by test, from `analysis/paper_a/regional_flips.csv` and `analysis/comparison_2025/movers.csv`]

The capital-city regions are over capacity in both years and both presets, except Murrumbidgee-ACT, which is under capacity in the short term in both years. Swan, which holds Perth, is the clearest boundary case. Its short-term capacity falls from 170 thousand to 6 thousand while its population rises from 1.70 to 2.27 million. The land layer alone takes its capacity to 0.01 of the 2011 value, because the 2011 zone was drawn before the Perth boundary change. Swan's 2025 status can be used, but its 2011 status cannot. Elsewhere on the coast the picture is mixed: Wet Tropics, Mackay Whitsunday, North Tasmania and Corangamite flip in the short term, while South Coast WA, Eyre Peninsula and Glenelg Hopkins are under capacity in both years.

The cautions are these. Five zones carry older 2011 boundaries. The land-definition flag is a fixed threshold at 2 and 0.5 times. Robustness is tested on the 2025 range only, because the 2011 inputs were not sampled. The artefact test is made on capacity, not on whether the population change is real, and the North East Victoria correction shows that a population input can make or unmake a flip. The flips have not been tested against the method tier, which the Mackay Whitsunday case shows can matter.

## 4. What drove the change

The change in capacity between 2011 and 2025 was taken apart on the corrected releases by a cumulative walk. The walk starts at `2011-fixed` and adds the 2025 data layers one at a time, in the order of the release chain: land, population, crop yields, livestock, consumption, the organic update, the resilience table, long-term liquid fuel and the red meat mix of the diet. It ends at `2025-fixed`. Each layer's effect is the capacity after it minus the capacity before it. The effects therefore sum exactly to the change, with no interaction term, and each effect depends on its place in the order. Kastner (2012) split a change in land need into population, yield and diet effects; here population is not one of the causes.

Population does not enter capacity in this model; it changes whether a zone is over or under its capacity. Nationally, people over capacity was 0.670 in the short term and 1.454 in the long term in 2011, and 0.665 and 1.767 in 2025 (Table 3). The short-term status held, while the long-term status worsened because the population grew and long-term capacity barely moved. The population layer moves capacity only through the infrastructure land that follows population, which changes capacity by 0 people nationally.

In the short term capacity rose by 8.2 million. Land took 2.6 million off. Livestock added 4.7 million, consumption 4.2 million and the red meat mix 1.8 million. Crop yields added 0.1 million, because short-term capacity is bound by pasture-fed meat and dairy rather than by field crops (section 6). The organic share is counted in the organic step, not in consumption; on the release chain as built, consumption would carry 3.8 million and the organic step 0.4 million, with the same sum.

In the long term capacity rose by 0.27 million. Land is the largest effect, at -1.2 million. Livestock added 0.6 million, the red meat mix 0.4 million, crop yields 0.2 million, the organic update 0.17 million and liquid fuel 0.09 million. The resilience table moved capacity by -7 people.

Figure 3 gives the effect of each layer in each of the 53 regions (52 NRM regions and the Northern Territory), in both presets. In the inland cropping regions the short-term gain is mostly livestock: in Central West NSW livestock added 356,807 people to a capacity that rose from 1,333,553 to 1,798,009. In Swan the land layer took 167,976 off a 2011 capacity of 170,101, the boundary case of section 3. The diet layers that raise the national figure can lower a region's figure. In Fitzroy consumption took 62,789 and the red meat mix 38,290 off short-term capacity, and in the long term crop yields added 34,818 there against 658 from livestock.

[Figure 3: effect of each layer on capacity by region, short and long term, corrected releases (`2011-fixed` to `2025-fixed`), from `analysis/comparison_2025/capacity_by_cause_corrected.csv`, zone rows]

Three caveats apply. The livestock layer rests on a five-year window of herd and meat data, 2020-21 to 2024-25, that ends in two years of heavy slaughter. Five zones carry older 2011 boundaries. The crop layer includes two very good winter-crop years, but on this walk it weighs little, adding 115,575 people in the short term and 219,940 in the long term.
