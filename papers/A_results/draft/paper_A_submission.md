# Australia's carrying capacity 2011 to 2025 by region on the corrected releases

## Abstract

The author's land-use model, method frozen, is run on 2011 and 2025 data for 53 regions, with no trade. At fixed settings short-term capacity rose from 33.3 to 41.5 million and the population from 22.3 to 27.6 million; capacity stayed about 1.5 times the population. In all 2,000 paired draws on the seven shared choices, 2025 short-term capacity is higher; the long-term change is not established (26% of draws fall once the 2025-only choices are drawn). Averaged over all orderings of eight layers, consumption and red-meat-mix layers add 5.6 million and land and livestock 2.5 million; a ten-year diet window gives 39.1 million (39.7 on unrounded inputs). A cereal-yield weather allowance on all output leaves 30.8 million in a 1-in-10 year, with capacity below the population in 37% of draws; on crops and grain-fed animals only, 41.2 million. A long-term preset (low meat, organic farming, little fuel, worst year in 100) covers 57% of the 2025 population; only the weather setting explains the shortfall (34.3 million without it), while low fuel raises capacity. None of eight short-term status flips is both robust across 19 choices and free of an artefact flag (SA Arid Lands keeps its status but fails the land-definition flag); one long-term flip passes both. If every SA2 (ABS Statistical Area Level 2) must feed itself, capacities sum to 37% of the national figure on state yields (54% on own yields), a partition effect argued to follow from the model's rule that pasture cannot carry crops.

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 Australian population debate and planning

How many people Australia can feed from its own land is an old question. It returned as the population grew between 2011 and 2025. The 2023 Intergenerational Report projected 26.5 million people in 2022-23 rising to 40.5 million in 2062-63 (Treasury 2023, p. 39). The 2025 Population Statement put the population at 27.5 million in the March quarter of 2025 and forecast 41 million by 2065-66 (Centre for Population 2026, p. 1). Section 8.1 sets these projections against capacity.

Growth of 5 million over two decades "has been described as unexpected, unprecedented, and unplanned" (Davies 2024, p. 149), but "Australia's population growth is highly managed" (Davies 2024, p. 150). Those two decades exceed the 14 years studied here, which added 5.3 million people, about 24% (section 3). Published estimates scatter widely, mostly because they answer different questions (Henry and Godden 1995; Foran and Poldy 2002; CSIRO and University of Queensland 2025).

### 1.2 The model, what was left open, and the questions

The model is the author's own. It was built for a doctoral thesis (Lane 2014), and its essential parameters were published (Lane et al. 2014). Lane et al. (2015) found that capacity shrinks as the unit of self-sufficiency shrinks from the nation to states and regions. Lane (2017) set a short and a long time frame side by side. The earlier papers gave one data year and, for the long term, one national and regional picture. Works citing them were listed, and one applies the model, a planning study of settling northern Australia (Bolleter 2018, p. 123).

The paper asks five questions. First, which regions moved between under and over capacity, and how much of the change in capacity is land, yields, livestock and diet, given that population changes status, not capacity? Second, what would the long-term preset, a low-meat, organic diet with little liquid fuel and the worst year in 100 of weather, ask of each region, and how has that changed? Third, how do the regional results hold under shocks? Fourth, how sure are the regional results? Fifth, how much of the national margin survives as the unit of self-sufficiency shrinks to the smallest statistical areas (SA2s), and has that changed since 2011? All results are for a closed model with no trade. A companion methods paper compares the published estimates and carries the choice of method.

Cohen (1995, p. 343) held that carrying capacity cannot be defined for a trading nation independently of other regions, so the results here are for a closed model. Peters et al. (2016, pp. 1, 9) found that US agricultural land could feed 402 to 807 million people across ten diets without trade, 1.3 to 2.6 times its 2010 population; Zumkehr and Campbell (2015, p. 247) that cropland within 50 miles of consumers could feed 82% of the US population on a standard diet in 2000. Fader et al. (2013, p. 7) found 66 countries unable to produce all the crops they consume within their land and water; Gerten et al. (2020) that a transformed food system could feed 10.2 billion people within four planetary boundaries. Australia's national margin of about 1.5 lies within the range of Peters et al.; this paper adds regional results and the loss as units shrink.

### 1.3 Food security in Australian planning and policy

Food security and regional self-sufficiency appear in Australian planning and policy only in patches. Of 102 food-related policy documents from Queensland, Western Australia, Victoria and the federal government, nine set food security as a specific goal, and none of the nine was federal or from Queensland (Summerhayes and Baker 2026, pp. 963-964). The federal committee's 2023 inquiry made a National Food Plan its key recommendation (House of Representatives Standing Committee on Agriculture 2023, p. iii). A regional capacity result could inform such a plan.

### 1.4 Observed drought and climate effects on Australian farming

Since 1990 the climate has lowered the simulated water-limited yield potential of Australian wheat, by 27% to 2015, while national actual yields did not decline (Hochman et al. 2017, pp. 2071, 2078). The 2017 to 2019 drought was a three-year rainfall deficit across the Murray-Darling Basin, unprecedented in the instrumental record (Falster et al. 2024, p. 1). No annual national series of farm output across 2011 to 2025 was read; section 6 gives the one check made, a meat balance. Exports were 71% of production by volume (ABARES 2026, p. 5).

## 2. Method and data in brief

Capacity here is the number of people a region's land could feed on a stated diet and settings, with no trade in or out. One ratio is used throughout: capacity over population, the margin. Above 1 a region can feed itself; below 1 it is over capacity. The model, its constants and its construction, published from 2014 to 2017 (section 1.2), are summarised in Supplementary Section S8.

The model is run under two presets. The short term takes the diet and production of the data year in an average year. The long term, the long-term preset, takes the weather of the worst year in 100 with a low-meat diet, organic farming, no avoidable waste and 210 litres of liquid fuel a person; section 7 defines it. The model has 60 zones: the nation, seven state zones and 52 natural resource management (NRM) regions. The regional results use 53 regions (52 NRM regions and the Northern Territory), the 2011 NRM regions kept as a fixed partition in both years, not as the NRM regions of 2025.

The 2011 inputs are those of the thesis, except the populations; the 2025 inputs were brought forward layer by layer, in the order of section 4. The method is frozen, so within one method tier the change between 2011 and 2025 is a data change. A data year pairs the production data of its window with the population to be fed at 30 June of that year; Table 1 gives the reference periods and the distance from each window's centre to that date. Set against the population at the centres of the 2025 windows, capacity is 1.51 to 1.65 times the population (1.54 to 1.65 on the production windows; 1.58 on the livestock window), against 1.50 at 30 June 2025, so the 30 June pairing gives the lowest margin; the same check for 2011 needs populations at 30 June 2007 and 2008, which the ABS publishes and this paper does not use. The diet inputs move by the change in FAO food supply for Australia (FAO n.d.) between 2010-12 and the three years 2021-23 (section 4 gives the reading on a ten-year window). Weighted by age and sex at reference body weights (NHMRC 2006), the mean energy requirement of the 2010 population is 8.30 MJ a day, 1.038 times the model's 8.0 MJ, so both years' capacities are about 4% high; the change in age structure from 2011 to 2025 was not computed, and non-resident populations are not counted (Supplementary Section S10).

The 2025 national figure, 27,608,200, is the allocated SA2 sum, 304 below the ABS total for the same SA2s, not a rounding. The national 2011 population is the sum of the 53 regions, 22,336,514, so national and regional figures share one base. Supplementary Section S8 lists the data sources by year and the thesis's own 2011 figures.

The method has three tiers: published, corrected and improved. The corrected tier applies two corrections to the published method, the biodiesel yield per tonne of oilseed (Geoscience Australia and ABARE 2010, p. 331) and the carcase-to-retail yield of beef (USDA 1992; AUS-MEAT 2005), and for 2011 sets each state's population to the sum of its regions, which moves the state ratios but no capacity. The paper reports the corrected method on 2011 and 2025 data unless a figure names another tier;[^1] Supplementary Section S11 keys the release names of the data files.

The weather allowance scales every yield by one factor per state, taken from the record of wheat, oats and barley yields (Lane 2014, p. 193). It is a cereal factor. For Australia it is built from 153 seasons from 1861 to 2024-25, each season's yield over the mean of the ten observations around it, and its 1-in-10 and 1-in-20 values are 0.742 and 0.634 (the 2011 data use the thesis table, 0.599 at 20 years, except where section 8.1 puts both years on one table). Applying a cereal factor to pasture-fed output has not been tested; section 8.1 gives a crop-only bound.

The 2025 results carry Monte Carlo ranges over 19 sampled choices, of which 18 vary on the corrected method. An earlier set of 13 of them, without the land source, the energy requirement and four other data choices, is kept for comparison in Supplementary Section S4. A definition (what is counted, the unit, the time frame, the diet standard, the weather allowed for) changes what the number means and is reported separately; a setting is a value within one meaning, drawn over the range its source gives. The 2011 results have no ranges of their own; section 9 pairs the two years on the seven choices they share (named in Supplementary Section S4).

[^1]: The 2011 values published in Lane et al. (2015) and Lane (2017) were computed before the two method corrections, before each 2011 state's population was set to the sum of its regions and before the regional populations were put on the ABS figures; correction notes to those papers set out the change. An errata list in the archive records ten errors in the thesis model and its papers; this paper corrects the biodiesel yield, which counted a seed's oil content twice (error 1), the beef carcase-to-retail yield of 1.0 (error 2) and the 2011 populations (errors 6 and 8 to 10).

## 3. The short-term picture, 2011 to 2025

On the corrected method, Australia's short-term capacity was 33.3 million people in 2011 against a population of 22.3 million. In 2025 it was 41.5 million against 27.6 million (Figure 1). Capacity was about 1.5 times the population in both years: 1.49 in 2011 and 1.50 in 2025. Capacity grew by 24.6% and the population by 23.6%.

Figure 1. National short-term capacity against population, Australia, 2011 and 2025, corrected method. Each dot is one year; a dot above the line has capacity above population. The 2011 value is the base case on the corrected 2011 release; 2011 has no sampled range. The 2025 bars are the 5th to 95th percentile of capacity: the thin line is the widened range over 19 choices and 2,000 draws, the thick band the earlier range over 13 choices and 1,000 draws.

The regional picture moved more. Of the 53 regions, 22 were over capacity in the short term in 2011 and 16 in 2025. In the long term, 35 and 32 regions were over capacity.

Eight regions changed status in the short term: seven moved from over to under capacity and one, Corangamite, from under to over. Each flip was tested three ways (Table 2). The first asks whether the land layer alone moves the region's capacity by more than 2 times or less than 0.5 times, a sign that land was defined differently, not used differently. The second asks whether the 2011 zone was drawn on an older boundary. The third asks whether the 2025 status holds across the 5th to 95th percentile of the region's capacity over all 19 sampled choices (section 9). On the full set of 19 choices none of the eight flips is both free of an artefact flag and robust to the range. Three are land-definition artefacts: Desert Channels, Southern Gulf and SA Arid Lands. Seven do not keep their 2025 status across the range: Wet Tropics, Desert Channels, Mackay Whitsunday, Rangelands (Western Australia), North Tasmania, Southern Gulf and Corangamite. SA Arid Lands keeps its status but is a land-definition case. "Robust" is used only for flips that pass on the full set.

## 4. What drove the change

The change in capacity was taken apart layer by layer on the corrected method. The eight layers are the 2025 data added to the 2011 inputs one set at a time: land and livestock together, infrastructure land, crop yields, consumption, the organic update, the resilience table, long-term liquid fuel, and the red meat mix of the diet. Land and livestock are one layer because the 2025 livestock yields divide each product's output by its share of the 2020-21 grazing area of the land layer plus the hay area, so a smaller recorded grazing area lowers capacity in the land layer and raises the livestock yield by the same ratio; neither alone is read as a change in land use (Supplementary Section S1; Table S12 gives nine layers). A layer's effect depends on the order, so the walk was run forward (the 2025 layers added in the order listed), in reverse (the opposite order) and in each of the 40,320 orderings. The main figures are the average over all orderings; the forward and reverse walks are the two ends of the chain, and the average lies between them in every row of Table 3. Each ordering's effects sum exactly to the change, so there is no interaction term.

Population does not enter capacity in this model; it changes whether a zone is over or under it. Nationally, capacity over population was 1.49 in the short term and 0.69 in the long term in 2011, and 1.50 and 0.57 in 2025 (Table 3). The short-term margin held, while the long-term margin fell because the population grew and long-term capacity barely moved.

In the short term capacity rose by 8.2 million. On the average of all orderings consumption added 4.15 million, land and livestock together 2.46 million, the red meat mix 1.43 million and crop yields 0.16 million. The consumption and red-meat-mix layers together add 5.6 million (68%). The red-meat share of meat fell from 0.64 to 0.578, and FAO food supply (availability, not intake) of beef and veal from 39.1 to 25.9 kg a person between 2010-12 and 2021-23; no second consumption series is held to corroborate the size of that fall. Fuel, biofuel and timber settings move the consumption layer by 0.2% or less, so it is almost wholly diet.

The diet layers rest on the 2021-23 window, the lowest-beef window of the FAO series, and beef supply falls over the series unevenly (Supplementary Section S10). On the ten-year window 2014 to 2023 national short-term capacity is 39.1 million (39.7 million on unrounded diet inputs) instead of 41.5, which is the same on rounded and unrounded inputs. The choice of window is worth about 2.4 million, about 30% of the 8.2 million rise (on the release's rounded inputs; 22% unrounded), and 2025 capacity is 17% to 19% above 2011's. This window effect is measured with the three diet inputs changed alone on 2025 data, and is not the same quantity as the 5.6 million of Table 3.

A change of definition could reach only a small part of the land and livestock layer. The ABS changed its land-use categories in 2016, and the grazing area it records for Australia fell from 3.64 million km2 (mean of four years to 2009-10) to 3.32 million in 2020-21; changes to its scope threshold and frame after 2008-09 are not documented in the sources read here (Supplementary Section S10). If the whole fall were definitional, then with output and diet held fixed it would account for about 0.19 million of the 2.46 million that land and livestock add (8%), an upper bound. Within pasture the area cancels; the 0.19 million comes from idle cropland, credited to pasture at the higher 2025 yield per hectare (Supplementary Section S10). The rest of the layer is mostly output. Crop yields add little (section 6).

In the long term capacity rose by 0.27 million. Land and livestock together are the largest layer, taking 0.60 million off on the average. The red meat mix added 0.36 million, crop yields 0.24 million, the organic update 0.18 million and liquid fuel 0.10 million.

Figure 3 gives each layer's effect in each region (Supplementary Section S1 gives the detail). On the average land and livestock together are the largest layer in 42 regions in the short term and 31 in the long term (crop yields in 7 and 20).

Two caveats apply. The livestock layer rests on a five-year window of herd and meat data, 2020-21 to 2024-25, that ends in two years of heavy slaughter (section 9 gives the range on full herd cycles). The 2011 windows lie in the Millennium Drought and the 2025 windows include the La Niña seasons 2020-21 to 2022-23 (Table 1), so the rise includes a weather component that this paper does not separate. The 2011 yields of crops and animal products, rebuilt on each of ten three-year subsets of 2005-06 to 2009-10, give 2011 short-term capacity of 32.7 to 34.0 million, with 2025 above it by 22% to 27% on every subset (crops alone move it by under 0.1 million; Supplementary Table S16); all lie inside the drought window, so they show stability within it, not that it is typical. The paired test of section 9.1 bounds the change on shared settings but does not remove the weather of the windows; no other bound is held.

## 5. Self-sufficiency by unit: the partition effect

This section asks how much of the national capacity is left when every unit feeds itself from its own land: the sum of the units' capacities as a share of the national capacity, the scale curve of Lane et al. (2015) extended to SA2. It was computed for 7 states, 53 regions and, in the ABS's Statistical Area Levels 4, 3 and 2 (ABS 2021), 89 SA4s, 336 SA3s and 2,450 SA2s. Every unit kept the national settings for diet and waste. The curve in Figure 4 is on the state-yield basis: every unit took the yields, climate zone and settings of its state. This is the conservative bound; section 5.4 gives the other. The loss is a partition effect, not an effect of size. It follows from the model's substitution rule (section 6): cropland can carry pasture-fed products but pasture cannot carry crops, so a unit whose land mix differs from the nation's loses capacity to the sum. The dependence of the loss on that rule is argued here, not measured: the one test that relaxes it, dairy on cropland, moves the SA2 share from 37% to 40% (Supplementary Section S2).

### 5.1 The curve and its stability

On the 2025 data the states summed to 75% of the national short-term capacity, the regions to 44%, the SA4s to 58%, the SA3s to 44% and the SA2s to 37% (Table 4). On 2011 data the SA2s gave 40%, against a 2020-21 land reference (Table 4). The long-term curve fell further at every level, to 20% for the SA2s in 2025.

The SA levels use 2020-21 land with each year's population.

The finding of Lane et al. (2015) that capacity falls as the unit shrinks held on the corrected method: on 2011 data the national short-term capacity was 33.3 million and the 53 regions summed to 14.7 million; the curve extends it to SA2.

### 5.2 The boundary effect and its cause

The 53 regions lost as much as the 336 SA3s, 44% against 44% in the short term in 2025. They lost far more than the 89 SA4s, which kept 58%. Rebuilding the regions from whole SA2s gave the same 44%, so the gap is a property of the partition, not of the land data. The gap lay where rangeland meets cropland, in New South Wales, South Australia and Western Australia (Supplementary Section S2).

Section 6 gives the cause. South Australia's Arid Lands region holds 326,000 km2 of pasture and 221 km2 of cropland. Its capacity was 41 thousand people on the state-yield basis (28,600 on its own yields, Table 8), far less than SA4 406, which joins that arid country with the cropland of the Eyre Peninsula.

### 5.3 How much a better drawing of regions keeps

Capacity is exactly proportional to land, so a split in which every unit has the national land mix loses nothing. In 200 random groupings of the 53 regions into seven groups, on the national yield vector (which removes the yield assignment of the state basis), the median sum is 91% of national capacity. The seven states keep 76% and the 53 regions 33% on the same basis, and about 180 of the 200 random groupings keep more than the states do. At 53 units, random groupings of the SA2s keep 68% to 81% on that basis, against the 33% of the regions as drawn (Supplementary Section S2). The best contiguous split found at 53 regions kept 76% of national capacity in the short term and 69% in the long term. The search was heuristic, so these are lower bounds on the best split.

### 5.4 Reading the curve

The other bound lets each unit keep its own yields: the 2025 short-term share is then 75% for the states and 54% for the SA2s, so yield differences within states are worth about 17 points at SA2 level, and the truth for SA2s lies between 37% and 54% (Supplementary Table S6). The regions' sums in sections 7, 8 and 11 are on own yields, which is why they differ from the curve's 44%.

## 6. The mechanism

One rule drives most of these results. A diet in the model needs cropland and pasture in nearly fixed proportion, so a unit's capacity is set by whichever land type runs short. Cropland can carry pasture-fed products when pasture falls short, but pasture is not necessarily fit for crops (Lane 2014, p. 266), and in the model it cannot carry them. Peters et al. (2016, p. 8) likewise set a diet's capacity at the least of three land limits, on cultivated cropland, on all cropland and on all agricultural land with grazing land, and find that how land is partitioned among these uses strongly influences the estimate (p. 13). Lane (2014, p. 3) found the share of cropping land to be the most significant determinant of capacity; that holds where cropland is scarce, as in SA Arid Lands, with 221 km2 of cropland (section 5). Nationally the diet needs only 141,000 km2 of food cropland (below), so cropland binds only at a share well below the 10% at which the national test starts; in that test, on the 2025 corrected method, capacity was unchanged for every cropland share from 10% to 99% of the combined area. The test was run for Australia only, so the rule is not shown for regions.

On the reference diet of the 2025 corrected method, pasture binds nationally. The diet needs 3.49 million km2 of pasture against 3.32 million km2 held. About 175,000 km2 of cropland therefore grazes animals, and 141,000 km2 grows food crops. Cropland that grazes animals is credited square metre for square metre at pasture's own output, and pasture never grows crops. This is why measures aimed at field crops barely move the 2025 short term while livestock output does (section 11). Field-crop yields 24% lower took 0.2% off the short term, against 26% off at a 1-in-10 drought, which scales every yield.

Short-term capacity is therefore close to a statement of how far meat production covers meat eaten, at the herd and diet of each year. Meat production over meat eaten as food, all meats at FAO's per-person food supply, was 1.57 in 2021-23 against the model's capacity over population of 1.50, and 1.51 in 2010-12 against 1.49. This is a consistency check, not a validation of pasture-fed food: the ratio that matches is all meat, including pork and poultry, and the agreement is of ratios, not of counts of people (at the model's own meat requirement the same production feeds 33 and 28 million people against the model's 41.5 and 33.3 million). It does not test cropland, regions or the split between red meat and dairy.

Each livestock yield divides output by the product's share, by dry sheep equivalent, of the grazing area of the land layer plus the hay area; cattle and sheep meat, whose output includes grain-fed animals, also divide by the land that grows the feedlot grain and the feedlot's own area, so grain-fed output is not credited to pasture alone. Because idle cropland is ceded to pasture where pasture binds, moving that land to the cropland account would leave the pooled national land need unchanged; this is argued from the land rule, not computed, and no regional test has been run.

The model has one pasture land type, and within a zone dairy and beef cattle are credited with the same average pasture yield. The dairy-on-good-land improvement moves dairy's land to the cropping column, at the observed yield of dairy farms, and it moves regions both ways (section 9). Livestock on land unsuited to crops use land that crops cannot (Poore and Nemecek 2018, p. 990; van Zanten et al. 2016; Röös et al. 2017).

## 7. The long term: a self-sufficiency preset

The long-term preset follows Lane (2014) and Lane (2017), who called it a 100-year plan; it is one scenario, not a time path. It plans for the worst year in 100 of weather, through the cereal factor of section 2. It sets a low-meat diet, with meat and eggs at 7.5% of the diet against 12% in the short term. It assumes no avoidable waste, full recycling of food waste, organic farming and liquid fuel cut from 2,360 to 210 litres a person. It also sets activity level 3, three fibre settings, timber, infrastructure area and a 30% reserve. The land for the remaining fuel rests on a biodiesel yield of 400 litres per tonne of oilseeds (Geoscience Australia and ABARE 2010, p. 331).

### 7.1 National capacity in both years

On the corrected method the long-term capacity was 15.35 million on 2011 data and 15.6 million on 2025 data (Table 5). The preset covers 69% of the 2011 population and 57% of the 2025 population. Capacity rose by 0.27 million while the population grew by 5.3 million. The long-term change is not established: 26% of the paired draws of section 9 show a fall. The weather setting lowers the figure and the low-fuel setting raises it (section 7.2). With the weather factor of a 1-in-20 year in place of 1 in 100, the 2025 figure would be 21.7 million, 79% of the population (34.3 million without the weather allowance, times 0.634, since capacity is linear in the factor).

## 8. Shocks and food security by region

The weather allowance of section 2 scales every yield, crops, pasture products and livestock alike, by a factor taken from wheat, oats and barley yields. Regions take their state's factor but do not all tip together: the five regions that tip at 1 in 10 in 2025 are in three states.

### 8.1 The national margin

National short-term capacity was about 1.5 times the population in an average year in both years (Table 7; section 3), 1.11 and 1.12 times in a 1-in-10 year and 0.68 and 0.69 times in a 1-in-100 year. In a 1-in-20 year capacity was below the population: 0.95 times in 2025, and in 2011 0.89 on the thesis weather table but 0.95 on the 2025 table. On one table the two years are nearly the same, so almost all of the apparent improvement from 0.89 to 0.95 is the table, not the data; the 1-in-10 margin is the same on either table. At the base settings the margin is above 1 at 1 in 10 and below 1 at 1 in 20 in both data years, but across the sampled choices both outcomes lie inside the range. The 5th to 95th percentile of 2025 capacity on the 19 choices of section 9 is 23.1 to 36.5 million at 1 in 10 (central 30.8; 0.84 to 1.32 times the population) and 19.7 to 31.2 million at 1 in 20 (central 26.3; 0.71 to 1.13 times). The population of 27.6 million lies inside both: 37% of the draws are below it at 1 in 10 and 78% at 1 in 20.

The factor itself depends on the record. Starting the cereal record in 1950-51, 1970-71 or 1990-91, or weighting the three cereals by area, gives a 1-in-10 capacity of 29.2 to 31.6 million on the model's table method, above the population in every variant, and 27.4 to 29.1 million on the leave-one-out reference of the model's own code, where one variant (the area-weighted record from 1990-91) sits 0.6% below the population; a 1-in-20 capacity is below it in every variant with enough seasons to support it (Supplementary Table S13). At the 2025 population the failure probability is 2.3% to 19.4% a year (5th to 95th percentile of the 2,000 widened draws, median 7.6%; linear in the return period between the weather table's anchors at 1, 3, 5, 10, 20, 30, 50 and 150 years; Supplementary Section S8).

Held at 2025 data, capacity of 41.5 million in an average year is not reached by either official baseline within its printed years (40.5 million in 2062-63 in the Intergenerational Report, 41.0 million in 2065-66 in the Population Statement). Extending the last printed slope of each, the Intergenerational Report would reach it in 2066 and the Population Statement in 2068; on straight lines between printed points both pass the 1-in-10 capacity of 30.8 million in 2034. The 2025 population is 1.8 times the long-term capacity of 15.6 million and the 2062-63 projection 2.6 times it. State government projections were not aggregated to these 53 regions, and official low and high series exist and were not used.

The cereal factor has not been tested on pasture-fed output, and almost all (about 97%) of the short-term drought effect at 1 in 10 falls on pasture-fed food. Applied to crops and grain-fed animals alone, with pasture-fed output at its average-year level, national short-term capacity is 41.2 million at 1 in 10 against 30.8 million with the factor on all output, and long-term capacity at 1 in 100 is 24.7 million against 15.6 million. Neither bound is preferred; over a drought and the year after it, the effect on pasture-fed output is expected to lie between them.

### 8.3 Named scenarios

The named scenarios were run on 2025 data, against a reference of 41.5 million in the short term and 15.6 million in the long term (Supplementary Section S3). Waste at 6% raised the short term by 16% and lowered the long term by 8.2%, because the long-term settings assume no avoidable waste. A grain store of one year raised the long term by 48% and of two years by 64%, with no short-term effect; each store takes its own resilience table (Supplementary Table S2).

### 8.4 What a single-year shock does not describe

A single-year shock does not describe a drought of several years. The 2017 to 2019 drought was exceptional as a three-year deficit (section 1.4), while its single years mostly fell within the expected range (Falster et al. 2024, pp. 6, 9). Bad wheat years share climate drivers (ENSO and the Indian Ocean Dipole together explain 30 to 41% of wheat production variability across Australia and East Africa, not Australia alone; Anderson et al. 2019), so a widespread drought can hit several states at once. An export-inclusive count sits beside the model's (Supplementary Section S9).

## 9. How sure are the regional results

### 9.1 The ranges and the status flips

This section tests the regional results for the 53 regions, each on its own yields. The national and regional ranges come from 2,000 Latin hypercube draws of 19 sampled choices, 18 of which vary, run for every region (Supplementary Section S4 lists the 19 and marks the seven both years share).

On the corrected 2025 method, the 5th to 95th percentile of national capacity is 31.1 to 49.2 million people in the short term, a factor of 1.58, and 13.3 to 18.8 million in the long term. Three choices carry four-fifths of the short-term variance: the source of the land areas (32%), the energy requirement per person (27%) and the window of herd and meat data (24%). Each rests on a judgement: equal chances between two land sources and between three meat windows, and an activity level of 1.8 at the top of the energy range (the source gives 1.2 to 2.2). If the meat window of the 13-choice draws is restricted to the four windows that span a full herd cycle, the short-term range is 39.4 to 43.8 million and the long-term range 13.9 to 16.2 million, and the red-meat share of the diet then explains 85% of the short-term variance.

2011 was not sampled on the 2025-only choices. On the seven choices both years share, drawn with common random numbers, 2025 short-term capacity is above 2011's in all 2,000 paired draws: by 6.5 to 8.4 million (5th to 95th percentile) on those choices alone, and by 2.3 to 16.5 million when the 2025-only data choices are drawn for 2025 as well (1.9 to 9.6 million on holdings land only, the same land source in both years). The 13% of 2025 draws below 2011's base value is an unpaired comparison. The long-term change is not established: 26% of paired draws show a fall with the 2025-only choices drawn (52% on holdings land only), and none do on the shared choices alone, so the long-term difference is carried by the 2025-only choices and the land source. Long-term capacity changed little (paired difference -0.9 to +3.4 million) while the population rose 24%.

Each status flip was tested against its region's 2025 range on the 19 choices. A flip holds when the 2025 population lies outside the region's 5th to 95th percentile of capacity; the test covers the 2025 status only, because the 2011 inputs have no draws. Of the eight short-term flips of section 3, none is both free of an artefact flag and robust. Seven do not keep their 2025 status: Wet Tropics, Desert Channels, Mackay Whitsunday, Rangelands (WA), North Tasmania, Southern Gulf and Corangamite, the one region that moved from under to over capacity. SA Arid Lands keeps its status but is a land-definition case. The land source and the energy requirement widen the ranges: North Tasmania is over capacity in 27% of draws on holdings land and none on the raster land, Mackay Whitsunday in 16% and 43%, so the overall shares depend on the equal chances given to the two sources. Of the three long-term flips, one passes: South West Queensland, whose 2025 capacity runs from 17,200 to 24,800 (5th to 95th percentile; lowest draw 15,500) against a population of 7,110. Desert Channels and SA Arid Lands do not. The method tier is not a flip test: it moves Corangamite in the short term between the corrected and improved 2025 methods, and Mackay Whitsunday on 2011 data.

### 9.2 What each method choice does, region by region

Each method choice was applied alone to the published method on 2025 data, the published method of Lane (2014). Its effect is given as a share of the published capacity (Figure 7; Supplementary Section S4 and Table S3). Two modules are the corrections of section 2, the beef carcase yield and the biodiesel yield; the other six are improvements. The beef carcase correction lowers the short-term national capacity by 16.0% and leaves the long-term figure unchanged. The biodiesel correction is a long-term correction, +20.7% nationally and +97.9% in South East Queensland. Dairy on land that can carry it is the largest improvement, +20.2% in the short term nationally, and it moves regions both ways.

## 11. Discussion: what would widen the margin

A measure is worth what it does to the constraint that binds. Each measure in Table 9 was applied alone to the corrected 2025 data, against a reference of 41.5 million in the short term and 15.6 million in the long term, and each is free of cost in the model. Short-term capacity is bound by pasture-fed meat and dairy (section 6), so measures aimed at field crops, irrigation or cropland taken from pasture move it by only 0 to 2.4%; livestock output, diet and waste move it most. The long term is set mainly by the weather allowance and by fuel (section 7), so drought-proofing and grain stores move it most; both rest on the cereal factor. Trade in products between regions could recover at most the pooling gain of the closed model, 15.5 million people in the short term (the national 41.5 million less the regional sum of 26.0 million), which assumes products move without loss or cost; a pool of regions does not escape a widespread drought (section 8.4). The effects are not additive, and cost, time and feasibility are not modelled. Supplementary Section S6 gives the measures with their sources, and the measures the model cannot carry.

## 12. Limitations and conclusions

The model is a closed land-use account for each region. It carries no trade, disease or biosecurity, and no limits from nutrients, soil or labour. It leaves out Indigenous food systems and wild harvest. Its named climate scenarios scale field-crop yields only; its weather allowance scales every yield by a cereal factor that is untested for pasture, and no grazing record is used. It counts residents only, not visitors or non-resident workforces. The 37% and the partition effect rest on the rule that pasture cannot carry crops, which no checked test has relaxed for arable grazing land in the mixed-farming zone, so the loss there may be overstated. 2011 was not sampled on the 2025-only choices, and five zones carry older 2011 boundaries (Alinytjara Wilurara, Desert Channels, Northern and Yorke, SA Murray Darling Basin and Swan).

### 12.1 Self-sufficiency as a policy concept

Self-sufficiency means production relative to consumption, so it does not rule out trade (Clapp 2017, p. 89). Local self-sufficiency is rarely possible: only 11 to 28% of the world's people could meet their demand for a given crop from within 100 km (Kinnunen et al. 2020, p. 229). The closed region of this paper is therefore a bound, not a policy goal.

### 12.2 Urban agriculture and other adaptations

Under forced local self-sufficiency, diet raises capacity at every scale but does not close the partition loss. On 2025 data and the state-yield basis, the sum of SA2 capacities stays between 33% and 45% of the national capacity on the same diet (Supplementary Section S7). Urban agriculture could supply 15.0% of food energy for the residents of the Sydney blocks modelled, in a 25% land-use scenario (Mcdougall et al. 2020, p. 8).

### 12.3 Water for food

The model has no water limit, so its irrigation results are an upper bound. Raising the irrigated share of all farmland from 0.7% to 5% adds 2.4% to the 2025 short term.

### 12.4 Fuel and energy in food production

Fuel enters only through the liquid fuel setting, the second-largest long-term setting (section 7). With the biofuel share at 1 and the other settings at their short-term values, the 2025 short term falls from 41.5 to 9.7 million. Australia's emergency oil stocks equalled 61 days of net imports against an obligation of 90 days (Soliman Hunter and Taylor 2021, section 5.1).

**Conclusions.** First, at fixed settings national short-term capacity rose from 33.3 to 41.5 million on the corrected method; the change is positive in every paired draw on the choices both years share, and the long-term change is not established. Averaged over all orderings of eight layers, the consumption and red-meat-mix layers added 5.6 million and land and livestock together 2.5 million; on a ten-year diet window the 2025 figure is 39.1 million (39.7 on unrounded inputs). Population changes status, not capacity: capacity over population moved from 1.49 to 1.50 in the short term and from 0.69 to 0.57 in the long term. Second, the long-term preset covered 69% of the population in 2011 and 57% in 2025, and 79% in 2025 on the 1-in-20 weather factor. Third, at the base settings a 1-in-10 year leaves 30.8 million against 27.6 million people in 2025, and capacity is 0.95 times the population in a 1-in-20 year in both years on one weather table; at 1 in 10 and at 1 in 20 the range across the 19 choices contains the population, and on crops and grain-fed animals only the 1-in-10 figure is 41.2 million. Fourth, no short-term flip is both robust across 19 choices and free of an artefact flag; South West Queensland's long-term flip is robust. The short-term count of regions over capacity moves by one region at most between method tiers; the long-term count moves by four. Fifth, on the state-yield basis the sum of SA2 capacities is 37% of national short-term capacity, a partition effect that the model's substitution rule is argued to drive.
