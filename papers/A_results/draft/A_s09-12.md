## 9. How sure are the regional results

### 9.1 The ranges and the status flips

This section tests the regional results for the 53 regions (52 NRM regions and the Northern Territory), each on its own yields. The national range comes from 2,000 Latin hypercube draws of 19 sampled choices, 18 of which vary; the regional ranges that test the flips come from 1,000 draws of the first 13 choices, 12 of which vary, because the six added choices were drawn nationally only. The 13 are crop yield factors, the herd and meat window, the meat-and-egg and red-meat shares, fuel, the organic factors, the resilience table and the long-term feeding benchmark; the six added are avoidable waste, the loss factors, the land source, the meat calibration, the energy requirement and the crop-trend window (Supplementary Section S4).

On the corrected 2025 release (2025-fixed), the 5th to 95th percentile of national capacity is 31.1 to 49.2 million people in the short term, a factor of 1.58, and 13.3 to 18.8 million in the long term. Three choices carry four-fifths of the short-term variance: the source of the land areas (32%), the energy requirement per person (27%) and the window of herd and meat data (24%). Each rests on a judgement: equal chances between two land sources and between three meat windows, and an activity level of 1.8 at the top of the energy range, a chosen part of the source's range of 1.2 to 2.2. If the meat window of the 13-choice draws is restricted to the four windows that span a full herd cycle, the short-term range is 39.4 to 43.8 million and the long-term range 13.9 to 16.2 million, and the red-meat share of the diet then explains 85% of the short-term variance.

The 2011 capacities, 33.3 million in the short term and 15.35 million in the long term, lie inside the 2025 ranges, and 2011 was not sampled. The short-term rise holds at the base settings, but it is a comparison of two years at fixed settings, not a test across the settings.

Each status flip was tested against its region's 2025 range on 2025-fixed. A flip holds when the 2025 population lies outside the region's 5th to 95th percentile of capacity. The test covers the 2025 status only, because the 2011 inputs have no draws. Of the eight short-term flips of section 3, three are not robust to the 2025 range on the 13-choice draws: Wet Tropics, Rangelands (WA) and Corangamite, the one region that moved from under to over capacity. Two pass every test in hand: Mackay Whitsunday and North Tasmania. Of the three long-term flips, one passes every test (section 7). The method tier is not a flip test: it moves Corangamite in the short term between the corrected and improved 2025 releases, and Mackay Whitsunday on 2011 data.

Five range ends rest on the author's judgement: the equal chances of the three meat windows, the low ends of the meat-and-egg share (0.115) and of the red-meat share (0.528), the equal weight on the leave-one-out resilience table, and the low end of the long-term feeding benchmark (0.30), which has no effect on the corrected release.

### 9.2 What each method choice does, region by region

Each method choice was applied alone to the published-method release on 2025 data (2025-mirror), the published method of Lane (2014). Its effect is given as a share of the published capacity (Figure 7; Supplementary Section S4 and Table S3). Two modules are the corrections of section 2, the beef carcase yield and the biodiesel yield; the other six are improvements. The beef carcase correction lowers the short-term national capacity by 16.0%, most in the beef and cropping regions, and leaves the long-term figure unchanged. The biodiesel correction is a long-term correction, +20.7% nationally and +97.9% in South East Queensland. Dairy on land that can carry it is the largest improvement, +20.2% in the short term nationally, and it moves regions both ways.

The short-term count of regions over capacity in 2025 is robust to the method tier: 15, 16 and 15 under the published, corrected and improved methods (Supplementary Table S4). The long-term count is not: 36, 32 and 36. The corrected tier has the fewest long-term regions over capacity in both years. The long-term regional map of section 7 therefore depends on judgement-based method choices as well as on the weather allowance. The modules interact, so the one-at-a-time effects are not additive: in the 2025 short term together they add 4.25 million people, where their effects alone sum to 7.13 million, and in the long term 4.35 million against 1.15 million alone.

## 10. Regions in depth

Five regions were chosen, one of each type, from the largest movers among the 53 regions (52 NRM regions and the Northern Territory): a capital with hinterland, a Murray-Darling Basin pair, a south-west Western Australian cropping region, Tasmania, and an arid region set beside a cropping neighbour. Each is read through the short term, the long term, drought and the 2025 range. All figures are on the corrected releases (2011-fixed-3 and 2025-fixed) and on each region's own yields (Table 7). Supplementary Section S5 gives the full reading of Hawkesbury-Nepean-Sydney, the Murray with Murrumbidgee-ACT, Avon, and SA Arid Lands with the Eyre Peninsula.

Hawkesbury-Nepean-Sydney is over capacity in both years and both terms; even the 95th percentile of its 2025 short-term capacity is 48,520 people, against a population of 5,401,609. Its fall in capacity carries the land-definition flag: with only the 2025 land layer swapped in, capacity is 0.491 of the 2011 figure, just below the rule's lower bound of 0.5. The Murray is under capacity in both years and both terms. Murrumbidgee-ACT, which holds Canberra, is under capacity in an average year, with its 2025 population 0.482 times its capacity. It is over capacity in the long term (2.431 times) and at a 1-in-100 drought (1.604 times). Avon is a cropping region under capacity throughout, and in the long term its 2025 population is 0.14 times its capacity. SA Arid Lands moved from over to under capacity in the short term, but the land layer alone raises its capacity 6.5 times, so the change is read as a change in the land definition. In the long term its 2025 status is not robust to the range. Its cropping neighbour, the Eyre Peninsula, carries no flag and is under capacity in both years and both terms.

**North Tasmania.** North Tasmania is the flip that passes the land, boundary and range tests in hand. It was over capacity in 2011 and is under capacity in 2025. Its short-term capacity rose from 139,165 to 187,593 against a population of 143,631 to 156,393. Its 2025 status holds across the range: the 5th percentile of capacity, 174,487, is above the 2025 population. It sits on the edge in a bad year. At a 1-in-10 drought in 2025 it is balanced (population 1.000 times capacity), and at 1 in 100 it is over capacity (1.253 times). Its 2011 status sits close to balance and has no range. In the long term it is over capacity in both years.

## 11. Discussion: what would widen the margin

A measure is worth what it does to the constraint that binds. Each measure below was applied alone to the corrected 2025 release (2025-fixed), against a reference of 41.5 million in the short term and 15.6 million in the long term (Supplementary Table S5), and each is free of cost in the model. Short-term capacity is bound by pasture-fed meat and dairy (section 6), so field-crop yields, irrigation and cropland taken from pasture move it by only 0 to 2.4%. Doubling cropland from non-agricultural land, the 1975 CSIRO case (Gifford et al. 1975), raises both terms by 8.7%, to 45.1 and 17.0 million. Supplementary Section S6 gives these measures, which have no read source beyond the model; the five below have one.

### 11.1 Livestock productivity

Livestock output 10% higher raises the short term to 45.5 million (+9.7%) and the long term to 17.1 million (+9.4%), on 2025-fixed. Output 20% higher gives 49.6 million (+19.4%) and 18.6 million (+18.8%). Across five-year averages from 1981-85 to 2006-10, the average daily gain of steers in the national beef herd rose from 0.39 to 0.72 kg (Wiedemann et al. 2015, their Table 1, p. 111). Beef produced per breeding cow mated rose 32%, from 301 to 396 kg, mainly from heavier steers (p. 113).

### 11.2 Diet composition

Setting the meat-and-egg share alone to 5% raises the short term to 76.8 million (+85.0%) and the long term to 19.6 million (+25.7%), on 2025-fixed. A red-meat share of 30% of meat, with more poultry and pork, gives 64.6 million (+55.6%) and 18.5 million (+18.6%). Studies of Australian and global diets give the land and emissions of observed and recommended diets (Hendrie et al. 2014; Tilman and Clark 2014; Ridoutt et al. 2020).

### 11.3 Food waste

The model's 6% setting halves the model's retail-and-consumer loss share, a choice that mirrors UN Target 12.3. Australia's own goal is to halve food waste by 2030 across the whole supply and consumption chain (DCCEEW 2017, pp. 3, 8). The National Food Waste Baseline estimated 7.3 Mt of food waste for 2016-17 (DCCEEW 2019, p. 3) and the 2021 Feasibility Study about 7.6 Mt a year (DCCEEW n.d.); the two are from different projects and are not combined. Section 8 gives the effect of the 6% setting.

### 11.4 Grain reserves

The store sizes of one and two years are the model's own scenarios; no Australian source sets them. With the store resilience tables applied to 2025-fixed, a one-year grain store raises the long term by 48%, to 23.2 million, and a two-year store by 64%, to 25.7 million (section 8). Both rest on the cereal factor. A one-year store is worth the same as planning for a drought of about 16 years instead of 100, and a two-year store about 10 years, read by interpolation between rows of the planning table. On one release, a two-year store (+64%) and drought-proofing (+60%, section 11.5) are the two largest long-term measures, and both act on the worst year. Reserves and trade together set exposure to supply shocks (Marchand et al. 2016).

### 11.5 Drought resilience

Drought-proofing the worst year, so that it loses half as much, moves the 100-year cereal factor for Australia from 0.46 to 0.73 of trend. On 2025-fixed it raises the long term by 60%, to 24.9 million, and leaves the short term unchanged. The Future Drought Fund embodies a shift to supporting preparedness and makes $100 million a year available for drought resilience (Productivity Commission 2023, pp. 3, v).

### 11.6 The regions as a pool

On each zone's own yields and on 2025-fixed, the national short-term capacity is 1.60 times the sum of the capacities of the 53 regions (52 NRM regions and the Northern Territory) in an average year; Supplementary Section S6 gives bad years and the long term. Trade in products between regions recovers at most about 15.5 million people-equivalents in the short term: the national 41.5 million less the regional sum of 26.0 million. In the long term it recovers 9.6 million. This is an upper bound, because it assumes products move without loss or cost. Because bad wheat years share climate drivers (Anderson et al. 2019; section 8.4), a pool of regions does not escape a widespread drought.

### 11.7 Measures the model cannot carry

The capacities assume that all production is eaten at home. In 2021-23 Australia produced 4.66 Mt of meat and ate 2.96 Mt, a surplus of 57% (FAOSTAT 2025). This is a different measure from the model's capacity of 1.50 times the population, and the two are not compared directly. Trade raises supply diversity but also import dependence (Kummu et al. 2020). Supplementary Section S6 gives the other measures the model cannot carry; water and fuel are limitations (section 12). The effects are not additive; cost, time and feasibility are not modelled; and settings such as the 5% meat share bound what is possible, not what is likely.

## 12. Limitations and conclusions

The model is a closed land-use account for each region. It carries no trade, disease or biosecurity, and no limits from nutrients, soil or labour. It leaves out Indigenous food systems and wild harvest. Its named climate scenarios scale field-crop yields only; its weather allowance scales every yield by a cereal factor that is untested for pasture. The 2011 inputs were not sampled, and five zones carry older 2011 boundaries.

### 12.1 Self-sufficiency as a policy concept

Self-sufficiency means production relative to consumption, so it does not rule out trade (Clapp 2017, p. 89). Local self-sufficiency is rarely possible: only 11 to 28% of the world's people could meet their demand for a given crop from within 100 km (Kinnunen et al. 2020, p. 229). The closed region of this paper is therefore a bound, not a policy goal.

### 12.2 Urban agriculture and other adaptations

Under forced local self-sufficiency, diet raises capacity at every scale but does not close the scale loss. On 2025-fixed and the state-yield basis, the sum of SA2 capacities stays between 33% and 45% of the national capacity on the same diet (Supplementary Section S7). Gardens add little (2025-fixed). Urban agriculture could supply 15.0% of food energy for the residents of the Sydney blocks modelled, in a 25% land-use scenario (Mcdougall et al. 2020, p. 8).

### 12.3 Water for food

The model has no water limit, so its irrigation results are an upper bound. Raising the irrigated share of cropland to 5% adds 2.4% to the 2025 short term (2025-fixed).

### 12.4 Fuel and energy in food production

The model carries fuel only through its liquid fuel setting, the second-largest long-term setting (section 7). With the biofuel share at 1 and the other settings at their short-term values, the 2025 short term falls from 41.5 to 9.7 million (2025-fixed). Australia's emergency oil stocks equalled 61 days of net imports against an obligation of 90 days (Soliman Hunter and Taylor 2021, PDF p. 11).

**Conclusions.** First, national short-term capacity rose from 33.3 to 41.5 million on the corrected method. On the average of every ordering of the data layers, the consumption and red-meat-mix layers added 5.6 million, and land and livestock together added 2.4 million, because the two layers share one grazing area; crop yields added 0.2 million. Population changes status, not capacity: people over capacity moved from 0.670 to 0.665 in the short term and from 1.454 to 1.767 in the long term. Second, a 100-year self-sufficient plan covered 69% of the population in 2011 and 57% in 2025. Third, at the base settings the worst of ten years leaves 30.8 million against 27.6 million people in 2025, and the population is 1.05 times capacity in a 1-in-20 year, a failure probability of about 5.8% a year, 1 in 17; across the settings varied the range at each return period contains the population, and the weather allowance is a cereal factor. Fourth, two short-term flips pass the tests in hand (Mackay Whitsunday and North Tasmania), and one long-term flip (South West Queensland). The short-term count of regions over capacity is robust to the method tier, while the long-term count is not. Fifth, on the state-yield basis the sum of SA2 capacities is 37% of the national short-term capacity in 2025, because in the model cropland can carry pasture-fed food but pasture cannot carry crops.
