## 9. How sure are the regional results

### 9.1 The ranges and the status flips

This section tests the regional results for the 53 regions (52 NRM regions and the Northern Territory), each on its own yields (the local basis). The ranges come from a Monte Carlo of 1,000 Latin hypercube draws over 13 sampled input choices. Twelve of the 13 vary in the corrected and published 2025 releases (2025-fixed and 2025-mirror). The thirteenth, the long-term feeding benchmark, has no effect in those releases. Paper B (section 6) describes and tests the method; this paper only uses it. The 2011 inputs were not sampled, so the 2011 results have no range.

On the corrected 2025 release (2025-fixed), the 5th to 95th percentile of national capacity is 36.3 to 45.8 million people in the short term and 13.4 to 16.5 million in the long term. The corrected long-term capacity was 15.35 million on 2011 data (2011-fixed) and 15.6 million on 2025 data (2025-fixed). Both figures lie inside the 2025 long-term range. The change between the two years is therefore smaller than the spread across input choices for 2025. That comparison is not a test of whether the change is real, because the 2011 figure has no range of its own.

Each status flip was tested against its region's 2025 range on 2025-fixed. A flip holds when the 2025 population lies outside the region's 5th to 95th percentile of capacity. The test covers the 2025 status only; a flip also needs a robust 2011 status, and the 2011 inputs have no draws. In the short term eight regions changed status between 2011 and 2025 on the corrected method: seven from over to under capacity and one from under to over. Three of the eight are not robust to the 2025 range: Wet Tropics, Rangelands (WA) and Corangamite, the one region that moved from under to over capacity. Three others are land-definition artefacts: Desert Channels, Southern Gulf and SA Arid Lands. Two pass every test in hand: Mackay Whitsunday and North Tasmania. In the long term four regions changed status, all from over to under capacity. Three of them carry an artefact flag, and two are not robust to the range. One, South West Queensland, passes every test.

The method tier is not a flip test. It does move single regions: Corangamite changes short-term status between the corrected and improved 2025 releases (2025-fixed and 2025-improved), and Mackay Whitsunday does so on 2011 data.

Five range ends rest on the author's judgement and have no outside source. They are the three-way choice of the meat data window, with equal chances; the low end of the meat-and-egg share of the diet, 0.115; the low end of the red-meat share of meat, 0.528; the equal weight given to the leave-one-out resilience table; and the low end of the long-term feeding benchmark, 0.30. The last has no effect in the corrected and published 2025 releases. It is drawn in the improved release (2025-improved), with a negligible national effect. The judgement ends drive most of the width of the ranges. On 2025-fixed the meat window alone explains 81% of the variance of the short-term national capacity and 36% of the long-term. The resilience table alone explains 52% of the long-term variance, and its weight is one of the judgement ends.

### 9.2 What each method choice does, region by region

Each method choice was applied alone to the published-method release on 2025 data (2025-mirror). Its effect is given as a share of the published capacity (Table 9.1, Figure 7). Lane 2014 is the published method the effects are measured from. Two modules are corrections: the beef carcase yield (USDA Handbook 697; AUS-MEAT 2005) and the biodiesel yield (Geoscience Australia and ABARE 2010). The other six are improvements.

The two corrections act in different places. The beef carcase correction lowers the short-term national capacity by 16.0%. It acts in the beef and cropping regions: Wet Tropics -21.5%, Lower Murray Darling -16.4% and Border Rivers-Maranoa-Balonne -15.4%. It leaves the long-term national figure unchanged. The biodiesel correction is a long-term correction, +20.7% nationally. It reaches +97.9% in South East Queensland, +95.8% in Burdekin and +83.5% in South Tasmania. In the short term it moves the national figure by +0.1%.

Dairy on land that can carry it is the largest improvement, and it moves regions both ways. It raises the short-term national capacity by 20.2%, with regional effects from -27.3% to +22.1%. It lowers SA Arid Lands (-27.3%) and Southern Gulf (-15.5%). It raises Wet Tropics (+22.1%), Rangelands (+22.0%) and Lower Murray Darling (+22.0%). In the long term it raises Rangelands by 53%. Australian loss factors raise nearly every region in the short term: +11.2% nationally and +0.8% to +22.6% by region, most in Swan. Reserve pro rata and recycling by stage act mainly in the long term, and they lower it: -5.8% and -9.7% nationally, against -1.1% and +0.1% in the short term. In the long term reserve pro rata takes up to 29.1% off a region (Condamine), and recycling by stage up to 18.2% (Southern Gulf). Beef output by state redistributes without changing the national figure: 0% nationally and -7.4% to +9.9% by region.

## 10. Regions in depth

Five regions were chosen, one of each type, from the largest movers among the 53 regions (52 NRM regions and the Northern Territory): a capital with hinterland, a Murray-Darling Basin pair, a south-west Western Australian cropping region, Tasmania, and an arid region set beside a cropping neighbour. Each is read through the short term, the long term, drought and the 2025 range. All figures are on the corrected releases (2011-fixed and 2025-fixed) and on each region's own yields (Table 10.1).

**Hawkesbury-Nepean-Sydney.** The region is over capacity in both years and both terms. Its short-term capacity fell from 66,198 to 46,572 people while its population grew from 4,073,137 to 5,401,609. Its long-term capacity is 7,186 in 2011 and 6,106 in 2025. Even the 95th percentile of its 2025 short-term capacity is 48,520 people. The fall in capacity carries the land-definition flag. With only the 2025 land layer swapped in, capacity is 0.491 of the 2011 figure, just below the rule's lower bound of 0.5. Cropping land is 389 km2 on the 2011 layer and 191 km2 on the 2025 layer, on an unchanged boundary. The change is therefore not read as a change in what the land can feed. James and O'Neill 2016 found Sydney Basin farm data too uncertain to show a trend, with error margins above 25 per cent in many ABS figures (p. 183).

**The Murray with Murrumbidgee-ACT.** The Murray region is under capacity in both years and both terms. Its short-term capacity rose from 752,498 to 905,523 against a population of 102,847 to 121,808. Murrumbidgee-ACT, which holds Canberra, is under capacity in an average year: its 2025 population is 0.482 times its capacity. It is over capacity in the long term (2.431 times) and at a 1-in-100 drought (1.604 times). Its short-term capacity rose from 1,185,593 to 1,533,929 while its population grew from 529,508 to 738,895. In both regions the largest short-term method effects are dairy on good land (+17.8% and +17.9%) and the beef carcase correction (-15.0%). The Basin accounts for about 40 per cent of national farm output (Wittwer 2021, p. 919). Its median projected streamflow is about 20% lower under 2 °C of global warming (Prosser 2021, p. 4). In northern Victoria, raw milk output fell by 1.3 billion litres between 2001-02 and 2009-10 (Sysak 2017, p. 416). The model carries no water limit, so none of this enters the capacities (section 12).

**Avon.** Avon is a cropping region under capacity throughout. Its short-term capacity rose from 971,132 to 1,080,065 against a population of 55,850 to 69,727. In the long term it is among the strongest regions: its 2025 population is 0.14 times its long-term capacity. At a 1-in-100 drought its 2025 short-term capacity is 508,513 people. The model scales its yields by Western Australia's worst-year factor. Hughes 2017 found a larger climate effect on cropping productivity in Western Australia than nationally: -7.63% against -4.71% for all cropping farms, 2000-01 to 2014-15 (Table 8.3, p. 189).

**North Tasmania.** North Tasmania is the flip that passes the land, boundary and range tests in hand. It was over capacity in 2011 and is under capacity in 2025. Its short-term capacity rose from 139,165 to 187,593 against a population of 144,582 to 156,393. Its 2025 status holds across the range: the 5th percentile of capacity, 174,487, is above the 2025 population. It sits on the edge in a bad year. At a 1-in-10 drought in 2025 it is balanced (population 1.000 times capacity), and at 1 in 100 it is over capacity (1.253 times). Its 2011 status sits close to balance and has no range. In the long term it is over capacity in both years, with capacity of 36,954 and 58,798.

North East Victoria is not among the flips because of a corrected input. Its 2011 population in the model inputs was a copy of Goulburn Broken's, 204,651 people (ERRATA, error 8). With the zone's own 2011 population, 98,604, it is under capacity in 2011 (capacity 105,525) as well as in 2025 (114,355 people against 129,164). It is therefore not a flip.

**SA Arid Lands beside the Eyre Peninsula.** SA Arid Lands moved from over to under capacity in the short term. Its capacity rose from 2,164 to 28,609 while its population fell from 31,361 to 8,049. Both changes are matters of definition. The land layer alone raises its capacity 6.5 times. The 2011 population is the model's own figure, while the 2025 population is allocated from estimated resident population by residential land. The change is therefore not read as a change on the ground. In the long term its 2025 status is not robust to the range: 7,194 to 9,031 people against a population of 8,049. Its cropping neighbour, the Eyre Peninsula, carries no flag. There, capacity rose from 217,967 to 384,476 against a population of 41,397 to 59,255, and the region is under capacity in both years and both terms.

## 11. Discussion: what would widen the margin

A measure is worth what it does to the constraint that binds. Each measure below was applied alone to the corrected 2025 release (2025-fixed), against a reference of 41.5 million in the short term and 15.6 million in the long term (Table 5). Short-term capacity is bound by pasture-fed meat and dairy. Measures aimed at field crops therefore do almost nothing: field-crop yields, irrigation and cropland taken from pasture move the short term by 0 to 2.4%. In the model, cropland can carry pasture-fed products but pasture cannot carry crops, so capacity is the same for any cropland share from 10% to 99% of the combined area. Pasture is fully used: the short term needs 3.49 million km2 of pasture against 3.32 million held. The 1.96 million km2 of unused land is non-agricultural land (section 6). Doubling cropland from that land, the 1975 CSIRO case (Gifford and others 1975, as reported in Henry and Godden 1995), raises both terms by 8.7%, to 45.1 and 17.0 million.

### 11.1 Livestock productivity

Livestock output 10% higher raises the short term to 45.5 million (+9.7%) and the long term to 17.1 million (+9.4%), on 2025-fixed. Output 20% higher gives 49.6 million (+19.4%) and 18.6 million (+18.8%). Wiedemann and others 2015 give five-year averages for the national beef herd from 1981-85 to 2006-10. The average daily gain of steers rose from 0.39 to 0.72 kg across those periods (Table 1, p. 111). Beef produced per breeding cow mated rose 32%, from 301 to 396 kg, mainly from heavier steers (p. 113). The industry forecasts in Mayberry and others 2019 and Ridoutt 2024 concern animal numbers, not output per animal.

### 11.2 Diet composition

Setting the meat-and-egg share of the diet to 5%, with the other diet settings at their base values, raises the short term to 76.8 million (+85.0%) and the long term to 19.6 million (+25.7%), on 2025-fixed. A share of 7.5% gives 48.2 million (+16.2%). A red-meat share of 30% of meat, with more poultry and pork, gives 64.6 million (+55.6%) and 18.5 million (+18.6%). Paper B gives a different figure under the same label: 103.3 million on the improved release (2025-improved), for the guideline diet with 5% meat and eggs. The two differ in release and in what else the diet changes. Studies of Australian and global diets give the land and emissions of observed and recommended diets (Hendrie and others 2014; Tilman and Clark 2014; Ridoutt and others 2020).

### 11.3 Food waste

The model's 6% setting halves the model's retail-and-consumer loss share, a choice that mirrors UN Target 12.3. Australia's own goal is to halve food waste by 2030 across the whole supply and consumption chain (DCCEEW 2017, pp. 3, 8). The National Food Waste Baseline estimated 7.3 Mt of food waste for 2016-17 (DCCEEW 2019, p. 3). The 2021 Feasibility Study estimated about 7.6 Mt a year (DCCEEW n.d.). The two are estimates from different projects and are not combined here. On 2025-fixed the 6% setting raises the short term to 48.3 million (+16.4%) and lowers the long term to 14.3 million (-8.2%). The long-term settings assume no avoidable waste, so the 6% setting is a step back there. No avoidable waste raises the short term to 55.3 million (+33.2%), and full recycling of food waste adds 2.2%. The waste scenario of section 8, rerun on 2025-fixed, gives the same result.

### 11.5 Drought resilience

Drought-proofing the worst year, so that it loses half as much, moves the 100-year factor for Australia from 0.46 to 0.73 of trend. On 2025-fixed it raises the long term by 60%, to 24.9 million, and leaves the short term unchanged. National drought policy has moved towards self-reliance and preparedness (Botterill 2021; Productivity Commission 2023, p. 3). The Future Drought Fund makes $100 million a year available for drought resilience (Productivity Commission 2023, p. v).

### 11.4 Grain reserves

The store sizes of one and two years are the model's own scenarios; no Australian source sets them. With the store resilience tables applied to 2025-fixed, a one-year grain store raises the long term by 48%, to 23.2 million, and a two-year store by 64%, to 25.7 million. Neither changes the short term. A one-year store is worth the same as planning for a drought of about 16 years instead of 100, and a two-year store about 10 years. Both equivalents are read by interpolation between rows of the planning table. On one release, a two-year store (+64%) and drought-proofing (+60%) are the two largest long-term measures, and both act on the worst year. The store is free in the model.

A one-year store for domestic use is about 11.5 Mt: 3.1 Mt for direct use at 112 kg a person, and 8.4 Mt for the feed grain embodied in meat at 303 kg a person. The 303 kg is a 2050 base-case figure of Foran and Poldy 2002, applied here to the 2025 population. The store is about a fifth of an average national cereal crop of 52.6 Mt. Wright and Cafiero 2011 discuss one or two years of supply for a dry importer, a different case (p. S72). Reserves and trade together set exposure to supply shocks (Marchand and others 2016).

### 11.6 The regions as a pool

On each zone's own yields (the local basis) and on 2025-fixed, the national short-term capacity is 1.60 times the sum of the capacities of the 53 regions (52 NRM regions and the Northern Territory) in an average year. The factor is 1.82 at a 1-in-10 year. In the long term it is 2.60, and 3.00 on 2011 data (2011-fixed). The regions' pasture and cropland complement each other. In an average year the regions over capacity are short by 20.7 million people-equivalents, and the regions under capacity have 19.1 million to spare. At a 1-in-10 year the shortfall is 21.5 million and the spare 10.8 million. Trade in products between regions recovers at most about 15.5 million people-equivalents in the short term: the national 41.5 million less the regional sum of 26.0 million. In the long term it recovers 9.6 million. This is an upper bound, because it assumes products move without loss or cost. It is not the 15.5 million of Paper B, which is the sum of SA2 capacities.

Moving food has a cost. Road and rail transport of agriculture and forestry cost A$6.8 billion, 9.8% of farm gross value of production, in 2015-16 (Higgins and others 2018a, p. 196). Northern supply chains run over 1000 km on roads cut by seasonal flooding (Higgins and others 2018b, p. 341). Few studies follow shocks along supply chains (Davis and others 2020), and no Australian study of food flows between regions was found for this paper. Bad wheat years are partly correlated across the southern states (Anderson and others 2019), so a pool of regions does not escape a widespread drought.

### 11.7 Measures the model cannot carry

The capacities assume that all production is eaten at home. In 2021-23 Australia produced 4.66 Mt of meat and ate 2.96 Mt, a surplus of 57% (FAO food balance). This is a different measure from the model's capacity of 1.50 times the population, and the two are not compared directly. If meat output fell by the national capacity factor, it would be about 3.45 Mt at a 1-in-10 year and 2.95 Mt at a 1-in-20 year; that assumption is untested. Farmers produce some 93 per cent of the food eaten at home (Lawrence, Richards and Lyons 2013, p. 32).

Fish imports, 593 thousand tonnes, are about as large as the food use of fish, 641 thousand tonnes. In the commodity balance fish is the item that runs out first, at 12 to 17 million people. Trade raises supply diversity but also import dependence (Kummu and others 2020).

Pulses, 4.4 Mt produced and mostly exported, keep for a long time. The herd is also a store: the single-year capacity runs from 43.5 to 55.4 million across 2016 to 2025 with the herd cycle, on the 2025-organic release as built, not on 2025-fixed.

The national 100-year worst-year factor, 0.46 of trend, is milder than those of New South Wales (0.30), Victoria (0.22), Queensland (0.35) and South Australia (0.35). It is not milder than those of Western Australia (0.47) or Tasmania (0.67). Spreading production across states and varieties therefore lowers national variability.

In the long term 94% of the population lives in regions over capacity (2025-fixed). Settlement policy changes where food must travel, not the national capacity. Aquaculture, seasonal forecasting, household and community stocks, and finance and insurance are further measures the model cannot carry; water and fuel are limitations (section 12).

Four cautions apply. Each measure was applied alone, so the effects are not additive. Cost, time and feasibility are not modelled. Settings such as the 5% meat share bound what is possible, not what is likely. The drought, store and livestock results rest on one weather factor applied to every yield.

## 12. Limitations and conclusions

The model is a closed land-use account for each region. It carries no trade, disease or biosecurity, and no limits from nutrients, soil or labour. It leaves out Indigenous food systems and wild harvest. Its climate scenarios scale field-crop yields only, not pasture. The 2011 inputs were not sampled, and five zones carry older 2011 boundaries. These limits bear most on the closed-region reading, which the four points below take in turn.

### 12.1 Self-sufficiency as a policy concept

Self-sufficiency means production relative to consumption, so it does not rule out trade (Clapp 2017, p. 89). Local self-sufficiency is rarely possible: only 11 to 28% of the world's people could meet their demand for a given crop from within 100 km (Kinnunen and others 2020, p. 229). Most foodshed studies measure capacity, and one hybrid study is Australian (Schreiber and others 2021, p. 9). Australia is a surplus producer in some of the paths modelled by Beltran-Peña and others 2020 (p. 4). The closed region of this paper is therefore a bound, not a policy goal.

### 12.2 Urban agriculture and other adaptations

Under forced local self-sufficiency, diet raises capacity at every scale but does not close the scale loss. On 2025-fixed and the state-yield basis, the sum of SA2 capacities is 37% of the national capacity on the base diet. It is 33% on a 2.5% meat-and-egg diet and 45% with no meat or eggs, each against the national capacity on the same diet. Against the national figure on the base diet, the SA2 sum on the 2.5% diet reaches 95%. Swaps within food groups raise the national capacity by about three fifths (+62%), from 53.6 to 86.9 million, on the improved release (2025-improved). Gardens add little: covering all fruit and vegetable land from gardens would free only about 7% of the cropland a person needs (2025-fixed). Urban agriculture could supply 15.0% of food energy for the residents of the Sydney blocks modelled by Mcdougall and others 2020 (p. 8).

### 12.3 Water for food

The model has no water limit, so its irrigation results are an upper bound. Raising the irrigated share of cropland to 5% adds 2.4% to the 2025 short term (2025-fixed). In the Murray-Darling Basin, median projected streamflow falls about 20% under 2 °C of warming (Prosser 2021, p. 4). Higher irrigation efficiency rarely lowers water use across a basin (Grafton and others 2018).

### 12.4 Fuel and energy in food production

The model carries fuel only through its liquid fuel setting. Without the low-fuel setting of the long term, the 2025 long-term capacity falls from 15.6 to 3.7 million (2025-fixed). With the biofuel share at 1 and the other settings at their short-term values, the 2025 short term falls from 41.5 to 9.7 million. Australia's refining and fuel stocks leave it exposed to imports of liquid fuel (Soliman Hunter and Taylor 2021). Energy used after the farm gate is outside the model (Ladha-Sabur and others 2019; D'Odorico and others 2018).

**Conclusions.** The five questions of section 1 are answered in turn. First, national short-term capacity rose from 33.3 to 41.5 million between 2011 and 2025 on the corrected method. Livestock added 4.7 million people, food consumption data 4.2 million and the red-meat mix of the diet 1.8 million, while land took away 2.6 million and crop yields added 0.1 million. Population changes status, not capacity: people over capacity moved from 0.670 to 0.665 in the short term and from 1.454 to 1.767 in the long term. Second, a 100-year self-sufficient plan covered 69% of the population in 2011 and 57% in 2025, with capacity of 15.35 and 15.6 million. Third, the worst of ten years leaves 30.8 million against 27.6 million people in 2025. At a 1-in-20 year the population is 1.05 times capacity, and the failure probability a year is 5.8 to 6.1%, about 1 in 16 to 17. Fourth, two short-term flips pass the tests in hand (Mackay Whitsunday and North Tasmania), and one long-term flip (South West Queensland). The short-term count of regions over capacity is robust to the method tier, while the long-term count is not. Fifth, on the state-yield basis the sum of SA2 capacities is 37% of the national short-term capacity in 2025.
