## Section 1. Introduction

### 1. Purpose and argument
The introduction sets the paper's question in the Australian debate about how many people the country can plan for, and says why a regional answer matters to planners. It gives the three context points Murray asked for (decision 52): the population debate across 2011 to 2025, food security in Australian planning and policy, and the drought and climate record that the margin is tested against. It then names the author's own model and the 2015 and 2017 findings, says what they left open (change over time, robustness, the long term and shocks region by region), and states the five questions, the first worded as decision 62 requires: how much of the change is land, yields, livestock and diet; population changes status, not capacity. Method comparison and the choice of method are left to Paper B with a pointer.

### 2. Content list
1. How many people Australia can plan to feed from its own land is an old question that came back as the population grew quickly across 2011 to 2025 (A1.1). The government framing is the Intergenerational Reports and the Population Statement (L226 to L228). Davies is quoted on both readings: growth "unplanned" (p. 149) and "in fact highly managed" (p. 150). McDonald is cited for employment growth, not population.
2. The paper sets capacity against each data year's own population; the population grew between the two years.
3. Published Australian estimates scatter widely, mostly by definition; Paper B compares the methods, so this paper gives one sentence and a pointer (A1.2).
4. Food security and regional food self-sufficiency appear in Australian planning and policy only patchily, which is why a regional capacity result matters to planners (A1.4); the federal committee's view is the 2023 inquiry (L229).
5. Since 1990 the climate has lowered Australian wheat "yield potential" (Hochman, simulated; national actual yields held flat) and "simulated profit since 2001" (Hughes), decision 52; the 2017 to 2019 drought was a three-year deficit in the Murray-Darling Basin (Falster); the margin is tested against this weather dependence (A1.5). No read source measures farm output across 2011 to 2025 itself (gap 3).
6. The model is the author's own; the 2015 paper found that capacity shrinks as the unit of self-sufficiency shrinks, and the 2017 paper set a short and a long time frame side by side (A1.3).
7. What those papers left open: change over time, robustness of the regional results, and the long term and shocks region by region (asserted until gap 4 is closed).
8. The five questions and the plan of the paper; question 1 reads "how much of the change is land, yields, livestock and diet; population changes status, not capacity" (decision 62; OUTLINE.md lists population as a cause, gap 5); Paper B carries the method comparison and the choice of method.

## Section 2. Method and data in brief

### 1. Purpose and argument
This section tells the reader in about one page what the model counts, which zones and data years are compared, and which version of the method the numbers come from. It says that the method is frozen and only the data move, so on the corrected chain the change between 2011 and 2025 is a data change; it names the other bases that appear in the paper and where. It names the two corrections behind the corrected releases and points to the supporting material for the full model and constants, and to Paper B for the Monte Carlo method.

### 2. Content list
1. What capacity means here: the number of people a region's land could feed on a stated diet and settings with no trade in or out; a region is over capacity when its population exceeds that number (people over capacity above 1).
2. The model is the author's own, built for the thesis and published 2014 to 2017; the full model, constants and construction method sit in the supporting material.
3. Two presets: the short term (today's diet and production, average year) and the long term (the settings of a 100-year self-sufficiency plan, defined in section 7).
4. Sixty zones (the nation, seven state zones and 52 NRM regions); regional results use 53 regions (52 NRM regions and the Northern Territory).
5. The data: the 2011 inputs as in the thesis; the 2025 inputs brought forward layer by layer (land, population, yields, livestock, consumption, organic share, resilience table, fuel, diet), each a release with hashes.
6. Method tiers: published, corrected and improved. The paper reports the corrected releases unless a figure names another, and the 2011 results appear as corrected only, with one footnote. Other bases, each named where it appears: the method effects of section 9 are measured on the published base, `2025-mirror` (A278 to A303); the diet swaps of section 12 are on the improved release (A134); the herd-cycle range of section 11 is on `2025-organic` (A317); the named scenarios and grain stores of sections 8 and 11 are being rerun on the corrected release with the store releases `2025-store-1` and `2025-store-2` frozen (decision 86, pending B91).
7. The drought allowance: one yield factor per state from the 150-year record of wheat, oats and barley; the national figure takes the Australian factor. Regions of one state share their state's factor (the five regions that tip at 1 in 10 in 2025 are in three states, A200 note). Applying the wheat-oats-barley factor to pasture-fed output is untested (A223 note; section 8 gap 3) and is named here.
8. Uncertainty: Monte Carlo ranges for the 2025 cells, 13 choices sampled, 12 vary in the 2025 corrected and published releases (B253); the method is described in Paper B section 6; the 2011 cells have no ranges.

## Section 3. The short-term picture, 2011 to 2025

### 1. Purpose and argument
This section gives the national short-term result for both years and then the region-by-region picture. Its argument is that the national margin held at about the same multiple of the population while the regional picture moved, and that the count of regions over capacity is not a finding on its own: each status flip has to pass the land-definition, boundary and range tests before it is reported as real, and the flips are also read against the population input (North East Victoria, pending B90) and the method tier (gap 3), which the existing tests do not cover. It ends by saying where capacity grew and where people grew: capacity grew inland, partly through diet change that applies everywhere, and people grew on the coast, which sets up the rest of the paper.

### 2. Content list
1. National short-term capacity against population in both years (a multiple of about 1.5; status ratios, where printed, are people over capacity).
2. Capacity grew about in step with the population nationally.
3. The number of regions over capacity fell, but the net fall is more than the clean flips explain, so the count is not itself a finding (pending B90 for the 2011 count).
4. The nine short-term flips, sorted into clean and robust, land-definition artefacts, and not robust to the 2025 range. Of the three "clean and robust" flips, North East Victoria rests on a 2011 population copied from Goulburn Broken (pending B90) and Mackay Whitsunday is under capacity in 2011 on the improved release (A309, not yet checked), so it does not flip there; North Tasmania's 2011 status sits close to balance and has no range.
5. Capacity grew in the inland cropping and basin regions, partly through diet settings (consumption and the red meat mix) that apply everywhere (section 4); people grew on the coast and in the capitals. Murrumbidgee-ACT gains both capacity and people (A329 note). The section says "capacity grew inland", not "production grew inland".
6. The capital-city regions are over capacity in both years, except that Swan's 2011 status is a boundary artefact usable for 2025 alone (A049, A052); Murrumbidgee-ACT is the exception. "The coast is mixed" is said only through the README's named examples (A044 note).
7. Cautions: the older 2011 boundaries (Swan the clearest case), the land-definition flag rule, robustness tested on the 2025 range only, the artefact test made on capacity, not on population (A047), and the method tier not yet a flip test.

## Section 5. The scale curve: where the margin goes

### 1. Purpose and argument

## Section 6. The mechanism

### 1. Purpose and argument
This short section explains the one rule that drives most of the results: a diet needs cropland and pasture in nearly fixed proportion, so a region's capacity is set by whichever land runs short, and cropland can graze animals while pasture cannot grow crops. It shows why field-crop measures and shocks barely move the short term, why on the corrected release a little pasture-fed meat can raise capacity while more lowers it (on the improved release no meat is the highest point, decision 31), why rangeland regions cannot use their pasture, and how the model treats pasture quality through dairy. Each point names its release and, for regions, its yield basis. It is placed after the results it explains so the reader meets the pattern before the reason.

### 2. Content list
1. The rule: cropland and pasture in nearly fixed proportion; capacity set by the land type that runs short; cropland can carry pasture-fed products, pasture cannot carry crops. Shown nationally only (A346 holds for Australia; regions not tested); the flat range runs to 100% and is wider at the low end than 10% (V31b), from an evaluator test with no saved output (gap 4).
2. Nationally on today's diet pasture binds and spare cropland grazes, which is why field-crop measures and shocks barely move the short term. The shock contrast in A223 sets -0.3% (improved release) beside -26% (corrected release) and assumes the grain factor applies to pasture-fed output, which is untested; the corrected-release shock is pending B91.
3. The meat limit, on the corrected release: pasture-fed meat raises capacity until the pasture is used up and lowers it after that; 2.5% meat and eggs is above none there, and on the improved release no meat or eggs is the highest point (B216; decision 31).
4. A rangeland region cannot use its pasture: cropland binds at every diet (SA Arid Lands on the state-yield basis, A116; the diet-grid result for the zone has no row).
5. Pasture quality: one pasture type, dairy credited at average pasture; the dairy-on-good-land improvement moves regions both ways.

## Section 8. Shocks and food security by region

### 1. Purpose and argument
This section tests the margin against bad years and named shocks, nationally and region by region. Its argument is that the comfortable average-year margin depends on the weather: it is thin in a 1-in-10 year and below balance in a 1-in-20 year in both data years, with the failure year between the two (Paper B gives a failure probability of 5.8 to 6.1% a year at the 2025 population, about 1 in 16 to 17, B130). A failed field-crop harvest barely moves the short term because field crops do not bind, while a grain store is the strongest named scenario in the long term (release named, pending B91). It closes by saying what a single-year shock does not describe, and by placing an export-inclusive count beside the model's with its different basis.

## Section 9. How sure are the regional results

### 1. Purpose and argument

## Section 11. Discussion: what would widen the margin

### 1. Purpose and argument
The discussion asks which measures would widen the margin, tested one at a time on the model and read against what binds. Its argument is that a measure is worth what it does to the binding constraint: livestock productivity, diet composition and waste move the short term, drought-proofing and storage move the long term, and field crops, irrigation and more cropland taken from pasture do almost nothing (cropland doubled from non-agricultural land gives +8.7%, A254, A255). Each measure names its release; storage and drought-proofing are compared only on one release (pending B91). It treats the regions as a pool, since trade in products between regions could recover much of what regional self-sufficiency loses, an upper bound that assumes products move without loss or cost. It then discusses, without measuring, the measures the model cannot carry, each set against Australian evidence where a read source exists.

### 2. Content list

## Section 12. Limitations and conclusions

### 1. Purpose and argument
This section states what the model cannot carry and what that means for reading the results, then answers the five questions in turn. Its central limitation is the closed region: self-sufficiency means production relative to consumption and does not rule out trade, so the closed-region reading is a bound, not a policy goal. It shows that under forced local self-sufficiency diet raises capacity at every scale but does not close the scale loss, and that gardens add little, and it names water and fuel as limits the model does not carry beyond its settings. It ends with the data and code availability statement.

### 2. Content list
1. What the model cannot carry (the `DISCUSSION.md` rows that bear on Paper A: trade, disease and biosecurity, nutrients and soil, labour, Indigenous food systems, the climate effect on pasture, the uncertainty of the 2011 inputs, older zone boundaries).
