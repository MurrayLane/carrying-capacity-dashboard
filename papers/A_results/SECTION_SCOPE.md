The introduction sets the paper's question in the Australian debate about how many people the country can plan for, and says why a regional answer matters to planners. ### 2. Content list

## Section 2. Method and data in brief

### 1. Purpose and argument
This section tells the reader in about one page what the model counts, which zones and data years are compared, and which version of the method the numbers come from. It says that the method is frozen and only the data move, so the change between 2011 and 2025 is a data change. It names the two corrections behind the corrected releases and points to the supporting material for the full model and constants, and to Paper B for the Monte Carlo method.

### 2. Content list
1. What capacity means here: the number of people a region's land could feed on a stated diet and settings with no trade in or out; a region is over capacity when its population exceeds that number.
2. The model is the author's own, built for the thesis and published 2014 to 2017; the full model, constants and construction method sit in the supporting material.
3. Two presets: the short term (today's diet and production, average year) and the long term (the settings of a 100-year self-sufficiency plan, defined in section 7).
4. Sixty zones (the nation, seven state zones and 52 NRM regions); regional results use 53 regions (the 52 NRM regions and the Northern Territory).

7. The drought allowance: one yield factor per state from the 150-year record of wheat, oats and barley; the national figure takes the Australian factor.
8. Uncertainty: Monte Carlo ranges for the 2025 cells, the method described and tested in Paper B section 6; the 2011 cells have no ranges.

## Section 4. What drove the change

This section takes the 2011 to 2025 change apart: how much came from land, crop yields, livestock, consumption and, for the long term, the updated resilience table, organic factors and fuel, one cause at a time. It makes the point that population does not enter capacity; it changes whether a region is over or under it. 4. Long term: crop yields are the largest cause, then the re-derived long-term fuel; land is small.
5. The causes do not add: they act on the same land, and the interaction is reported.

## Section 5. The scale curve: where the margin goes

### 1. Purpose and argument
This section shows how much of the national margin is left when every unit has to feed itself from its own land, from the nation down to SA2, for both years. Its argument is that most of the margin dissolves at fine scale, that this has barely changed since 2011, and that the 2015 finding survives the corrections and reaches SA2. It explains why the NRM regions do worse than their number of units suggests (rangeland pasture cut off from cropland) and how much a better drawing of regions would keep. It reads the curve for its shape, not for any single SA2.

### 2. Content list
1. The measure: the sum of the units' capacities as a share of the national capacity, at state, NRM region, SA4, SA3 and SA2 level.
2. The short-term curve for 2025 with 2011 alongside: the shares barely changed.
3. The long-term curve falls further at every level.
4. The 2015 finding survives the corrections and extends to SA2.
5. The boundary effect: the NRM regions lose about as much as the SA3s with far fewer units, and far more than the SA4s.
6. Why: rangeland pasture stranded from cropland (pointer to section 6).
7. A partition effect, not a data effect: random groupings and an ideal contiguous split bound how much a better drawing of regions keeps.
8. Reading for shape: SA2 units are administrative, the 2011 SA cells use 2020-21 land, and the state-yield basis is the conservative bound against the local-yield basis.

## Section 6. The mechanism

### 1. Purpose and argument
This short section explains the one rule that drives most of the results: a diet needs cropland and pasture in nearly fixed proportion, so a region's capacity is set by whichever land runs short, and cropland can graze animals while pasture cannot grow crops. It shows why field-crop measures and shocks barely move the short term, why a little pasture-fed meat can raise capacity while more lowers it, why rangeland regions cannot use their pasture, and how the model treats pasture quality through dairy. It is placed after the results it explains so the reader meets the pattern before the reason.

### 2. Content list
1. The rule: cropland and pasture in nearly fixed proportion; capacity set by the land type that runs short; cropland can carry pasture-fed products, pasture cannot carry crops.
2. Nationally on today's diet pasture binds and spare cropland grazes, which is why field-crop measures and shocks barely move the short term.
3. The meat limit: pasture-fed meat raises capacity until the pasture is used up and lowers it after that.
4. A rangeland region cannot use its pasture: cropland binds at every diet.
5. Pasture quality: one pasture type, dairy credited at average pasture; the dairy-on-good-land improvement moves regions both ways.

## Section 11. Discussion: what would widen the margin

### 1. Purpose and argument
The discussion asks which measures would widen the margin, tested one at a time on the model and read against what binds. Its argument is that a measure is worth what it does to the binding constraint: livestock productivity, diet composition and waste move the short term, drought-proofing and storage move the long term, and field crops, irrigation and more cropland do almost nothing. It treats the regions as a pool, since trade in products between regions recovers much of what regional self-sufficiency loses. It then discusses, without measuring, the measures the model cannot carry, each set against Australian evidence where a read source exists.

### 2. Content list
1. The frame: what binds decides what helps.
2. A11.1 Livestock productivity: the effect of livestock output up 10% and 20%, read against the past trend (decision 47) and industry forecasts of numbers, not output per animal.
3. Diet composition: the meat-and-egg share and a shift from red to white meat, set against Australian diet studies.
4. A11.3 Food waste: the national target as halving the retail-and-consumer avoidable share (decision 44); the long-term settings already assume none, so the target is a step back there.
5. A11.5 Drought resilience: drought-proofing the worst year; national drought policy and the climate effect on productivity.
6. A11.4 Grain reserves: one and two years of store and their drought equivalents; store sizes are the model's own (decision 48); reserves and trade together set exposure.
7. Field crops, irrigation and cropland: why they move almost nothing (pointer to section 6).
8. A11.6 The regions as a pool: what trade between regions recovers; the cost and distance of moving food; correlated state shortfalls (decision 51).

## Section 12. Limitations and conclusions

### 1. Purpose and argument
This section states what the model cannot carry and what that means for reading the results, then answers the five questions in turn. Its central limitation is the closed region: self-sufficiency means production relative to consumption and does not rule out trade, so the closed-region reading is a bound, not a policy goal.
