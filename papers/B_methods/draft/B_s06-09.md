# Paper B, sections 6 to 9 (first draft, D04)

## 6. What moves the number

This section measured, with the author's model on the same 2025 data, how far each design choice moved the answer. Each choice was set at the ends that the methods of section 4 take, with every other setting held at the model's defaults. A factor here is the higher capacity over the lower one; it is not a ratio of people to capacity. Table 6 lists the choices in order of their factor, and Figure 3 shows them beside the ladder of definitions. The comparison shows where each choice matters; it does not show that the model's settings are right.

### 6.1 Exports counted or not

The published counts that include exports scale the population by the share of production sold abroad. CSIRO and the University of Queensland gave 27 million people fed at home and 63 million abroad, "around 100 million people in total" (CSIRO and UQ 2025, p. 30). The two parts sum to 90 million; this paper keeps the report's own figure of 100 million. The report itself called the measure misleading as an indicator of food security (CSIRO and UQ 2025, p. 30). Bellotti (2017) counted 61 million: the population plus 36.6 million fed by exports. An energy count of exports at 2,100 kcal a person a day, built here from the FAO food balance, fed 87 to 128 million from wheat and rice alone and 184 to 264 million from all foods. Against the model's 41.5 million in an average year, the proportional count moved the answer by a factor of 2.41 and the energy count by a factor of 4.4 to 6.4. The fruit, vegetables and dairy that bind the model's stated diet are exported far less than wheat and beef: 11 to 26% of production against 67 to 78%.

### 6.2 Grazing and pasture-fed food

Pasture is most of the land behind an Australian diet. Simpson, Petroeschevsky and Lowe (2000) counted 2.61 ha of pasture in a whole footprint of 5.96 ha a person in 1991-92 (p. 14). That is 44% of the whole footprint, not of the food row alone. Their footprint valued land at world-average productivity (p. 14). Lenzen and Murray (2001) counted the land Australia actually uses, and found their modified footprint mostly pasture: 8.1 of 13.6 ha a person in 1994-95 (p. 238). Alexander et al. (2016) attributed the high land-per-diet values of New Zealand, Argentina and Australia together to animal products, particularly beef (p. 91). They gave no split for Australia alone. Their method uses global average intensities for grassland, which spans very different systems (p. 89). Peters et al. (2016) found that the land needs of United States diets turned on grazing land, and that the results were sensitive to the cropland assumed available. Cassidy et al. (2013) counted cropland only, so grazing, sheep and goats fell outside that method.

In the model, crops and pasture-fed food are paired in a fixed diet, so whichever land runs short sets the capacity. On the improved release the default diet used 5.93 ha of pasture and 0.3 ha of cropland a person. The model's Australian settings for this choice were beef output by state, dairy on land that can carry it, and an organic factor for grazing ruminants in the long term. Together with the waste and land-base settings of sections 6.3 and 6.4, they moved the short-term answer from 41.5 million on the corrected method to 53.6 million on the improved method, a factor of 1.29.

### 6.3 How waste is treated

The methods treat waste in three ways. Energy and protein balances and people per hectare count production as eaten (Henry and Godden 1995; Cassidy et al. 2013). Land-per-diet methods carry waste inside the diet. The thesis model applied loss factors from the United States (Buzby 2010; Muth 2011). The improved model replaced them with Australian loss factors and limited its recycling credit to waste fit for feed. Taking avoidable waste from 12% to none moved the corrected method from 41.5 to 55.3 million, a factor of 1.33. On the improved method the same change moved it from 53.6 to 62.9 million, a factor of 1.17.

The model's 6% setting halves the model's retail-and-consumer loss share, mirroring UN Target 12.3 as Spang et al. (2019, p. 118) state it. Australia's own goal is whole-chain halving by 2030 (National Food Waste Strategy 2017, pp. 3, 8). The 2016-17 National Food Waste Baseline put Australia's food waste at 7.3 Mt (National Food Waste Baseline 2019, p. 3). The estimate of about 7.6 Mt a year is the 2021 Feasibility Study's, as the department reports it (DCCEEW, "Reducing Australia's food waste", web page). Household totals differ widely between estimation methods (Reutter et al. 2017). Any waste rate in a capacity estimate is therefore a setting with a stated source.

### 6.4 Which land counts

Henry and Godden (1995) rested their answer on Nix's estimate of 70 Mha of land available for intensive production (p. 98). Gifford et al. (1975), as Henry and Godden report them, scaled intensive land from 40.5 to 70 Mha, a factor of 1.73 (Henry and Godden 1995, p. 99). Peters, Wilkins and Fick (2007) divided New York State's 2.03 Mha into three classes of land (p. 148). Fischer et al. (2002) rested land on its suitability for crops. The model takes the land as it is and sets aside a reserve; on the improved method the reserve and infrastructure shortfall are taken pro rata. Doubling cropland out of non-agricultural land, the 1975 case, moved the model's answer from 41.5 to 45.1 million, a factor of 1.09. On the improved method the same case gave 58.3 million. The gain was 9% on both methods. The model reads this as the short-term diet being bound by pasture-fed meat and dairy, not by cropland.

### 6.5 Diet

Diet has two readings in Table 6. Across the guideline diets from 15% to 5% meat and eggs, the improved release fed 48.3 to 103.3 million, a factor of 2.14. Against the default diet's 53.6 million, the nutrition-optimised diet with free choice and ten micronutrients fed 422.3 million, a factor of 7.88. Capacity was not monotone in the meat share on the improved release (Table 7). The diet with no meat or eggs was the highest point, at 103.6 million. There was a dip at 2.5% meat and eggs, and a cliff between 5% (103.3 million) and 7.5% (66.9 million). A little pasture-fed meat beat none on the corrected release only: 2.5% meat and eggs fed 118.0 million, above none at 112.4 million. The 103.3 million for 5% meat and eggs is the guideline diet on the improved release, set by the direct route. The same share applied alone as a measure to the corrected release gives 76.8 million. Willett et al. (2019) found that diet change alone moved global cropland use by 0 to 2% (p. 472). Their work supports diet as a lever on emissions more than on cropland.

### 6.6 The drought allowance

Most methods count an average year. Fearnside (1985) treated capacity as the density at which the probability of failing a consumption standard stays acceptable. In the model the allowance is a quantity. With an allowance for the worst year in 50, the corrected and improved releases fed 23 to 30 million; for the worst year in 100 they fed 19 to 25 million. From the average year to the worst year in 100, the corrected method moved from 41.5 to 18.9 million, a factor of 2.19. The figures of the Jones committee (1994) are not quoted, because the report was not available for this review and the summaries of it that were held disagree.

### 6.7 The unit of self-sufficiency

Fairlie (2007) took a country as the unit, Gutteridge (2005) a region, and Peters et al. (2009) a foodshed around each centre of population. Khorsandi et al. (2022) found that capacity in Iran fell as the unit shrank. Three points from the model show the effect, on the corrected method, 2025 data, short term and the state yield basis. The nation fed 41.5 million. The states summed to 75% of that, and the SA2s to 37%. That 37% is 15.5 million: the sum over SA2s of their capacities. Nation against the SA2 sum is a factor of 2.67. The national and state points sit on the release land and the SA2 point on 2020-21 land, which gives the same national capacity. On the local yield basis, where each unit takes the yields of its own zone, the SA2 sum is 54%. The full curve is in the companion results paper.

### 6.8 The time frame

The thesis model set a short-term and a long-term setting side by side (Lane 2017). The long-term setting bundles a low-meat diet, organic yields, no avoidable waste, a 100-year drought allowance and a 30% reserve. On the corrected method it fed 15.6 million, against 41.5 million in the short term, a factor of 2.66.

### 6.9 How sure the answer is, as a method

A Monte Carlo drew 1,000 Latin hypercube samples of 13 uncertain choices (McKay, Beckman and Conover 1979; Helton and Davis 2003). Twelve of the 13 varied in the corrected and published releases. In the improved release the long-term benchmark was drawn but had almost no effect. The draws used an additive shortcut, which was checked against full runs. Each choice's share of the variance was estimated from the draws (Saltelli et al. 2008). On the corrected method the 5th to 95th percentile of Australia's short-term capacity was 36.3 to 45.8 million, a factor of 1.26 (Table 8). These ranges are a spread across the settings the author judges defensible, not confidence intervals. One judged choice, the window of herd and meat data, explained 81% of the short-term variance. Five range ends are the author's judgement and have no outside source. They are the three-way meat-window choice with equal chances, the low end of 0.115 for the meat-and-egg share of the diet, and the low end of 0.528 for the red-meat share of meat. The other two are the 50/50 weight on the leave-one-out resilience table and the low end of 0.30 for the long-term benchmark.

### 6.10 The spread across settings against the definitions

By definition alone, one model on one data set spans 18.9 million (the worst year in 100) to 55.3 million (no avoidable waste) on the corrected method. With the improved settings the top reaches 62.9 million. Counting exports as a proportion takes the published figures to 100 million. The spread across defensible settings, a factor of 1.26, is small beside these definitions. The published estimates run from 9.4 million to 100 million, a factor of 10.6. The 9.4 million is the thesis's long-term figure by the published method, on 2011 data as built. Figure 3 sets the estimates on a ladder of ten steps. Its steps multiply to the whole: 1.66 from 9.4 to 15.6 million, 4.03 from 15.6 to 62.9 and 1.59 from 62.9 to 100. The product is an identity that holds for any steps chosen, so the ladder is a reading of the spread, not a decomposition. Its first step mixes a change of data, from 2011 to 2025, with the correction of the method. Its low steps are on the corrected method and its high steps on the improved method.

## 7. Which method for which purpose

### 7.1 The rating key and the methods

This section turned the tests into a guide. Table 9 rates seven methods against four purposes as well suited, partly suited or not suited. The purposes run in order of how strongly the evidence of the tests supports them. The seven methods are the energy and protein balance, the food-row footprint, land per diet (HALF), people per hectare, the commodity balance, the failure probability and the resource-based land-and-diet balance. The four planning-oriented studies of the earlier work were treated as the balance family at other scales. They are Peters, Wilkins and Fick (2007) for a state, Fairlie (2007) for a country, Gutteridge (2005) for a region, and the studies of Noosa and Douglas Shire. The ratings are the author's judgement from the tests. A second reader re-derived them blind, and they are labelled as judgement.

### 7.2 Food security and drought risk

For food security and drought risk, the balance and its failure view were rated well suited, as one model read two ways. The balance supplies the level, with a stated diet and the pairing of pasture with crops. The failure view reads that level against the weather record. On the corrected method, 41.5 million in an average year falls to 30.8 million at a 1-in-10 failure level and 18.9 million at 1 in 100. At the 2025 population the probability of failure is 5.8 to 6.1% a year, about 1 in 16 to 17. The commodity balance was rated partly suited. It names the item that runs out first, fish at 12 to 17 million and then meat at 40 to 41 million, but it carries no weather and no land. The aggregate methods were rated not suited, because they count an average year at a single productivity. Food security is a planning and policy purpose in Australia: the 2023 House of Representatives inquiry recommended a National Food Plan (House of Representatives Standing Committee on Agriculture 2023). The Jones committee's figures are not set beside these points, for the reason given in section 6.6.

### 7.3 Why national estimates range from 9 to 100 million

Only the balance was rated well suited to explaining the range, because one model run under each definition spans most of it (section 6.10). Each other method was rated partly suited, because each reproduces one family of published estimates and shows what that family counts. The energy balance is the method of the 1975 and 1995 estimates (Gifford et al. 1975; Henry and Godden 1995). The commodity balance gives the 30 to 32 million meat count derived from Foran and Poldy's table. HALF is not predictive in its authors' words (Alexander et al. 2016, p. 89), and people per hectare counts cropland only (Cassidy et al. 2013). Read as capacities, they give 276 million and 145 to 183 million. Those readings show why land-per-diet and calorie counts read high, which is part of explaining the range.

### 7.4 Regional and local land-use planning

For regional and local planning, only the balance carries a unit of land, a diet and the pairing of pasture with crops. The unit decides the answer: on the state yield basis the states summed to 75% of the national figure and the SA2s to 37% (section 6.7). The footprint was rated partly suited, because its own local-productivity adjustment sets a diet's demand against a given area. At Australian productivity it read 64.8 to 84.6 million. The failure view was rated partly suited. Fearnside's own application covered a 23,600 ha study area, and he states that his simulation needs so much data that it is impractical for large areas (Fearnside 1990, p. 195). The other methods are national or global in construction and were rated not suited.

### 7.5 Diet change and land use

For diet change and land use, HALF was rated well suited for comparisons across countries. Rebuilt on 2011 data, it came within 0.8% of the published value for Australia and within 2.1% for the world, and an independent rebuild agreed within 2%. It measures a diet's land at world-average production: on 2021-23 data Australia's value was 2.4 times the world average. The balance was rated well suited for what Australia's land can feed. On the improved release the default diet needs about 6.2 ha a person, and a diet with no meat 0.27 ha. Its capacity against the meat share has the dip and the cliff of section 6.5 on the improved release. A little meat beats none on the corrected release only. The footprint and people per hectare were rated partly suited, and the other methods not suited.

### 7.6 The short reading

No single method serves all four purposes. Read as capacities, the aggregate methods give 146 to 343 million on current yields and 103 million at 1990 world yields, about three to eight times the balance. This is consistent with none of them carrying the pairing of pasture-fed food with crops that binds the model in Australia. The pairing was not removed from the model to test it. For a security claim, report a level and a failure probability from the balance run through the failure view. Add a commodity balance for the item that fails first.

### 7.7 A minimum reporting checklist

Table 10 gives a minimum reporting checklist of twelve items. Items 2 to 8 are the design choices measured in section 6, and items 1 and 9 to 12 are what a reader needs to use the number. Of the seven design choices, exports, diet, weather, the unit and the time frame each moved the answer more than 1.2 times. Waste moved it 1.33 times on the corrected method and 1.17 on the improved, and the land base 1.09. The land base stays on the list because readers argue most about it.

### 7.8 Cautions

A figure of people fed is not a forecast or a target. None of these methods says what a population should be. The tests used Australian data, and the ratings may differ where grazing is a small share of food land. The ratings are the author's judgement on seven methods. The failure view here is the model's own runs read through Fearnside's frame, not Fearnside's own simulation.

## 8. Limitations and what no method yet does

This section states what no method reviewed can do, and what the author's own method lacks beside the others. The same terms are used for every method.

### 8.1 Variability

Every method reviewed except the failure view works in an average year. Production shocks have become more frequent on land and sea (Cottrell et al. 2019, p. 130). National totals smooth sudden losses in large countries such as Australia (Cottrell et al. 2019, p. 135). The risk of simultaneous low wheat production across the world's breadbaskets is rising, and precipitation risks for wheat have risen in Australia (Gaupp et al. 2019). Bad wheat years across the southern states are partly correlated, through the El Niño Southern Oscillation and the Indian Ocean Dipole (Anderson et al. 2019). Australian wheat yield potential since 1990 has fallen in simulation, while actual national yields held flat (Hochman et al. 2017, p. 2071). The failure view here carries one criterion, the default diet. Fearnside tested calories, total protein, animal protein and cash separately (Fearnside 1985, p. 345). The model treats the weather as one factor on every yield, taken from the record of wheat, oats and barley. Its reading of the failure probability is coarse: six run points, interpolated. No method reviewed gives the probability of a failure in the herd cycle.

### 8.2 Trade

Most methods treat the region as closed, or count exports at full value. The footprint and HALF are consumption-based, so imports and exports drop out. Reserves and trade together set a country's exposure to supply shocks (Marchand et al. 2016). Trade has raised the diversity of food supply for most of the world's people (Kummu et al. 2020). Self-sufficiency is better read as a continuum between closed borders and open trade than as a yes or no (Clapp 2017). Production within 100 km can meet the demand for a given crop for only 11 to 28% of the world's people (Kinnunen et al. 2020, p. 229). That is a range across crops, not an uncertainty. Production and consumption are increasingly separated in space (Fader et al. 2013). The model is self-sufficient by design. Trade is a way around its constraint, not part of it.

### 8.3 Energy

Simpson, Petroeschevsky and Lowe (2000) counted 0.43 ha of energy land in the food row, about a sixth (16%) of that row (p. 14). Lenzen and Murray (2001) also carried energy land in their modified footprint. The model counts no energy land. Energy after the farm gate is documented for manufacturing and distribution (Ladha-Sabur et al. 2019), and the links between food, energy and water are reviewed (D'Odorico et al. 2018). No Australian figure for the energy used in farming was found for this review. The model's farm diesel share is the model's own statement, with the National Farmers' Federation figure as Soliman Hunter and Taylor (2021) quote it. The model's bioenergy yields came from the national energy resource assessment (Geoscience Australia and ABARE 2010).

### 8.4 Diet dynamics

Each method fixes one diet. Diets are moving. The nutrition transition shifts diets towards animal-source foods and empty calories (Bodirsky et al. 2020). Meat consumption appears near a peak in some countries (Whitton et al. 2021). Capacity is tied to a period and a dietary standard (Gilland 2006), and diet trajectories differ with income (Tilman and Clark 2014). One response is to report the option space under several diets, as Erb et al. (2016) did; the diet settings of section 6.5 are another.

### 8.5 Borrowed constants and data limits

The thesis model borrowed constants from the United States: loss factors (Buzby 2010; Muth 2011) and a carcase-to-retail factor for beef (USDA Handbook 697). Its nutrient values came from AUSNUT 2007. These constants are declared as assumptions, and the corrections made to them are listed in the archive's errata. An audit raised each group of constants in turn and found red meat the heaviest group, because red meat is most of the land in the default diet. Many constants carry no recorded source in the workbook. The model also cannot carry several limits that bear on capacity: soil, fertiliser and labour, a water ceiling on irrigation, and climate effects on pasture and livestock.

### 8.6 Implementations resting on description alone

Where the authors' figure could not be rebuilt, the test rests on the method as described. Simpson's 0.312 ha of crop land for plant foods was not reproduced; the rebuild gave 0.107 ha (Simpson, Petroeschevsky and Lowe 2000, p. 14). Simpson's 0.854 ha of pasture for clothing was not reproduced from the published sources (p. 14). HALF for New Zealand was rebuilt at 94.8% of world land against the paper's 135.8, and the cause is unknown (Alexander et al. 2016, p. 91). The footprint test omitted pulses from 1991-92 consumption and did not rebuild energy land. The HALF rebuild covered 66 of the paper's 90 commodities. The people-per-hectare test used crop energy, feed shares and livestock values typed as assumptions. The energy and protein test was a reconstruction on FAO food balance data, not on the model's own production layer.

### 8.7 Where another method does better

For comparisons across countries HALF does better than the balance, and it was reproduced closely on 2011 data. For risk, a failure probability does better than any single level; here it is the model's own runs read through Fearnside's frame. The guide of section 7 holds only within these limits.

## 9. Conclusions, data and code availability

### 9.1 The answer

Published estimates of how many people Australia can feed differ because they answer different questions by different methods. One model on one data set spans 18.9 to 55.3 million by definition alone on the corrected method, and reaches 62.9 million with the improved settings. Counting exports takes the published figures to 100 million. Across the settings the author judges defensible, the answer moves by a factor of 1.26, small beside the definitions. Each figure here keeps its basis: the corrected or improved method, 2025 data and yields, and a range that is a spread across judged settings.

### 9.2 Equalised inputs

With inputs equalised, the gap between methods is the method. Equalising inputs narrowed the spread within the energy balance and people per hectare, and the footprint kept its definitional spread. The spread among methods did not narrow: 12 to 343 million as run and 12 to 343 million with inputs equalised. Inputs accounted for 10% of the distance between the methods and the corrected model. That share runs from 10 to 18% with the set of rows chosen.

### 9.3 What to do

Choose the method by purpose, as section 7 sets out. Report the twelve items of the checklist with every capacity figure. Distrust a capacity figure given without its definition.

### 9.4 Data and code availability
