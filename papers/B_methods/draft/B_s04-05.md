## 4. A catalogue of methods

### 4.1 Resource-based land and diet

The method sets the land a population needs for a stated diet against the land available (Peters, Wilkins and Fick 2007; Peters et al. 2016; Fairlie 2008). The author's thesis method belongs here (Lane 2014): it pairs pasture-fed food with crops, charges land for waste, fuel, fibre and a reserve, and reads capacity as the population the land feeds on a stated diet. The method sees land, yields and diet together; it does not see trade, and sees variation between years only through an allowance.

### 4.2 Energy and protein balance

The method counts the food energy and protein of domestic production, treats exports as food, scales production to a potential land base and divides by a consumption factor per person (Henry and Godden 1995). It counts each nutrient alone, with no balanced diet, waste or pairing of pasture with crops.

### 4.3 Commodity balance and the nutritional food balance

A commodity balance asks whether domestic production covers the human requirement for each commodity. Foran and Poldy 2002 set the requirements for grain, meat and fish under three population paths to 2050 against production; they give no capacity figure, but one can be read as production over the requirement per person, the smallest population binding. The method sees tonnes, not land, weather or a change of diet.

### 4.4 Export-inclusive counts

Export-inclusive counts add the people fed abroad by Australian exports to the people fed at home. CSIRO and University of Queensland 2025 extrapolate in proportion: if the share eaten at home feeds the population, the exported share feeds a proportional number abroad. These counts are neither energy-based nor land-based, and assume exports would be eaten in the proportions of the domestic diet.

### 4.5 Ecological footprint

The footprint inverts carrying capacity: it asks how much productive land a population appropriates, wherever that land lies (Rees 1992; Wackernagel and Rees 1996). Wackernagel 1994 set out its rates, the land a tonne of each product needs at world-average productivity. The food row sums consumption per person times that land, so trade drops out; it becomes a capacity only when a country's own land is divided by the land a person needs. Wackernagel et al. 1999 took the method to national accounts on 1993 data, with footprint and bio-capacity both in hectares of world-average productivity (pp. 377, 380, 386). Simpson, Petroeschevsky and Lowe 2000 applied it to Australia, and Lenzen and Murray 2001 modified it to count Australia's actual land use. The method sees the demand for land at world-average productivity, not the productivity of the actual land or any pairing of pasture with crops.

### 4.6 Land per diet and people per hectare

Land-per-diet indices express a diet as the land it needs at a stated productivity. Alexander et al. 2016 define the HALF index: the land the world would need if everyone ate a given country's diet, as a share of world land, at world-average production; the authors state that it is not predictive (p. 89). Cassidy et al. 2013 turn the measure around: people nourished per hectare of cropland, on a diet of 11.3 MJ (2,700 kcal) a day (p. 4). These methods see land at world-average productivity, or crop calories, by diet, but not the productivity of a country's own land or a diet that has to balance.

### 4.7 Optimisation models (diet and land use)

Optimisation models choose a diet or land use that meets stated limits at least cost or least departure from what is observed (Chaudhary and Krishna 2019; Gazan et al. 2018; van Dooren 2018). LUTO allocates Australian land uses on a fine grid against land-sector targets (Gao and Bryan 2017). These models see trade-offs among nutrients, cost and environmental limits; a people count is not their output.

### 4.8 Integrated resource-flow models

Integrated resource-flow models trace stocks and flows of land, water, energy and materials through an economy. Foran and Poldy 2002 built the Australian framework behind their commodity balance, and Hatfield-Dodds et al. 2015, Bryan et al. 2016 and Navarro Garcia et al. 2023 ran national scenario models of Australia; none gives a people count. These models see feedbacks and pressures.

### 4.9 Water-bounded capacity

Water-bounded methods cap capacity by land suitability, water and realistic yields. Khorsandi et al. 2022 combine land suitability, water and a cap on the share of net primary production that people take, at several spatial scales in Iran; their capacity falls as the units become smaller and more local. These methods treat as limits what the land and diet methods take as given.

### 4.10 Failure probability

Fearnside 1985 defines capacity as a relation between population density and the probability that settlers fail, not as one number. A settler fails in a year when consumption falls below a standard; Fearnside tests calories, total protein, animal protein and cash separately, with the calorie standard set at 10.7 MJ (2,550 kcal) a person a day (p. 352). The method sees variation between years, which the other families average away.

### 4.11 Index methods and planning studies

Graymore, Sipe and Rickson 2010 rank regional indicators against thresholds in place of a carrying capacity they treat as not measurable, so their index gives no people count. Gutteridge 2005 set a footprint against a carrying capacity for South East Queensland; the Noosa and Douglas Shire studies applied carrying capacity to local planning, and are cited through Lane 2010 and the thesis only.

## 5. Testing the methods on common Australian data

Tables 3 to 7 carry the numbers; supplementary section S3 gives the implementation detail and the arithmetic of each fidelity check.

### 5.1 The common data set

Every method used one data year, FAOSTAT (2025) data for 2021 to 2023. The land base, common to the methods and the model, is Australia's 31.65 Mha of cropland and 331.87 Mha of pasture, 363.5 Mha in all (the ABS holdings under the model match it to 0.01 Mha). The methods share the FAO food balance per person for 2021-23, 14.8 MJ (3,525 kcal) and 120.7 g of protein a day, which includes retail and household waste, and FAO's head count (26.20 million); the model's population enters no method reading. The model keeps its own diet and ABS-based production layer, which equals FAOSTAT's for field crops, lies within 4% for milk and differs for fruit and vegetables; its meat inputs, which set the output on the binding land, carry a calibration to the thesis and could not be compared (Table S11). FAOSTAT's crop output for 2021 to 2023 is 21% above its ten-year mean and its livestock output 0.4% below. With those years as a proxy for the model's own windows (meat 2020-21 to 2024-25, with a record 2024-25 cattle year), rescaling its yield layers to the ten years would raise short-term capacity by 6%, an upper indication and not a measured effect (section S3.14).

### 5.2 Fidelity check and test

A fidelity check compares an implementation with its authors' published figure on their data; the test applies the method to Australia on the common data. Each component checked was graded on the rebuild from the authors' printed inputs where one exists, and otherwise on the rebuild as run: reproduced (within the rounding of the published figure, or within 2%), partly (within 10%) or not reproduced (beyond 10%). Where no published figure could be reached, the method was reported as implemented from its published description alone. The authors were not consulted.

### 5.3 Energy and protein balance

*Access.* Henry and Godden 1995 print the recipe, its 1990-91 production totals and the consumption factors they infer from Gifford et al. 1975 (p. 99).

*Fidelity.* Run on the printed totals, the recipe gave 108.9 million (energy) and 95.7 million (protein), against the published 109 and 96 (p. 100): reproduced within rounding. The 1990-91 totals were not rebuilt, because the factors for each item are not published.

*Test.* On the FAO food balance for 2021-23, with exports counted as food, the method reads 134 to 211 million: two currencies and three land-base cases, not an uncertainty range, with the 1990-91 in-use land carried forward (section S3.1).

### 5.4 Ecological footprint, food row

*Access.* Simpson, Petroeschevsky and Lowe 2000 give a food row of 1.758 ha a person of pasture, 0.095 ha of crop land for feed and 0.312 ha of crop land for plant foods (p. 14).

*Fidelity.* Three builds differ in how pasture is charged (section S3.2). At the sheep-meat rate Wackernagel's thesis prints (build 3), pasture is 1.718 ha against 1.758, 2.3% low: partly reproduced. Crop land for feed is 21.6% high on the thesis rates (not reproduced), though builds 1 and 2 come within 4%; crop land for plant foods, the crop-plus-pasture total (11.9% low) and the clothing row's wool pasture are not reproduced.

*Test.* With Australia's land counted at the productivity of its own output (section 5.11), the footprint reads 64.8 to 117.0 million, and 64.8 to 84.6 with crop and pasture together; the two are summed as world-average hectares, with no equivalence factor. Counted in world-average hectares, a counterfactual (section 5.11), Australia's land divided by the land a person needs gives 146 to 343 million on current yields, and 103 million with build 3's yields held at their printed 1990 values.

### 5.5 HALF index

*Access.* Alexander et al. 2016 and its supplement, which prints each country's index (their Table S5, p. SI-6).

*Fidelity, on 2011 data.* The index is partly reproduced. On the current FAOSTAT revision the world is 2.2% low (34.4 against 35.13) and Australia 0.8% high (113.1 against 112.20); on the inputs the authors printed the world is 2.8% high and Australia 8.4% high (121.6), mostly from the world pasture area (section S3.3). New Zealand is not reproduced (94.8 against 135.76); Table S6 gives the other countries.

*Test.* Australia's diet in 2021-23 needs 1.32 ha a person at world-average production, a HALF of 81.0% of world land. Read in the footprint way, which HALF is not, Australia's 363.5 Mha divided by 1.318 ha a person gives about 276 million people at world-average production and 76.0 million at Australia's own productivity (section 5.11).

### 5.6 People per hectare

*Access.* Cassidy et al. 2013 print the recipe, the feed-to-food efficiencies kept here (Table 1, p. 3) and results for the world and four countries (Table 2, p. 3). Their supplementary information was not read, so crop energy per kilogram and the grain-fed share of beef are assumptions.

*Fidelity, on 2011 data against the paper's years of about 2000.* The delivered share of calories is partly reproduced (the world 61% against 59%), but the United States feed share is not (41% against 67%). People per hectare are not reproduced: they run 22 to 42% above the paper's, on 22 crop items against its 41 (section S3.4).

*Test.* On 2021-23 data Australian cropland delivers 6.0 people a hectare, against 7.9 for the world. Read as a capacity, which Cassidy's measure is not, the delivered calories of Australia's 22 crop items feed 147 million people at 11.3 MJ (2,700 kcal) a day, and 145 million in the second build.

### 5.7 Commodity balance

*Access.* Foran and Poldy 2002 set the 2050 requirements for grain, meat and fish against production in 1997-98 (p. 64); the capacity reading is this project's (section 4.3).

*Fidelity.* FAOSTAT reproduces their 1997-98 production of grain and meat within 1.2% each way, and their balance is positive for grain and meat and short for fish, as they conclude.

*Test.* On production in 2021-23 (not their 1997-98 production, which gives the 30 to 32 and 76 to 80 of Table 1), meat supports 40 million people on their requirement per person and 41 million on actual use, grain with its embodied feed grain 128 and 127 million, and fish 17 and 12 million.

### 5.8 Failure probability

*Access.* Fearnside 1985 gives the method; his 1990 result for a Brazilian study area (p. 195) cannot be checked with Australian data, so the failure frame was applied as a test of the idea, not as an independent method.

*Assumptions.* The model's drought allowance scales the harvest to the worst year expected once in T years, from a 150-year record of wheat, oats and barley yields.

*Test.* On the corrected model with 2025 data, capacity is 41.5 million in an average year. With the weather factor on every yield, the model's rule, it is 30.8 million at a maximum acceptable failure of 1 in 10 and 18.9 million at 1 in 100 (26% and 54% lower); with the factor on crop output alone and pasture-fed output at its mean it is 41.2 and 40.4 million. At the 2025 population the short-term failure probability is 5.8 to 6.1% a year with the factor on every yield (about 1 in 16 to 17) and below 1% on crops alone, because the crop-only capacity at 1 in 100 is above the population. The first figure applies a cereal-yield factor to pasture-fed output, which no livestock series has tested, so the failure view does not cover the binding constraint (section S3.6).

### 5.9 The author's model as an exemplar

The author's model (Lane 2014) was run on the same data at three tiers, each from a frozen release: the thesis method as built with its errors kept, the thesis method corrected, and an improved tier that builds on it with Australian settings (Table 4).

### 5.11 The harmonised comparison

Every method was run on the common inputs of section 5.1 and kept its defining choices; none was given the model's diet, yields or land classes (Table 5). Equalising narrows the spread within the energy and protein balance (145.2 to 148.8 million) and within people per hectare (110.9 to 112.9); the footprint keeps its definitional spread, and HALF and the meat reading barely move.

Whether equalising narrows the spread among the methods depends on where the line between input and method is drawn, so the comparison is reported under two classifications. On line (a) the productivity basis, world-average hectares for the footprint and HALF, is part of the method and stays. On line (b) it is an input: the footprint (builds 1 and 3) and HALF take Australia's land expressed as the world-average hectares that would grow its output in 2021-23, the adjustment Wackernagel and Rees (1996, p. 78) describe. Line (b) is the footprint's own accounting; line (a) is a counterfactual, Australia's physical hectares divided by a per-person footprint in world-average hectares, and neither line is a capacity reading that HALF's authors, who call the index not predictive, make. At Australia's own productivity the footprint reads the population times the ratio of Australian output to consumption, each in world-average hectares (build 1: 4.29 for cropland, 2.06 for pasture, 2.47 pooled), so it counts exports as the export-inclusive counts do.

Table 6 leads with the measures that need no reference point. On line (a) the spread of method centres does not narrow: the standard deviation of its logs goes from 0.646 to 0.702 and highest over lowest from 6.22 to 6.95. On line (b) it narrows by about a third, to 0.440 and 3.70 (about a sixth over all 19 readings), but that depends on the commodity balance: with it left out the spread widens by 12%, and with any other single method left out it narrows by 23% to 48% (Table S13). A method's distance from a reference is the mean absolute natural-log ratio of its readings to it, the share closed is the fall in that distance on harmonising over the distance as run (five methods weighted equally), and the references are the corrected model (41.5 million) and the median and geometric mean of the method centres.

On line (a) equalising closes 4.1% of the distance to the model in the base case and -1% to 8% across references from 31.1 to 53.6 million, each method left out, the fish reading dropped and other variants: about none throughout. On line (b) it closes 38.5% in the base case and 28% to 46% across the variants, and 5% to 36% of the distance to the median of the centres (9% to 36% without the combined land scale-up variant). Without the footprint and HALF it closes 10.5%, so the line (b) closure is mostly (about 87%) the productivity conversion of the footprint and HALF. Which line holds is a classification, not a finding; the productivity basis is the largest single input once it is counted as one.

A third line equalises the per-person requirement and the waste as well, for the methods and the model. With the model's 8.0 MJ a day as eaten and its loss factors applied to the food balance mix, every reading is multiplied by 1.51, except the commodity balance's grain reading, whose embodied feed grain is kept (1.10). Harmonising then closes 14.7% of the distance to the model and 20% of that to the median of the centres, and the spread of centres narrows by 26% from as run (0.65 to 0.48), less than on line (b) (0.44). The level depends on the loss-factor weighting and on whether the requirement is 8.0 or the 7.376 MJ of the diet the model's land is computed from; it is 10% to 16%, and the direction holds throughout. The footprint and HALF close 38% and 46% of their own distances; the other three methods move away from the model.

### 5.12 Gap attribution

Each residual against a published figure was attributed by changing one candidate cause at a time; no output was adjusted to meet a published figure. Table S1 prints a cause's share of a residual only where the residual is 5% or more of the published figure, and otherwise the change each cause makes in the figure's own unit. HALF's Australian residual is 0.92 points, and the HALF supplement's world pasture alone moves the figure 5.76 points the other way, 9.2 million people on Australia's 2011 cropland and pasture (396.1 Mha; the 363.5 Mha of section 5.1 is the 2021-23 land).

### 5.13 An intake basis

FAO's food supply includes retail and household waste, while the model charges its waste separately. On an intake basis, with each food's per-person use multiplied by the share eaten on FAO's waste factors or on the model's own loss factors (Table S7), the gap to the model widens for every reading above it, fish narrows, and meat, level with the model on supply, crosses above it; the calorie and land readings rise from 2.7 to 9.3 times the corrected model to 3.1 to 10.9 times on FAO's factors and 3.3 to 12.9 on the model's.

### 5.14 The pairing of pasture-fed food with crops, relaxed

After the adjustment for productivity, what remains above the model could come from a feature that the model carries and neither the footprint nor HALF does: it pairs pasture-fed food with crops and cedes the cropland the crops do not need to pasture, which then binds. The pairing was relaxed in the corrected release in two ways (Table 7, panel A). Variant 1 replaces the pasture-fed meat and dairy that the pasture cannot carry, kilojoule for kilojoule, by the diet's cropland foods, and changes nothing else. Variant 2 lowers the meat-and-egg share within the model's own diet family to what the pasture carries, a mix of its 3.5% and 4.0% diets.

Variant 1 reads 81.7 million, inside the footprint's pooled span of 64.8 to 84.6 and above HALF's 76.0, at the diet's energy, with protein falling from 93 to 81 g a day. Variant 2 also changes the diet: it reads 107.7 million, with energy 0.5 to 0.6% above the base and protein 4.2% below. Variants that keep the diet's energy within 0.6% give 82 to 108 million, short of the unadjusted 134 to 343 million. The footprint read at its binding land type, 53.9 to 77.9 million, holds each land type as its own constraint, as the pairing does; its low end lies beside the improved model's 53.6.

A bridge says more than the variants (Table 7, panel B). Between HALF at Australian productivity (76.0 million) and the corrected model (41.5) lie 34.5 million. Averaged over every ordering of seven features, the model's diet composition lowers the reading by 22.6 million, its pasture-fed yield layer by 13.0, its cropland yield layer by 6.4, its pooling rule by 6.3 and its non-food land by 4.4; its charging of waste lowers it by 17.9 and its lower per-person requirement raises it by 36.2. The pooling rule is 18% of the gap when land is pooled in HALF's own currency, the world-average hectare, and 60% of a larger gap (from 113.6 million) when food is substituted at the crop-equivalent energy rate, as in variant 1. The pairing is a sufficient cause of the gap, not shown to be the only one. Holding the diet's protein as well, with legumes and cereals replacing pasture-fed red meat and dairy allowed only to fall, the model reads 85.4 million.
