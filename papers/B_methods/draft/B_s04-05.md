## 4. A catalogue of methods

This section sorts the methods into families, each described by the question it answers, its inputs and outputs, and its scale, so that the question behind each published figure is clear before section 5 compares any number. For each family it states what the method can see and what lies outside it. Families that the author's earlier review did not cover are treated in full (Lane 2010). The planning-oriented studies that the review did cover get one line each.

### 4.1 Resource-based land and diet

The method sets the land a population needs for a stated diet against the land available, crop by crop and product by product. Peters, Wilkins and Fick 2007 built a complete-diet land model for New York State, across a range of diets and with land in three classes. Peters et al. 2016 applied the same frame to the agricultural land of the United States under ten diets, as a closed system with no trade. Fairlie 2007 drew a land budget for Britain under six farming regimes. The author's thesis method belongs here (Lane 2014; Lane, Dawes and Grace 2014): it pairs pasture-fed food with crops, charges land for waste, fuel, fibre and a reserve, and reads capacity as the population the land feeds on a stated diet. The method sees land, yields and diet together; it does not see trade, and sees variation between years only through an allowance.

The foodshed is the spatial form of the same method. Peters et al. 2009 allocated the production zones of New York State to its population centres by least distance, with the 2007 diet model giving the need per person. Zumkehr and Campbell 2015 asked what share of the population of the United States its cropland could feed within a given distance, a result that turns on the radius more than on the diet. Kurtz et al. 2020 mapped the same question for metropolitan areas of the United States across diets and land types, and called each county's figure its carrying capacity. A foodshed adds where the land lies relative to the people.

### 4.2 Energy and protein balance

The method counts the food energy and the protein of domestic production, treats exports as food, scales production to a potential land base, and divides by a consumption factor per person (Henry and Godden 1995). It counts each nutrient alone. It has no diet that has to balance, no waste and no pairing of pasture with crops. Its founding work, Gifford et al. 1975, a submission to the national population inquiry, is cited here second-hand through Henry and Godden 1995. The land base is Nix's estimate of about 70 Mha of land for intensive production, also as cited by Henry and Godden 1995 (p. 98). Shepon et al. 2016 use the same two currencies to estimate how many more people the cropland that grows feed for beef in the United States could feed if it fed poultry or people directly: a gain from reallocation, not a capacity.

### 4.3 Commodity balance and the nutritional food balance

A commodity balance asks whether domestic production covers the human requirement for each commodity, item by item. Foran and Poldy 2002 set the requirements for grain, meat and fish under three population paths to 2050 against production. They give no capacity figure. A capacity reading can be drawn from the balance: production over the requirement per person, commodity by commodity, with the smallest population binding. The method sees tonnes. It does not see land, weather or a change of diet. Ridoutt et al. 2017 extend the balance to nutrition. They set Australian intake scenarios against projected production by food group and summarise the result in a Nutritional Production Sufficiency Index. The index carries no people count.

### 4.4 Export-inclusive counts

Export-inclusive counts add the people fed abroad by Australian exports to the people fed at home. Bellotti 2017 counts the population plus the people fed by exports. CSIRO and UQ 2025 extrapolate in proportion: if the share of production eaten at home feeds the population, the exported share is taken to feed a proportional number abroad. The report itself calls the measure misleading as an indicator of food security. PMSEIC 2010 states, with no source, that Australia produces enough food to feed 60 million people (p. 1). On p. 15, citing the federal agriculture department, it says that production feeds some 40 million people outside Australia. The report does not add the two figures. These counts are neither energy-based nor land-based. They assume that the exported commodities would be eaten at home in the proportions of the domestic diet. The statistics they draw on are the national food trade accounts (Department of Agriculture 2014). Fader et al. 2013 take the reverse view of trade: they ask which countries could not be self-sufficient within their own land and water.

### 4.5 Ecological footprint

The footprint inverts carrying capacity. In place of the people a region can carry, it asks how much productive land a population appropriates, wherever that land lies (Rees 1992; Rees 1996). Wackernagel 1994 set out the rates behind the first accounts: the land a tonne of each product needs at world-average productivity. The food row multiplies each food's consumption per person by the land a tonne needs and sums the result to crop land and pasture. Imports and exports drop out. It becomes a capacity only when a country's own land is divided by the land a person needs. Simpson, Petroeschevsky and Lowe 2000 applied the method to Australia. Lenzen and Murray 2001 modified it to count Australia's actual land use. Lin et al. 2018 and Wackernagel et al. 2019 describe the national accounts that carry the method.

Blomqvist et al. 2013 find that the global overshoot rests almost wholly on the carbon footprint and one assumed sequestration rate, and that at national scale the footprint measures self-sufficiency. Giampietro and Saltelli 2014 argue that the protocol does not match its narrative, and they call for several indicators across scales. Goldfinger et al. 2014 reply that the footprint reflects actual productivity and is not prescriptive about trade. Wiedmann and Lenzen 2018 place footprints within the work on impacts displaced through trade. The method sees the demand for land at world-average productivity. It does not see the productivity of the actual land or any pairing of pasture with crops.

### 4.6 Land per diet and people per hectare

Land-per-diet indices express a diet as the land it needs at a stated productivity. Alexander et al. 2016 define the HALF index: the land the world would need if everyone ate a given country's diet, as a share of world land, at world-average production. The recipe allocates world cropland to products by mass and value and charges livestock for their feed through feed conversion ratios, with ruminants taking all pasture. The authors state that the index is not predictive (Alexander et al. 2016, p. 89). Kastner et al. 2012 split the change in cropland per person by world region into the effects of population, yield and diet. Röös et al. 2017 ask whether cropland suffices under several livestock futures, in a regional model with no trade between regions. Van Zanten et al. 2016 compare livestock fed on leftovers or on land unsuited to crops with food crops on the same land. Schön and Böhringer 2023 apply land per diet to a German state, and Ridoutt et al. 2020 to Australian diets. None of these works states a people count.

Cassidy et al. 2013 turn the measure around: people nourished per hectare of cropland. Each crop's calories go to food, feed and other uses. Feed calories return as meat, eggs and dairy at fixed efficiencies, and exports follow the average use of the importing countries. The calories are divided by a diet of 2,700 kcal a day (p. 4). Cropland is the only land counted, so grazing is left out. These methods see land at world-average productivity, or crop calories, by diet. They do not see the productivity of a country's own land or a diet that has to balance.

### 4.7 Integrated resource-flow models

Integrated resource-flow models trace stocks and flows of land, water, energy and materials through an economy. Foran and Poldy 2002 built the Australian stocks-and-flows framework from which their commodity balance comes. No separate method paper for the framework was reached, so the framework is described here from that report alone. Hatfield-Dodds et al. 2015 linked several models in a national outlook for Australia across many scenarios. Bryan et al. 2016 ran a land-use model of Australia over many scenarios. Neither gives a people count. Navarro Garcia et al. 2022 ran the FABLE Calculator, a spreadsheet model of the food and land system with no optimisation, for Australia against environmental targets, within the initiative that Jones et al. 2023 describe. The World3 line asks whether the world system overshoots; Herrington 2020 compares its scenarios with observed data. Limits to Growth itself is covered in Lane 2010 and gets this line only. These models see feedbacks and pressures; they are not built to say how many people the land can feed.

### 4.8 Optimisation models (diet and land use)

Optimisation models choose a diet or a land use that meets stated limits at least cost or with least departure from what is observed. Chaudhary and Krishna 2019 optimised national diets for many countries against nutrient limits and limits per person drawn from planetary boundaries. Gazan et al. 2018 review the diet-optimisation studies. Van Dooren 2018 reviews linear programming for diets, including the studies that apply ecological constraints. Ridoutt et al. 2021 score observed Australian diets on environmental footprints and on diet quality. They compare diets rather than optimise one. LUTO is a land-use optimisation model of Australia: a constrained linear programme that allocates land uses on a fine grid along pathways against land-sector targets (Gao and Bryan 2017). The EAT-Lancet reference diet is a judged diet set within planetary boundaries, not an optimisation, and it enters here as context only (Willett et al. 2019). These models see trade-offs among nutrients, cost and environmental limits; a people count is not their output.

### 4.9 Failure probability

Fearnside 1985 defines capacity as a relation between population density and the probability that settlers fail, not as one number. A settler fails in a year when consumption falls below a standard. Fearnside tests calories, total protein, animal protein and cash separately, with the calorie standard set at 2,550 kcal a person a day (Fearnside 1985, p. 352). Yields and weather are drawn from fitted distributions over runs of years at fixed densities. The failure rate is the share of settler-years below the standard. Capacity is read from the curve of failure against density, at the maximum failure probability the planner accepts. Fearnside 1990 gives an overview and one result for a Brazilian settlement area. The method sees variation between years, which the other families average away.

### 4.10 Water-bounded capacity

Water-bounded methods cap capacity by land suitability, water and realistic yields. Khorsandi et al. 2022 combine land suitability, water and a cap on the share of net primary production that people take, at several spatial scales in Iran. Their capacity falls as the units become smaller and more local. Khorsandi et al. 2023 reduce the method to simplified equations. The method is catalogued here and is not tested in section 5. Land-suitability assessment is its basis (Fischer et al. 2002; Mesgaran et al. 2017). Rosa et al. 2018 bound the expansion of irrigation by environmental flows. Kummu et al. 2016 trace water shortage and stress over the twentieth century. Li et al. 2018 review the methods for water resources carrying capacity. Gerten et al. 2020 bound global food production within planetary boundaries and ask how many people a balanced diet could then reach. These methods treat as limits what the land and diet methods take as given.

### 4.11 Index methods and planning studies

Graymore, Sipe and Rickson 2010 build a regional sustainability index from indicators ranked against thresholds. They treat carrying capacity as not measurable and let pressure against thresholds stand in for it, so the index gives no people count. The planning-oriented studies reviewed in Lane 2010 get one line each here. Gutteridge 2005 set a footprint against a carrying capacity for South East Queensland. The Noosa and Douglas Shire studies applied carrying capacity to local planning; they are cited through Lane 2010 only. Limits to Growth is placed in section 4.7.

## 5. Testing the methods on common Australian data

Each method was applied to the same Australian data and, where its authors' own figure could be reached, checked against it. For each method this section states the sources the project had, the assumptions made, and whether the authors' figure was reproduced on that basis, with the build named. Tables 3 to 5 carry the numbers.

### 5.1 The common data set

Every method was run on one population, data year, land base and consumption basis. The population is the model's 2025 figure, 27.6 million. The data year is FAOSTAT 2021-23, with the model's 2025 data taken as the same vintage. The land base is Australia's 31.65 Mha of cropland and 331.87 Mha of pasture, 363.5 Mha in all, which equals the model's ABS holdings to within 0.01 Mha. The consumption basis is the FAO food balance per person for 2021-23: 3,525 kcal and 120.7 g of protein a day. Its figures per person rest on FAO's head count for those years, 26.20 million, not on the model's population.

### 5.2 Fidelity check and test

A fidelity check compares an implementation with its authors' published figure on their data and years; the test applies the method to Australia on the common data. Where a fidelity check was possible, each component was reported as reproduced or not reproduced on the sources the project had. Where no published figure could be reached, the method was reported as implemented from its published description alone and was not called reproduced. The authors were not consulted, and a second build, where one exists, was written independently from the same description.

### 5.3 Energy and protein balance

*Access.* Henry and Godden 1995 print the recipe and its totals: production in 1990-91, excluding wool and cotton, of 404 × 10¹⁵ J of food energy and 306 × 10¹⁰ g of protein (p. 99). The consumption factors, inferred from Gifford et al. 1975, are 0.1760 and 0.2041 million people per unit (p. 99). Production is scaled by Nix's 70 Mha of intensive land over the 45.7 Mha in use (p. 99). Gifford et al. 1975 enters only through them.

*Fidelity.* The recipe run on the printed totals gave 108.9 million (energy) and 95.7 million (protein), against the published 109 and 96 (p. 100). The residuals are within rounding (section 5.12). The 1990-91 totals were not rebuilt, because the factors for each item are not published.

*Test.* On the FAO food balance for 2021-23, food products only, with exports counted as food and FAO's energy and protein per tonne, the method reads 134 to 211 million. The span covers two currencies and three land-base cases, not an uncertainty range: 138 and 134 million (energy and protein) on production as it is, 161 and 156 scaled to Nix's 70 Mha over 59.95 Mha in use, and 211 and 205 over the 45.7 Mha of 1990-91. The 2025 in-use land was not measured; the 28.3 Mha of sown pasture of 1990-91 was carried forward.

*What it hides.* The land base alone moves the result by 31%. Exports are counted as if eaten at home. The consumption factor implies 15.6 MJ a person a day, nearly twice a plain requirement of 8.8 MJ; at 8.8 MJ the same production feeds 243 million.

### 5.4 Ecological footprint, food row

*Access.* The reconstruction rested on Simpson, Petroeschevsky and Lowe 2000, ABS 4306.0 for consumption in 1991-92, Wackernagel and Rees 1996, and the rates in Wackernagel 1994. The food row gives 1.758 ha a person of pasture, 0.095 ha of crop land for feed and 0.312 ha of crop land for plant foods (Simpson, Petroeschevsky and Lowe 2000, Table 1, p. 14).

*Assumptions.* Three builds differ in how pasture is charged. Build 1 used FAOSTAT world yields for 1990-92 and shared world pasture among beef, sheep meat and milk by edible protein. Build 2, written independently, shared it by production times feed conversion. Build 3 charged all red meat at the sheep-meat rate the thesis prints, 34 kg/ha a year (Wackernagel 1994, p. 285), with no dairy on pasture.

*Fidelity.* Pasture is reproduced within 3% at that sourced rate in build 3: 1.718 ha against 1.758. Build 1 gives 1.737 ha, but not at the thesis's printed rate, and build 2 gives 2.385 ha, 36% high. Crop land for feed is reproduced by builds 1 and 2 (0.098 and 0.094 ha against 0.095); build 3 is 22% high. Crop land for plant foods is not reproduced from the published sources: the builds give 0.074 to 0.142 ha against 0.312. Wool pasture in the clothing row (0.854 ha, p. 14) is not reproduced either.

*Test.* Australia's cropland and pasture, divided by the land a person needs on consumption in 2021-23, give 146 to 343 million on current yields, and 103 million when build 3's yields are held at their printed 1990 values. The readings are high because all of Australia's pasture, most of it rangeland, counts at world-average productivity, with no pairing of pasture-fed food with crops.

### 5.5 HALF index

*Access.* The recipe is Alexander et al. 2016 and its supplement, which prints each country's index (Table S5, p. SI-6). The reconstruction used FAOSTAT's food balance series that starts in 2010, not the download the authors used, and covered 66 of the paper's 90 commodities.

*Assumptions.* Raw animal fats get no land of their own; butter and cream get land through milk's processing share.

*Fidelity, on 2011 data.* The index is reproduced for the world, 34.4 against 35.13 (2.1% low as printed, 2.2% unrounded), and for Australia, 113.1 against 112.20 (0.8% high). The United States and Argentina are within 5%, and India is 7% low. New Zealand is not reproduced (94.8 against 135.76). A second, independent build agrees with this one within 2%.

*Test.* Australia's diet in 2021-23 needs 1.32 ha a person at world-average production, a HALF of 81.0% of world land. Read in the footprint way, Australia's 363.5 Mha divided by 1.318 ha a person gives about 276 million people. HALF is not a capacity, and its authors say it is not predictive.

### 5.6 People per hectare

*Access.* Cassidy et al. 2013 print the recipe and the results for the world and four countries (Table 2, p. 3). The supplementary information was not read, so crop energy per kilogram and the grain-fed share of beef are assumptions.

*Assumptions.* The paper's feed-to-food efficiencies (Table 1, p. 3) and its diet of 2,700 kcal a day were kept, and cropland was the only land.

*Fidelity, on 2011 data against the paper's years of about 2000.* The calorie shares and the delivered fraction come within a few points of the paper for the world, India, China and Brazil; for the world, 61% of calories are delivered against the paper's 59% (p. 4). The United States is not reproduced: its feed share is 41% against the paper's 67%. People per hectare are 22 to 42% above the paper's, on 22 crop items and 1,061 Mha against the paper's 41 crops on 947 Mha (p. 4). A second, independent build agrees with this one within 5% for Australia and within 8% for the world on delivered calories.

*Test.* On 2021-23 data Australian cropland delivers 6.0 people a hectare, against 7.9 for the world. Read as a capacity, which Cassidy's measure is not, the delivered calories of Australia's 22 crop items feed 147 million people at 2,700 kcal a day, and 145 million in the second build.

*Harvest matching.* FAO's food balance books Australia's wheat, barley, oats, rapeseed and winter pulses one year behind the crops file, while every item in the world totals is booked in the same year. Pairing each lagged item with the harvested area of the year before gives 5.8 people a hectare delivered and 10.1 produced, on 25.5 Mha rather than 24.5 Mha, about 4% lower. The 147 million do not change, because no area enters them. Scaled to all 31.65 Mha of cropland at the matched rate, the reading is about 183 million, in place of about 190. The world figure stays at 7.9. The pairing moves Australia's figure about as much as the whole range of the grain-fed share of beef (5.9 to 5.6 people a hectare, harvests matched).

### 5.7 Commodity balance

*Access.* Foran and Poldy 2002, in Table 2.5, set the 2050 requirements for grain, meat and fish against production in 1997-98 of 31.578 Mt of grain, 3.514 Mt of meat and 0.223 Mt of fish (p. 64).

*Assumptions.* The authors give no capacity figure, so the capacity reading is this project's: production over the requirement per person, commodity by commodity, with each scenario's requirement divided by its population. FAO cereals stood for their total grain.

*Fidelity.* FAOSTAT gives production in 1997-98 of 31.95 Mt of grain and 3.47 Mt of meat, 1.2% above and 1.2% below their ABS figures. On their own numbers the balance is positive for grain and meat and short for fish, as they conclude.

*Test.* On production in 2021-23 (grain 52.6 Mt, meat 4.66 Mt, fish 0.29 Mt), meat supports 40 million people on their requirement per person and 41 million on actual use, grain with its embodied feed grain 128 and 127 million, and fish 17 and 12 million. The fish reading of 12 million counts a single commodity; the method has no diet change through which another food could replace fish.

### 5.8 Failure probability

*Access.* Fearnside 1985, in the English original, gives the method. Fearnside 1990 gives one result: at most 0.24 persons a hectare for a Trans-Amazon study area (p. 195). No check against that figure is possible on Australian data, so the failure frame was applied as a test of the idea, not as an independent method.

*Assumptions.* The model's drought allowance scales the harvest to the worst year expected once in T years, from a 150-year record of wheat, oats and barley yields. The capacity at return period T is the population the model feeds in a year with a failure probability of 1/T. The readings are therefore the model's own runs. Failure has one criterion, the default diet, where Fearnside uses four, and the curve rests on six run points, interpolated.

*Test.* On the corrected model with 2025 data, capacity is 41.5 million in an average year. At a maximum acceptable failure of 1 in 10 it is 30.8 million, 26% lower, and at 1 in 100 it is 18.9 million, 54% lower. At the 2025 population the failure probability is 5.8 to 6.1% a year, about 1 in 16 to 17. It is about 1.5% a year on the improved method and about 2% on the thesis method as built, so the risk depends on the method as much as the capacity does.

### 5.9 The author's model as an exemplar

The model is the author's own (Lane 2014) and was entered and tested like the other methods. It was entered at three tiers, each from a frozen release: the thesis method as built with its errors kept, the thesis method corrected, and an improved tier that builds on it with Australian settings (Table 4). Each tier carries a Monte Carlo range, the 5th to 95th percentile of 1,000 draws over 13 uncertain choices. On 2025 data the corrected tier, the baseline entry, reads 41.5 million in the short term (36.3 to 45.8) and 15.6 million in the long term (13.4 to 16.5). The improved tier raises these by about 12 million and 3 million.

### 5.11 The harmonised comparison

Every method was run on the common inputs of section 5.1 and kept its defining choices: world-average productivity for the footprint and HALF, each nutrient counted alone for the energy and protein balance, cropland only for people per hectare, and the item-by-item reading for the commodity balance. No method was given the model's diet, yields or land classes. The failure view took no equalised input (Table 5).

Equalising the inputs narrows the spread within the energy and protein balance, to 145.2 million on energy and 148.8 million on protein (3.50 to 3.59 times the corrected model). It narrows the spread within people per hectare, to 112.9 million in this build and 110.9 million in the second. There the food balance's 3,525 kcal a day in place of the paper's 2,700 kcal is a factor of 0.766, and the all-cropland reading of 183 million falls to 112.9 million, a factor of 0.618. The footprint keeps its definitional spread: 146 to 288 million for build 3 and 160 to 343 million for build 1. HALF stays at 275.8 million, and the commodity balance's meat reading is 41 million, 0.99 times the corrected model.

The spread among the methods does not narrow: it is 12 to 343 million as run and 12 to 343 million harmonised. Both lows are the single-commodity fish reading, and both ranges leave out the footprint's readings on pasture alone. On a ratio scale, inputs account for about 10% of the distance of each reading from the corrected model and the methods for about 90%, on one set of rows with the methods weighted equally; on other row sets the inputs' share is 10 to 18%. The gap between the methods is the method.

Wackernagel and Rees 1996 describe an adjustment for local productivity: a hectare twice as productive as the world average counts as two (p. 78). With Australia's land expressed as the world-average hectares that would grow its output in 2021-23, the footprint reads 64.8 million (build 1) and 84.6 million (build 3) with crop and pasture together, and HALF 76.0 million, against the model's 41.5 and 53.6 million. What remains above the model is the pairing of pasture-fed food with crops, which neither method carries.

### 5.12 Gap attribution

Each residual against its authors' published figure was attributed by changing one candidate cause at a time; no output was adjusted to meet a published figure.
