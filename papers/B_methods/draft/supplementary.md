# Paper B, supplementary information

Supplementary sections S1 to S8 and Tables S1 to S15 hold the detail moved out of the main text when it was cut to the journal's length, so that none of it is lost, and the tables and rubric the revision adds. Each section names the main-text section it supports. The claims behind every number are in `supplementary_claims.csv`, beside this file.

## S1. Further positions on the concept (supports section 2)

By the end of the nineteenth century carrying capacity had become a measure of rangeland productivity, and critiques of the rangeland use came in turn from Australia and Africa (Sayre 2008, p. 126). Ecology itself uses the term in many senses (Chapman and Byron 2018).

Among the defenders, Meyer and Ausubel (1999) fitted logistic growth under a capacity that itself grows; their criticism is of a fixed capacity, not of the concept. Hopfenberg (2003) tied human capacity to food availability, so that capacity moves as food supply moves. Gilland (2006) tied his own estimates to a nutrition standard.

The Jones committee called even serious estimates "little more than 'guesstimates'" (para 2.13, pp. 39 to 40) and proposed no target (para 8.10, p. 144). Chapter 6 of the thesis reviewed Limits to Growth, the ecological footprint, the South East Queensland, Noosa and Douglas Shire studies, Peters for New York State, Fairlie for Britain and Fearnside for Brazil; the 2010 review covered the same methods except Douglas Shire and Peters. Rees (1996) defined carrying capacity as the maximum load an environment can sustain. Giampietro and Saltelli (2014) argued that, because the footprint protocol has no external referent, its results do not support the narrative built on them.

Hardin (1986) proposed a cultural carrying capacity, set by the quality of life a society chooses and always below the biological limit (p. 603). Gilland (2006, p. 2) held that an estimate can be defensible if its assumptions apply to a stated period and it is not taken as an ultimate limit.

Price (1999) took as his example the Tasmanian sheep population of the nineteenth century, which levelled out because farmers learned not to overstock their pastures, not because of a natural limit (pp. 9 to 10). Van den Bergh and Rietveld (2004) found that estimates of world capacity move with the method, the limiting factor, the technology and diet assumed and the land available (pp. 201, 202); the authors offer their own figures as conditional estimates, "assuming it exists" (pp. 195, 201).

Biermann and Kim (2020) appraised the approaches used to define planetary limits and set out critiques of them from earth system science, development studies, and science and technology studies. Wang (2022) framed land carrying capacity as a question of food security, to be set region by region. Montoya et al. (2018) argued that the boundary for biodiversity lacks support for a global threshold. Herrington (2021) found that, of the World3 scenarios of Limits to Growth, comprehensive technology fitted most observed variables best, with a business-as-usual variant close behind.

## S2. The catalogue: further detail by family (supports section 4)

### S2.1 Resource-based land and diet

Peters, Wilkins and Fick (2007) built a complete-diet land model for New York State and ran it across a range of diets. Peters et al. (2016) applied the same frame to the United States under ten diets, treating its agricultural land as a closed system with no trade. Fairlie (2008) drew a land budget for Britain under six farming regimes.

The foodshed studies are the spatial form of the method. Peters et al. (2009) allocated the production zones of New York State to its population centres by least distance, with the 2007 diet model giving the need per person. Zumkehr and Campbell (2015) asked what share of the population of the United States its cropland could feed within a given distance, a result that turns on the radius more than on the diet. Kurtz et al. (2020) mapped the same question for metropolitan areas of the United States across diets and land types, and called each county's figure its carrying capacity.

### S2.2 Energy and protein balance

The method's founding work was a 1975 submission to the national population inquiry (Gifford et al. 1975), and its land base is Nix's estimate (n.d.) of about 70 Mha for intensive production, both cited in Henry and Godden (1995, p. 98). Shepon et al. (2016) estimate how many more people the United States cropland that grows feed for beef could feed if it fed poultry or people.

### S2.3 Commodity balance and the nutritional food balance

Ridoutt et al. (2017) set Australian intake scenarios against projected production by food group in a Nutritional Production Sufficiency Index.

### S2.4 Export-inclusive counts

PMSEIC (2010) states, with no source, that Australia produces enough food to feed 60 million people (p. 1) and, citing the federal agriculture department, that production feeds some 40 million people outside Australia (p. 15); it does not add the two. The 2025 report of CSIRO and the University of Queensland calls its own measure misleading as an indicator of food security (section 1). Fader et al. (2013) take the reverse view of trade: which countries cannot be self-sufficient on their own land or water limits (66 countries in 2000; pp. 4 and 8).

### S2.5 Ecological footprint

Rees (1996) restated the framework. Wackernagel et al. (1999) took the method to national accounts on 1993 data (pp. 377, 380, 386), and both sides of the comparison are in hectares of world-average productivity, not of the nation's own land. Lin et al. (2018) and Wackernagel et al. (2019) describe the national accounts that carry the method. The accounts' own Australian reading is not used here: the public release has no Australian row, and the licensed data were not sought. Wackernagel et al. (1999) print 9.0 ha a person of footprint for Australia against 14.0 of bio-capacity, a remainder and not a deficit (p. 386); they give no count of people, so they are no comparator in section 5. Blomqvist et al. (2013), cited in Giampietro and Saltelli (2014, p. 619), find that without carbon dioxide the footprint shows the planet on a sustainable path, and that its totals turn on an uncertain rate of carbon uptake by forests. Giampietro and Saltelli (2014) argue that the protocol does not match its narrative, and they call for several indicators across scales. Goldfinger et al. (2014) reply that the footprint reflects actual productivity and is not prescriptive about trade. Wiedmann and Lenzen (2018) place footprints within the work on impacts displaced through trade.

### S2.6 Land per diet and people per hectare

The HALF recipe allocates world cropland to products by mass and value and charges livestock for their feed through feed conversion ratios. Ruminants take all pasture. Kastner et al. (2012) split the change in cropland needed by world region into the effects of population, yield and diet. Röös et al. (2017) ask whether cropland suffices under several livestock futures, in a regional model with no trade between regions. Van Zanten et al. (2016) compare livestock fed on leftovers or on land unsuited to crops with food crops on the same land. Schön and Böhringer (2023) apply land per diet to a German state, and Ridoutt et al. (2020) to Australian diets. None of these works states a people count. In Cassidy et al. (2013) each crop's calories go to food, feed and other uses. Feed calories return as meat, eggs and dairy at fixed efficiencies, and exports follow the average use of the importing countries (Cassidy et al. 2013, p. 4).

### S2.7 Optimisation models (diet and land use)

Chaudhary and Krishna (2019) optimised national diets against nutrient limits and per-person limits drawn from planetary boundaries; Gazan et al. (2018) and van Dooren (2018) review diet optimisation. Ridoutt et al. (2021) score observed Australian diets on footprints and diet quality, comparing diets rather than optimising one. LUTO is a constrained linear programme (Gao and Bryan 2017). The EAT-Lancet reference diet, judged within planetary boundaries rather than optimised, is context only (Willett et al. 2019).

### S2.8 Integrated resource-flow models

Foran and Poldy (2002) built the Australian framework behind their commodity balance; no method paper for it was reached. Hatfield-Dodds et al. (2015) linked several models in a national outlook for Australia, and Bryan et al. (2016) ran a land-use model of Australia, each over many scenarios; none gives a people count. The FABLE Calculator is a spreadsheet model of the food and land system with no optimisation; Navarro Garcia et al. (2023) ran it for Australia against environmental targets, within the initiative that Jones et al. (2023) describe. Herrington (2021) compares the World3 scenarios with observed data.

### S2.9 Water-bounded capacity

Khorsandi et al. (2023) simplify the method of Khorsandi et al. (2022); it is catalogued and not tested in section 5. Land-suitability assessment is the basis of these methods (Fischer et al. 2002; Mesgaran et al. 2017). Rosa et al. (2018) bound the expansion of irrigation by environmental flows. Kummu et al. (2016) trace water shortage and stress over the twentieth century. Li et al. (2018) review the methods for water resources carrying capacity. Gerten et al. (2020) find that the present food system within four planetary boundaries would give a balanced diet (2,355 kcal) to 3.4 billion people, and that a transformed system (redistributed cropland, water and nutrient management, less waste, diet change) could support 10.2 billion (the 10.2 billion appears on pp. 3, 7 and 10); trade is not modelled in the global figure.

### S2.10 Failure probability

In Fearnside's simulation (Fearnside 1985), yields and weather are drawn from fitted distributions over runs of years at fixed densities; capacity is read from the curve of the failure rate, the share of settler-years below the standard, against density. Fearnside (1990) gives an overview of the method and one result for a Brazilian settlement area (section S3.6).

## S3. Implementation and fidelity detail by method (supports section 5)

For the common data set of section 5.1, the model's 2025 data were taken as the same vintage as FAOSTAT's data for 2021 to 2023. The land base of 363.5 Mha is FAOSTAT's land use and equals the model's ABS holdings to within 0.01 Mha. The ABARES land-use raster, the second land source drawn in the Monte Carlo, counts more cropland than the holdings (one at a time it raises short-term capacity by 17.3% and long-term capacity by 38.2%), which is why the land source carries most of the variance (section S5). The FAO food balance per person rests on FAO's head count of 26.20 million, not the model's population.

### S3.1 Energy and protein balance

Henry and Godden (1995) print production in 1990-91, excluding wool and cotton, of 404 × 10¹⁵ J of food energy and 306 × 10¹⁰ g of protein (p. 99). The consumption factors, inferred from Gifford et al. (1975), are 0.1760 and 0.2041 million people per unit (p. 99). Production is scaled by Nix's (n.d.) 70 Mha of intensive land over the 45.7 Mha in use (p. 99). Gifford et al. (1975) enters only through them.

The test used food products only, with FAO's energy and protein per tonne. Its 134 to 211 million are 138 and 134 million (energy and protein) on production as it is, 161 and 156 scaled to Nix's 70 Mha over 59.95 Mha in use, and 211 and 205 over the 45.7 Mha of 1990-91. The 2025 in-use land was not measured; the 28.3 Mha of sown pasture of 1990-91 was carried forward. The land base alone moves the result by 31%. The top of the primary range, 211 million, therefore rests on 1990-91 land with that sown pasture carried forward, not on 2021-23 land. The consumption factor implies 15.6 MJ a person a day, nearly twice a plain requirement of 8.8 MJ; at 8.8 MJ the same production feeds 243 million.

### S3.2 Ecological footprint: the three builds

Besides Simpson, Petroeschevsky and Lowe (2000), the rebuild drew on ABS (1994, catalogue 4306.0) for consumption in 1991-92, Wackernagel and Rees (1996), and the rates in Wackernagel (1994). Build 1 used FAOSTAT world yields for 1990-92 and shared world pasture among beef, sheep meat and milk by edible protein. Build 2, written independently, shared it by production times feed conversion. Build 3 charged all red meat at the sheep-meat rate Wackernagel's thesis prints, 34 kg/ha a year (Wackernagel 1994, p. 285), with no dairy on pasture.

Graded on the printed inputs (build 3), pasture is partly reproduced: 1.718 ha against 1.758, 2.3% low. Build 1 gives 1.737 ha (1.2% low), but not at Wackernagel's printed rate, and build 2 gives 2.385 ha, 36% high. Crop land for feed is 0.116 ha in build 3, 21.6% high, which is not reproduced, although builds 1 and 2 give 0.098 and 0.094 ha against 0.095. Crop land for plant foods is not reproduced from the published sources: the builds give 0.074 to 0.142 ha against 0.312. The crop-plus-pasture total of 2.165 ha is 10.3% low in build 1 and 11.9% low in build 3, not reproduced. Garden land (0.014 against 0.032 ha) and wool pasture in the clothing row (0.127 against 0.854 ha, p. 14) are not reproduced either.

### S3.3 HALF index

The rebuild used FAOSTAT's food balance series that starts in 2010, not the authors' download. The reconstruction covered 66 of the paper's 90 commodities. Raw animal fats get no land of their own; butter and cream get land through milk's processing share. On the 2025 FAOSTAT revision for 2011 the rebuild is 2.2% low for the world, 0.8% high for Australia, 3.3% and 4.6% low for the United States and Argentina and 7.2% low for India, all partly reproduced; New Zealand is 30.2% low, not reproduced. On the inputs the authors printed the world, Australia, the United States and Argentina are 2.8%, 8.4%, 3.4% and 3.0% high and India 4.1% low. For Australia the supplement's world pasture, 3,364 Mha against 3,182 Mha in the 2025 revision, gives 118.88 (6.0% high), which adds 5.7% of pasture to every tonne of ruminant product; animal products are 94.7% of Australia's index; and all printed inputs together give 121.59 (8.4% high). What offsets the pasture in the authors' Australian figure is not identified. A second, independent build agrees within 2%.

### S3.4 People per hectare

Cropland was the only land counted. On 2011 data, for the world, 61% of calories are delivered against the paper's 59% (p. 4), partly reproduced; the United States delivered share is 33% against 34%. The United States feed share is not reproduced: 41% against the paper's 67%, and its other-use share is 32% against 6%. People a hectare run 22% to 42% above the paper's as run, and 5% to 25% above on FAOSTAT yields of the 2025 revision for the paper's years; the paper prints no yields, so that rebuild does not decide the grade, and people per hectare is not reproduced. The rebuild used 22 crop items and 1,061 Mha against the paper's 41 crops on 947 Mha (p. 4). A second, independent build agrees within 5% for Australia and 8% for the world on delivered calories.

Harvest matching. FAO's food balance books Australia's winter crops (wheat, barley, oats, other pulses, and rape and mustardseed) a year behind the crops file. Pairing each with the harvest it came from gives 5.8 people a hectare delivered and, on all 31.65 Mha of cropland, about 183 million in place of about 190 (`cassidy_matched.csv`).

In the harmonised comparison (section 5.11), the food balance's 3,525 kcal a day in place of the paper's 2,700 is a factor of 0.766.

### S3.5 Commodity balance

### S3.6 Failure probability

Fearnside (1990) gives at most 0.24 persons a hectare for a Trans-Amazon study area (p. 195). The model's drought allowance scales the harvest to the worst year expected once in T years, so the capacity at return period T is the population the model feeds in a year with a failure probability of 1/T. The failure view here has one criterion, the default diet, where Fearnside uses four; it treats the weather as one factor on every yield, and the curve rests on six run points, interpolated. At the 2025 population the short-term failure probability is 5.8 to 6.1% a year on the corrected method with the factor on every yield (about 1 in 16 to 17), against about 1.5% on the improved method and about 2% on the thesis method as built, so it depends strongly on the method.

With the factor on crop output alone (pasture-fed output, fish, honey and timber at their means), the corrected method reads 41.4, 41.2, 41.0, 40.8 and 40.4 million at 1 in 5, 10, 20, 50 and 100 (against 37.0, 30.8, 26.3, 22.9 and 18.9 on every yield), and 24.7 million in the long term at 1 in 100 (against 15.6). The crop-only curve never falls to the population of 27.6 million, so the failure probability is below 1% a year; that is a bound set by the last run point, not an estimate. In the short term, 93% to 98% of the fall comes from applying the factor to pasture-fed output (by how the pasture rows are separated); in the long term at 1 in 100 it is about half, because cropland binds there. The improved release's crop-only figures (about 53.5, 53.2, 52.8, 52.5 and 52.0 at 1 in 5 to 1 in 100) are approximate, because the row-scaling rule differs from that release's own drought run by 0.1% to 0.4% from 1 in 20. The factor comes from the wheat, oats and barley record and no livestock series tests it for pasture-fed output (none is held for 1982-83 or 2002-03, and the single-year series puts 2018-19 and 2019-20 near its median), so the failure view does not cover the binding constraint.

### S3.7 Gap attribution

Each residual against the authors' published figure was attributed by changing one candidate cause at a time; no output was adjusted to meet a published figure. A residual within the rounding of the published whole number (Henry and Godden) reads "within rounding". The rule applies to HALF's Australia (0.8%), world (2.2%), United States (3.3%) and Argentina (4.6%), Simpson's pasture (2.3%) and Foran and Poldy's grain and meat (1.2% each). Shares are kept where the residual is 5% or more of the figure: HALF's India (7.2%) and Cassidy's rows in Table S8 (22% to 37%).

The adjustment of 1.129 that the HALF authors' supplement prints moves the figure by -0.38 points (+0.6 million people), its world pasture by +5.76 points (-9.2 million people) and every input the paper and supplement print by +8.47 points (-13.2 million people), so no cause tested closes the residual. For HALF's world, United States and Argentina the residuals are of the size and sign of the revisions to FAOSTAT since the authors' download, chiefly the world pasture area. ### S3.8 Bases of each method

The bases of each method are in Table S10, as run (the reading in the cross-method table) and harmonised (the reading in the harmonised comparison). No method reading uses the model's population of 27.6 million: the per-person bases are FAO's head count for the same years (26.20 million, the 2021-23 mean, which Foran and Poldy's actual-use reading also uses), and Henry and Godden's and Cassidy's readings as run divide output by a per-person requirement with no head count. The energy bases differ: 15.6 MJ (3,718 kcal) implied by Henry and Godden's factor, 2,700 kcal (11.3 MJ) for Cassidy, FAO's 3,525 kcal (14.8 MJ) on the harmonised basis, and the model's 8.0 MJ as eaten, which is intake; the first three are supply-level or larger. FAO's food supply includes retail and household waste, so on the harmonised basis waste sits inside each method's per-person need, while the model charges its own (12% avoidable on US factors, corrected; Australian factors, improved). The land bases agree to 0.01 Mha (363.52 Mha), but Henry and Godden's run carries forward 1990-91 sown pasture, and the model's 2025 data layer, taken as the same vintage as FAOSTAT 2021-23, has a meat window of 2020-21 to 2024-25.

### S3.12 The pairing test and the bridge

The pairing test is on the corrected release, for Australia only, with no states, no improved release and no micronutrients. Variant 1 replaces food and does not grow beef at cereal yields. Variant 2 holds energy within 0.5 to 0.6% in the short term and protein falls 4.2%; its mix of two neighbouring diets exists because the model's diet family exists only on a grid of 0.5% steps. Of the three further tests proposed after it, two have been run, as the bridge's land-pool switch (land pooled at world-average hectares) and as the protein-held variant below; diet variant A on the corrected release and crop-fed protein within the meat group were not run.

The bridge (Table 7, panel B) uses one machine for both ends: people are land over land a person, built group by group in the model's eleven food groups. The model world is the evaluator's diet (cropland and pasture square metres a person, energy and protein, at the model's avoidable waste of 0.12 and at 0). The HALF world is the food balance mix of the 46 items HALF counts (13.60 MJ a day), with HALF's land a tonne split into cropland and pasture and converted to Australian square metres with Australia's own productivity. All switches model-like give 41.5035 million (the evaluator and `results/2025-fixed.csv`); all switches HALF-like give 75.97 (column (e) of Table 5, 76.0). The seven switches are the land pool, the per-person requirement, waste, the diet composition, the cropland yield layer, the pasture-fed yield layer and non-food land; the exact average over 5,040 orderings agrees with a subset-weight formula to 1e-9 and the averages add to the total change of -34.46 million.

Requirement and waste have opposite signs, and the records cannot separate them (FAO's food supply holds retail and household waste and no record splits it), so the pair is the robust number. In a six-switch game with the two as one switch, from HALF toward the model: requirement with waste +16.44 million (-48% of the change), diet composition -21.89 (64%), pasture-fed yield layer -12.53 (36%), cropland yield layer -6.39 (19%), land pool -6.17 (18%) and non-food land -3.92 (11%). The two games are not to be mixed. The model's guideline diet takes 9.2% of its energy from pasture-fed red meat against 4.9% for the food balance's diet, which is why diet composition is the largest single feature.

The land pool three ways. In the first game the pool is one formula with an exchange rate k between a cropland square metre and a pasture square metre: k = 4.01 is HALF's own, with end points exactly HALF's 76.0 and the model's 41.5. With k = 91.3, the exchange rate of the energy substitution of variant 1, the HALF world reads 113.6 (the difference from 76.0 is printed, not attributed), the pool switch alone is -43.2 on average and 60% of the change. The protein-held variant is a linear program at the model's own pool rule: pasture-fed groups between 0 and their present amount (dairy is allowed only to fall), every other group between 1 and 3 times its present amount, energy and protein at least the diet's (93.1 g a day). The corrected model reads 85.4 million (84.9 with a cap of 2 and 86.2 with 5), with cereals multiplied by 1.61, legumes by 3 and pasture-fed red meat by 0.33; if dairy may rise like the other groups the reading is 92.4 million, so the classing of dairy is a choice that moves the figure. Its exchange rate is k = 145.6 and the HALF world with that pooling reads 116.9. The cap of three on legumes is a stated bound.

What the records cannot separate: inside the pasture-fed yield layer, the allocation rule (HALF shares all world pasture by feed need; the model uses national output over dry-sheep-equivalent land), the data (FAOSTAT world totals against ABS and AWI national series) and the conversion from world-average to Australian productivity; inside the diet composition, the pattern and what counts as food (46 items against eleven groups, supply energy against eaten energy); the protein floor, which binds only when food is substituted and so appears only in the third and fourth ways; land base and population, which are not switches (363.52 Mha agrees to 0.01 Mha and no population enters either capacity); and exports, which enter through the exchange of productivity and cannot be told from pasture productivity. The bridge counts the requirement as 7.376 against 13.60 MJ a day, and line (c) as 8.0 against 14.75 MJ; the ratio is 1.84 in both (on the switch definitions set in the script).

## S5. The Monte Carlo (supports section 6.9)

A choice is a definition when it changes what the number means: what is counted (exports or not), the unit of self-sufficiency, the time frame (the short-term or long-term bundle), the diet standard (observed, guideline or optimised) and the weather the capacity is for (an average year or the worst in N). Published methods differ on definitions, so each is reported as a separate reading and never drawn. A choice is a setting when it is a value within one meaning: a loss factor, a yield window, a calibration, a measured share, a requirement. A setting is drawn over the range its sources give; where no source gives an end or a weight, the range is the author's judgement and is said to be. The analysis lists the 19 drawn settings with range, distribution and source, and 23 undrawn choices with the reason for each: the definitions, the improved-line modules other than the loss factors, and the settings left out for a stated reason (the crop data window, the plausibility rules, the five crops' organic factors, the reserve and infrastructure, thin-zone yield error, the wool, milk and feedlot series, textiles and fibre, the workbook's processing constants, the climate trend, FAOSTAT pastures, the deviation of national pasture output from the observed meat series (bounded one at a time, below) and nutrient composition).

In this model pasture-fed meat output is national meat output shared among zones by dry sheep equivalent (DSE, the grazing unit of Australian pastures), so its uncertainty is the window of herd and meat data and the calibration of the ABS meat tables, and both are drawn: the window has a first-order index of 0.24 in the short term (95% interval 0.21 to 0.27) and 0.14 in the long term (0.12 to 0.16), and the calibration 0.03 (0.02 to 0.04) and 0.01 (0.01 to 0.02). The dairy and wool series, which are part of pasture output, are not varied (no second source sets a range). What is not drawn is a deviation of national pasture output from the observed meat series, for example a carrying-capacity range in dry sheep equivalents by land class. One at a time, with everything else at base, pasture output 20% below and 20% above moves the short-term capacity from 41.5 to 33.4 million (-19.4%) and to 49.5 million (+19.2%); pasture binds at both ends, so a 20% error carries to capacity almost one for one. This is an illustration at 20% either way, not a bound and not a range with a probability.

The Monte Carlo drew Latin hypercube samples (McKay, Beckman and Conover 1979; Helton and Davis 2003). The corrected release was drawn 2,000 times over 19 settings, 42,000 runs in all with the runs for the indices; 18 of the 19 vary, because the long-term benchmark is fixed at 0.4 on this release. The improved release was drawn the same way, 2,000 times over 18 settings, 40,000 runs in all with the runs for the indices: the Australian loss-factor weight has no meaning there, because the release already applies the Australian factors, and the other 18 all vary. The mirror release keeps an analysis of 1,000 draws of 13 settings, run before six settings were added, which was not rerun. First-order indices use the estimator of Saltelli et al. (2010), with the output centred, and total-order indices that of Jansen (1999), with 95% bootstrap intervals over 1,000 resamples. The first-order shares add to 97% in the short term and in the long term, and each total-order index is within two points of its first-order index, so interactions carry about 3% of the variance.

The six settings added to the 13 drawn before are listed in Table S14. Six settings rest on the author's judgement: the three-way choice of the window of herd and meat data with equal chances, the low end of 0.115 for the meat-and-egg share of the diet, the low end of 0.528 for the red-meat share of meat, the 50/50 weight on the leave-one-out resilience table, equal chances between the two land sources, and the upper end of the energy requirement. The uniform weights between two point estimates, for the loss factors and the meat calibration, are judgement too. The energy range, physical activity level (PAL) 1.4 to 1.8, is part of the range in the Nutrient Reference Values of the National Health and Medical Research Council (2006), here the NHMRC range, of PAL 1.2 to 2.2, so a wider window would widen the short-term range, since the energy requirement is the second driver. One at a time, PAL 1.2 and 2.2 give 48.0 and 26.9 million against 41.5 (+15.6% and -35.1%); the drawn window 1.4 to 1.8 spans 41.5 to 32.7 million, a factor of 1.27 against 1.78 for the NHMRC range (on the improved release 61.8 and 35.2 against 53.6). The drawn window has no draw below the base, and the long-term energy requirement is not drawn at all.

## S7. The rating rubric of section 7 (supports section 7.1)

The rule, as the second rater applied it: a method is well suited to a purpose when the tests show it sees what the purpose turns on; partly suited when it does so with a stated limit or only beside another method; not suited when it cannot see it. Every method has stated limits, so partly suited is rated only when the stated limit touches the core of what the purpose turns on. What each purpose turns on, in the second rater's reading:

1. Food security and drought risk: whether a diet that has to balance can be supplied from domestic land, and how often it cannot (a bad year).
2. Why national estimates range from 9 to 100 million: the definitions that move the number (exports, energy count against diet balance, drought allowance, diet, time frame, land base), and whether a method reproduces or explains a part of the published spread.
3. Regional and local land-use planning: a unit of land below the nation, at that land's own productivity, with what it can feed.
4. Diet change and land use: the land a diet needs, with the diet as an input that can be changed.

These definitions are the second rater's, written from the test records, and not the author's. They are stated in the model's own terms: the first, whether a diet that has to balance can be supplied from domestic land, is what the model does by construction, so a method built on a balanced diet from domestic land will satisfy it. No outside person has rated the table, and the table is the author's judgement, not a finding.

The author drafted the 28 ratings (seven columns, four purposes) from the test records; Table 9 of the paper shows the model in one column, the land-and-diet balance, and gives the failure view's ratings in its note. The planning-oriented studies (Peters, Wilkins and Fick 2007; Fairlie 2008; Gutteridge 2005; Noosa and Douglas Shire) were treated as the balance family at other scales. A second rating was made by a blind checker session, an AI model working as a separate session from the one that drafted the ratings, given the rule, the test records and the claims register, and not the draft. It rated each cell with a reason and opened the draft only after filing its ratings. To show that its comparison could fail, it fed its script one planted wrong cell (HALF for purpose 1 set to well suited), which the script reported as 24 of 28 agreeing, against 25 of 28 for its true ratings.

The two ratings agreed on 25 of 28 cells: 7 of 7 for purposes 1 and 4, 5 of 7 for purpose 2 and 6 of 7 for purpose 3. The three differences (Table S15) were judgement calls, not factual ones: both raters cite the same test facts and differ on whether a method that is not itself a capacity method, or that needs its own adjustment, counts as partly suited. In each the author had rated not suited and the second rater partly suited, and all three were adopted. The model's eight ratings agreed; the second rater called its rating for regional planning, well suited, a judgement call, since partly suited is defensible. No outside person has rated the table. In the table the model's two columns are one model read two ways, the balance and the failure frame, and its own limits (no trade, no herd-cycle risk, constants without recorded sources; section 8) are not rated.

Grazing (section 6.2). Simpson, Petroeschevsky and Lowe (2000) counted 2.61 ha of pasture in a whole footprint of 5.96 ha a person in 1991-92 (p. 14): 44% of the whole footprint, not of the food row alone. Lenzen and Murray (2001) found their modified footprint mostly pasture: 8.1 of 13.6 ha a person in 1994-95 (p. 238). Alexander et al. (2016) attributed the high land-per-diet values of New Zealand, Argentina and Australia together to animal products, particularly beef, with no split for Australia alone (p. 91).

Waste (section 6.3). The model's 6% setting halves its retail-and-consumer loss share, mirroring UN Target 12.3 as Spang et al. (2019, p. 118) state it. The Department of Climate Change, Energy, the Environment and Water (DCCEEW) sets Australia's own goal as whole-chain halving by 2030 (DCCEEW 2017, pp. 3, 8). The 2016-17 National Food Waste Baseline put Australia's food waste at 7.3 Mt (DCCEEW 2019, p. 3). The estimate of about 7.6 Mt a year is the 2021 Feasibility Study's, as the department reports it (DCCEEW n.d.).

Land (section 6.4). Peters, Wilkins and Fick (2007) divided New York State's 2.03 Mha into three classes of land (p. 148).

Diet (section 6.5). Willett et al. (2019) found that diet change alone moved global cropland use by 0 to 2% (p. 472).

Regional planning (section 7.4). Fearnside's own application, which the failure view borrows, covered a 23,600 ha study area, and his simulation needs too much data for large areas (Fearnside 1990, p. 195).

Food security policy (section 7.2). A 2023 parliamentary inquiry recommended a National Food Plan (House of Representatives Standing Committee on Agriculture 2023).

Diet dynamics (section 8.4). Erb et al. (2016) report the option space under several diets, as section 6.5 does.

Variability (section 8.1). Bad wheat years across the southern states are partly correlated (Anderson et al. 2019), and Australian wheat yield potential since 1990 has fallen in simulation while actual yields held flat (Hochman et al. 2017, p. 2071).

Trade (section 8.2). About 16% of the world's population consumes food that is internationally traded (Fader et al. 2013, pp. 2 and 4).

Energy (section 8.3). The model's farm diesel share is its own statement, with the National Farmers' Federation figure as Soliman Hunter and Taylor (2021) quote it; its bioenergy yields are from Geoscience Australia and ABARE (2010).

Constants (section 8.5). An audit found red meat the heaviest group of constants, because red meat is most of the land in the default diet, and many constants carry no recorded source.
