# Paper B, supplementary information

Supplementary sections S1 to S6 and Tables S1 to S4 hold the detail moved out of the main text when it was cut to the journal's length (decision 92), so that none of it is lost. Each section names the main-text section it supports. The claims behind every number are in `supplementary_claims.csv`, beside this file.

## S1. Further positions on the concept (supports section 2)

By the end of the nineteenth century carrying capacity had become a measure of rangeland productivity, and critiques of the rangeland use came in turn from Australia and Africa (Sayre 2008, p. 126). Ecology itself uses the term in many senses (Chapman and Byron 2018).

Among the defenders, Meyer and Ausubel (1999) fitted logistic growth under a capacity that itself grows; their criticism is of a fixed capacity, not of the concept. Hopfenberg (2003) tied human capacity to food availability, so that capacity moves as food supply moves. Gilland (2006) tied his own estimates to a nutrition standard.

Rees (1996) defined carrying capacity as the maximum load an environment can sustain. Giampietro and Saltelli (2014) argued that, because the footprint protocol has no external referent, its results do not support the narrative built on them.

Biermann and Kim (2020) set out critiques of planetary limits from earth system science, development studies, and science and technology studies.

## S2. The catalogue: further detail by family (supports section 4)

### S2.1 Resource-based land and diet

Peters, Wilkins and Fick 2007 ran their New York State model across a range of diets. Peters et al. 2016 treated the agricultural land of the United States as a closed system with no trade.

The foodshed studies are the spatial form of the method (decision 57). Peters et al. 2009 allocated the production zones of New York State to its population centres by least distance, with the 2007 diet model giving the need per person. Zumkehr and Campbell 2015 asked what share of the population of the United States its cropland could feed within a given distance, a result that turns on the radius more than on the diet. Kurtz et al. 2020 mapped the same question for metropolitan areas of the United States across diets and land types, and called each county's figure its carrying capacity.

### S2.2 Energy and protein balance

The method's founding work was a 1975 submission to the national population inquiry (Gifford et al. 1975), and its land base is Nix's estimate of about 70 Mha for intensive production, both cited as reported in Henry and Godden 1995 (p. 98). Shepon et al. 2016 (not seen; cited from its abstract) estimate how many more people the United States cropland that grows feed for beef could feed if it fed poultry or people.

### S2.3 Commodity balance and the nutritional food balance

Ridoutt et al. 2017 set Australian intake scenarios against projected production by food group in a Nutritional Production Sufficiency Index.

### S2.4 Export-inclusive counts

The 2025 report of CSIRO and the University of Queensland calls its own measure misleading as an indicator of food security (section 1). Fader et al. 2013, as reported in Clapp 2017 (p. 90), take the reverse view of trade: which countries cannot be self-sufficient on their own land, water and soil.

### S2.5 Ecological footprint

Lin et al. 2018 and Wackernagel et al. 2019 describe the national accounts that carry the method. Blomqvist et al. 2013, as reported in Giampietro and Saltelli 2014 (p. 619), find that without carbon dioxide the footprint shows the planet on a sustainable path, and that its totals turn on an uncertain rate of carbon uptake by forests. Giampietro and Saltelli 2014 argue that the protocol does not match its narrative, and they call for several indicators across scales. Goldfinger et al. 2014 (not seen; cited from its abstract) reply that the footprint reflects actual productivity and is not prescriptive about trade. Wiedmann and Lenzen 2018 (not seen; cited from its abstract) place footprints within the work on impacts displaced through trade.

### S2.6 Land per diet and people per hectare

The HALF recipe allocates world cropland to products by mass and value and charges livestock for their feed through feed conversion ratios. Kastner et al. 2012 (not seen; cited from its abstract) split the change in cropland needed by world region into the effects of population, yield and diet. Röös et al. 2017 (not seen; cited from its abstract) ask whether cropland suffices under several livestock futures, in a regional model with no trade between regions. Van Zanten et al. 2016 compare livestock fed on leftovers or on land unsuited to crops with food crops on the same land. Schön and Böhringer 2023 apply land per diet to a German state, and Ridoutt et al. 2020 (not seen; cited from its abstract) to Australian diets. None of these works states a people count. In Cassidy et al. 2013 each crop's calories go to food, feed and other uses.

### S2.7 Optimisation models (diet and land use)

Ridoutt et al. 2021 score observed Australian diets on footprints and diet quality, comparing diets rather than optimising one. LUTO is a constrained linear programme (Gao and Bryan 2017). The EAT-Lancet reference diet, judged within planetary boundaries rather than optimised, is context only (Willett et al. 2019).

### S2.8 Integrated resource-flow models

Hatfield-Dodds et al. 2015 linked several models in a national outlook for Australia, and Bryan et al. 2016 ran a land-use model of Australia, each over many scenarios. The FABLE Calculator is a spreadsheet model of the food and land system with no optimisation; Navarro Garcia et al. 2022 ran it for Australia against environmental targets, within the initiative that Jones et al. 2023 describe. Herrington 2020 compares the World3 scenarios with observed data.

### S2.9 Water-bounded capacity

Rosa et al. 2018 bound the expansion of irrigation by environmental flows. Kummu et al. 2016 trace water shortage and stress over the twentieth century. Li et al. 2018 review the methods for water resources carrying capacity. Gerten et al. 2020, as reported in Beltran-Peña, Rosa and D'Odorico 2020 (p. 5), find that existing cropland could feed 10.2 billion people sustainably.

### S2.10 Failure probability

Fearnside 1990 gives an overview of the method and one result for a Brazilian settlement area (section S3.6).

## S3. Implementation and fidelity detail by method (supports section 5)

### S3.1 Energy and protein balance

Henry and Godden 1995 print production in 1990-91, excluding wool and cotton, of 404 × 10¹⁵ J of food energy and 306 × 10¹⁰ g of protein (p. 99). The consumption factors, inferred from Gifford et al. 1975, are 0.1760 and 0.2041 million people per unit (p. 99). Production is scaled by Nix's 70 Mha of intensive land over the 45.7 Mha in use (p. 99). Gifford et al. 1975 enters only through them.

The test used food products only, with FAO's energy and protein per tonne. Its 134 to 211 million are 138 and 134 million (energy and protein) on production as it is, 161 and 156 scaled to Nix's 70 Mha over 59.95 Mha in use, and 211 and 205 over the 45.7 Mha of 1990-91. The 2025 in-use land was not measured; the 28.3 Mha of sown pasture of 1990-91 was carried forward. The land base alone moves the result by 31%. The consumption factor implies 15.6 MJ a person a day, nearly twice a plain requirement of 8.8 MJ; at 8.8 MJ the same production feeds 243 million.

### S3.2 Ecological footprint: the three builds

Build 1 used FAOSTAT world yields for 1990-92 and shared world pasture among beef, sheep meat and milk by edible protein. Build 2, written independently, shared it by production times feed conversion. Build 3 charged all red meat at the sheep-meat rate Wackernagel's thesis prints, 34 kg/ha a year (Wackernagel 1994, p. 285), with no dairy on pasture.

Pasture is reproduced within 3% in build 3: 1.718 ha against 1.758. Build 1 gives 1.737 ha, but not at Wackernagel's printed rate, and build 2 gives 2.385 ha, 36% high. Crop land for feed is reproduced by builds 1 and 2 (0.098 and 0.094 ha against 0.095); build 3 is 22% high. Crop land for plant foods is not reproduced from the published sources: the builds give 0.074 to 0.142 ha against 0.312. Wool pasture in the clothing row (0.854 ha, p. 14) is not reproduced either.

### S3.3 HALF index

The reconstruction covered 66 of the paper's 90 commodities. Raw animal fats get no land of their own; butter and cream get land through milk's processing share. On 2011 data the United States and Argentina are within 5%, and India is 7% low.

### S3.4 People per hectare

Cropland was the only land counted. On 2011 data, for the world, 61% of calories are delivered against the paper's 59% (p. 4). The United States is not reproduced: its feed share is 41% against the paper's 67%. The rebuild used 22 crop items and 1,061 Mha against the paper's 41 crops on 947 Mha (p. 4).

Harvest matching. FAO's food balance books Australia's winter crops (wheat, barley, oats, other pulses, and rape and mustardseed) a year behind the crops file. Pairing each with the harvest it came from gives 5.8 people a hectare delivered and, on all 31.65 Mha of cropland, about 183 million in place of about 190 (`cassidy_matched.csv`).

In the harmonised comparison (section 5.11), the food balance's 3,525 kcal a day in place of the paper's 2,700 is a factor of 0.766.

### S3.5 Commodity balance

In their Table 2.5, production in 1997-98 is 31.578 Mt of grain, 3.514 Mt of meat and 0.223 Mt of fish (Foran and Poldy 2002, p. 64). FAO cereals stood for their total grain. FAOSTAT gives production in 1997-98 of 31.95 Mt of grain and 3.47 Mt of meat, 1.2% above and 1.2% below their ABS figures. Production in 2021-23 was 52.6 Mt of grain, 4.66 Mt of meat and 0.29 Mt of fish.

### S3.6 Failure probability

Fearnside 1990 gives at most 0.24 persons a hectare for a Trans-Amazon study area (p. 195). The failure view here has one criterion, the default diet, where Fearnside uses four, and the curve rests on six run points, interpolated. At the 2025 population the failure probability is about 1.5% a year on the improved method and about 2% on the thesis method as built, against 5.8 to 6.1% on the corrected method, so the failure risk at the 2025 population depends strongly on the method.

## S5. The Monte Carlo (supports section 6.9)

The Monte Carlo drew 1,000 Latin hypercube samples of 13 uncertain choices (McKay, Beckman and Conover 1979; Helton and Davis 2003; each not seen; cited from its abstract). Twelve of the 13 varied in the corrected and published releases. In the improved release the long-term benchmark was drawn but had almost no effect. The draws used an additive shortcut, which was checked against full runs. Each choice's share of the variance was estimated from the draws (Saltelli et al. 2008; not seen; cited from its abstract).

Five range ends are the author's judgement and have no outside source: the three-way meat-window choice with equal chances, the low ends of 0.115 for the meat-and-egg share of the diet and 0.528 for the red-meat share of meat, the 50/50 weight on the leave-one-out resilience table, and the low end of 0.30 for the long-term benchmark.
