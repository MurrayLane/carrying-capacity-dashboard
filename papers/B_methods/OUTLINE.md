# Paper B (methods): a critical review of carrying capacity methods, tested on common Australian data

Redrafted 2026-11-06 after Murray's decisions (below). This is an outline only: it fixes the structure, what each section covers and the files it would draw on. No results are stated and no tables or prose have been built.

**Working title** (Murray: a good title; to revisit now the paper is a review): Why estimates of Australia's carrying capacity differ, and how sure we can be. *Possible subtitle:* a critical review and a common-data comparison of methods.

**Readers.** Land use planners and sustainability analysts.

**Type.** A critical narrative review (decided 2026-11-06): organised around an argument and a stated, purposeful selection of literature, not a systematic search protocol.

**Narrative (Murray asked for a story to drive each paper, 2026-11-06).** *Claim to test:* the 9 to 100 million spread is mostly a spread of questions, not of facts, so the method should follow the purpose. *Arc:* (1) the puzzle: "100 million", "61 million", "30 to 35 million", "9 to 14 million" are all called carrying capacity; (2) is the concept sound, and why planners still need it; (3) the test: well-known methods on the same 2025 data; (4) what moves the number: a handful of design choices, with the model's own spread under them set beside the published range; (5) the author's own model as one exemplar, critiqued by the same tests and built on; (6) the payoff: a guide to which method for which purpose, which headline numbers to distrust, and what to report. *Takeaway for planners and analysts:* a capacity figure is only as good as its stated question and uncertainty.
**Headline (corrected releases; the method tests and design choices are run, `analysis/method_tests_2025/README.md`; the Monte Carlo range is set beside the spread there, the guide in section 7 is not yet drafted).** One model on one data set spans about 16 million (its long-term settings) or 19 million (worst of 100 years) to 55 million (no avoidable waste) by definition alone, 63 million with the improved settings; counting exports then stretches the claims to 60 to 100 million and beyond. Exists now: `analysis/comparators_2025/` (the model under other authors' definitions: the Jones committee's two figures, the proportional export counts, Gifford not reproduced). Not yet done: the method tests, the Monte Carlo range against the spread, the guide.

**The question.** Which method suits which purpose? How much of the spread of published estimates of Australia's capacity (9 to 100 million) is the definition, the diet or the method, and which design choices decide the answer?

**The paper's contribution beyond Murray's earlier work.** The thesis (chapter 6) and *Land Use Policy* 2010 ("The carrying capacity imperative") already review the planning-oriented methods: Limits to Growth, the ecological footprint, South East Queensland, Noosa and Douglas Shire, Peters (New York State), Fairlie (Britain) and Fearnside (reviewed there as a planning method; applied here as a test for the first time). This paper does not repeat that review. It adds:
1. Method families not in the earlier work: energy and protein balance (Gifford 1975; Henry and Godden 1995), integrated resource flow (Foran and Poldy 2002), people per hectare (Cassidy 2013), the HALF land index (Alexander 2016), Simpson et al. 2000, the critiques of the footprint, and the literature since 2011.
2. **Testing the methods through the model, on Murray's own data:** the same current Australian production, land, population and diet data (2025) put through each method, with each method checked against its authors' published figure where possible.
3. What each design choice does to the answer, measured, not asserted.
4. A guide to which method fits which purpose.

## Sections

**1. Introduction.** The spread of published estimates, why it matters to planners and analysts, and the question. Sequel to the 2010 methodology review. States at the outset, and again in the competing-interests statement, that the model is the author's own (the thesis method of 2014 and the papers to 2017) and is one of the methods reviewed, held to the same tests.

**2. The concept and its critics.** Hardin, Seidl and Tisdell, Sayre, Meyer and Ausubel, Price, Cohen and the thesis typology (inputs and outputs, scale). Brief: points back to the thesis chapter 6 and the 2010 paper. *Sources: `Literature review/` library.*

**3. How the review was done.** What was selected and why: the well-known methods across the main approaches, plus papers published since 2011 from Murray's citation exports; what the earlier work already covered. Stated briefly and transparently.

**4. A catalogue of methods.** Families, each by the question it answers, its inputs and outputs, and its scale: resource-based land and diet; energy and protein balance; commodity balance; ecological footprint; land per diet indices; integrated resource-flow models; failure-probability simulation (Fearnside); nutrition optimisation; export-inclusive counts. The planning-oriented studies get one line each and a pointer to the earlier work; the families new to Murray's work are treated in full.

**5. Testing the methods on common Australian data (the core).**
* The common data set: production, land, population and diet for 2025, from the model's releases (decided 2026-11-06: 2025 only; the 2011 data and the change since 2011 belong to the results paper; at most one 2011 column in one table as a check that method differences are not an artefact of the data).
* Each method: how it was implemented, what it needs, and and how closely the implementation follows the authors' description. Where the authors' own figure is published and the inputs are obtainable, the implementation is checked against it (a fidelity check, not the test); the test itself is the method applied to the Australian data.
* Murray's own method is an exemplar like the others, critiqued by the same tests. It is entered twice: as published (the thesis method, the 2011-era design, run unchanged on the 2025 data) and as built on (the settings the comparison shows matter, section 6). Where the other methods expose a weakness in the thesis method, the paper says so (section 4 and section 8); the published 2011 result sits in the register of estimates as one of the 9 to 100 million, not as a separate analysis.
* The result: the same data through each method, side by side.

**6. What moves the number.** The design choices measured with the model on the same data: whether exports count, the diet and waste assumed, a drought allowance (the failure-probability view), the unit of self-sufficiency (scale: the full 2025 curve from national to SA2, the boundary effect and where the curve bends, held here as a method finding), the yield basis and the time frame. Uncertainty as a design dimension, not a stand-alone section.
   Three of the design choices are where the model's improvements enter, each introduced by what the other methods show (the improvements are the model's setting for that choice, run beside the baseline):
   * **What a method assumes about grazing and pasture-fed meat and dairy** (the yield basis). Every land-based method is decided by it: pasture is 44% of Simpson's footprint, beef drives Australia's HALF value, Peters's US diets turn on grazing land, Lenzen and Murray's footprint is mostly pasture (8.1 of 13.6 hectares), and Cassidy leaves grazing out altogether; Alexander notes grassland spans very different intensities and uses global averages. The model's Australian setting: beef output by state (`regional-beef-output`), dairy on land that can support it at dairy-farm yields (`dairy-good-land`), the organic factor for grazing ruminants (`organic-ruminant-factor`, long term only).
   * **How waste is treated** (the waste choice). The energy and protein balances (Gifford; Henry and Godden) and Cassidy count production as eaten; Alexander counts consumer waste and over-eating inside the diet; Peters applies US loss rates; the thesis model used US loss factors too. The model's Australian setting: measured Australian loss factors (`loss-factors-australia`), and a recycling credit limited to feed-grade, lawful, dry-matter-correct waste (`recycling-stage`), the counterpart to the no-avoidable-waste case.
   * **Which land counts as available** (the land base). Henry and Godden's answer rests on Nix's 70 Mha of intensive land; Peters finds results highly sensitive to cultivable cropland; Gifford doubled cropland; Fischer et al. rest on suitability. The model's setting states how a reserve and infrastructure shortfall is taken from cropping, pasture and other land (`reserve-pro-rata`).
   * **The unit of self-sufficiency** (scale). Methods differ most in the area they treat as self-sufficient: nation (Simpson, Alexander, Foran and Poldy), state or country (Peters, Fairlie), shire or region (Noosa, Douglas, Gutteridge's South East Queensland). Shown here with the model's levels (national, states, regions, and the SA4, SA3 and SA2 sums as the design-choice table lists them) to show that the choice matters; the full curve down to SA2, the boundary effect and the ideal split are the Australian result in Paper A. *Sources: `results/scale_curve.csv` (national, state and NRM rows).*
   * **How sure the answer is, as a method.** The design choices are also varied together: the Monte Carlo method (Latin hypercube over the 13 uncertain choices, 1,000 draws, the one-at-a-time effects, the additive shortcut and its check against full runs) gives the model's own national range and which choices drive its width. This is the paper's test of how much of the spread of published estimates (9 to 100 million) can be produced by definition, diet and method choices alone, and the model's range is set beside the other methods' figures. *Sources: `analysis/sensitivity_2025/` (`monte_carlo.mjs`, `summarise_mc.py`, `rss_check.py`), `results/ranges_*.csv`.*
   Caution for the drafting: the improvements were made on their own evidence (SCOPE E-items); the paper says the comparison shows where each choice matters and that the model's setting for it is an Australian one, not that the comparison produced the improvements.

**7. Which method for which purpose.** (Draft matrix, ratings, recommendations and reporting checklist: `SECTION7_DRAFT.md`, 2026-11-06, for Murray's edits.) A matrix of intended outcomes against method features, with a fit-for-purpose recommendation and a minimum reporting checklist. Outcomes, in order of how strongly the paper's evidence supports them:
1. food security and drought risk;
2. why national estimates range from 9 to 100 million (exports, energy balance, definitions);
3. regional and local land-use planning;
4. diet change and land use.

**8. Limitations and what no method yet does.** Includes a short list of what the comparison shows the thesis method lacks (for example fossil energy land, which Simpson's footprint counts and the model does not; imports and exports; variability between years; a single fixed diet; US-sourced constants) and where another method does better for a stated purpose. What the model cannot carry (`DISCUSSION.md`); where an implementation could not be checked against its authors' figure and rests on their description alone; the constants declared as assumptions.

**9. Conclusions, data and code availability.**

Test rule (Murray, 2026-11-06): the focus is the method, not where its author applied it. ## Relationship to Paper A (proposed 2026-11-06, to confirm)

* **Shared base, neither predicated on the other.** Both rest on the frozen releases and the archived model (the supporting paper's DOI comes first). Paper B does not need Paper A's findings (the change since 2011, the scale curve); Paper A does not need Paper B's findings.
* **B first, then A, because A leans on B for the method.** Paper A's headline (national capacity, the change by region) invites the question of why this method and how far the answer depends on it; Paper B answers that, so Paper A cites it. Paper B is where the improved model first appears; Paper A leads with the thesis method on corrected data in both vintages.
* **2011 in Paper B.** The 2011 corrected result appears only as the model's own published exemplar (in the register of published estimates) and as the single check column already allowed (section 5). The 2011 to 2025 comparison belongs to Paper A alone.
